\[
n=a\,b+c ,\qquad 1\le a\le b-1,\;0\le c\le b-1

be the two–digit representation of \(n\) in base \(b\).  
The condition “\(n\) is \(b\)-beautiful’’ is

\[
a+c=\sqrt n .
\]

Hence \(\sqrt n\) must be an integer, so \(n=k^{2}\) for some integer \(k\ge 2\).
Now

\[
k^{2}=a b+c = a(b-1)+a+c = a(b-1)+k .
\]

Let \(d=b-1\).  Then

\[
a=\frac{k(k-1)}{d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad c=k-a .
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  When is \(a\) an integer?

\(a\) is an integer iff \(d\mid k(k-1)\).  
Because \(k\) and \(k-1\) are consecutive, \(\gcd(k,k-1)=1\).  
For a prime power \(p^{e}\mid d\) we must have

\[
k\equiv 0 \pmod{p^{e}}\quad\text{or}\quad k\equiv 1 \pmod{p^{e}} ,
\]

otherwise \(p^{e}\) could not divide the product.  
Thus, for every distinct prime factor of \(d\) we have two possible
congruences.  
If \(d\) has \(\omega(d)\) distinct prime factors, the number of
solutions of

\[
k(k-1)\equiv 0\pmod d
\]

in the full residue system \(\{0,1,\dots ,d-1\}\) is \(2^{\omega(d)}\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Restricting to \(k\le d\)

From \(a=\dfrac{k(k-1)}{d}\le d-1\) we obtain \(k\le d\).  
The numbers \(k=0\) and \(k=1\) are not in the interval \([2,d]\);  
\(k=d\) is a solution because \(d\mid d(d-1)\).

Hence the number of admissible \(k\) (and thus of \(b\)-beautiful numbers)

\[
N(d)=2^{\omega(d)}-1 .
\]
