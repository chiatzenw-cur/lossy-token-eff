### 1.  The median must be an integer that does **not** appear in the list  

If \(n\) is odd, the median is one of the elements of the list, contradicting the condition that the median is not in the list.  
Hence \(n\) must be even.  
Let \(n=2k\).  
The median is  
\[
m=\frac{a_k+a_{k+1}}{2},
\]
where \(a_1\le a_2\le\dots\le a_{2k}\) is the sorted list.  
Thus \(a_k\) and \(a_{k+1}\) are distinct and their average \(m\) is an integer not occurring in the list.

---

### 2.  The unique mode is \(9\)

The number \(9\) appears more often than any other number.  
At least two \(9\)’s are required.

---

### 3.  Try the smallest possible even \(n\)

*For \(n=4\) (\(k=2\)):*

We need two \(9\)’s, so the remaining two numbers must sum to \(30-18=12\).

Possible unordered pairs of positive integers that sum to \(12\) are  

\[
(1,11),\;(2,10),\;(3,9),\;(4,8),\;(5,7),\;(6,6).
\]

Because the median is \((a_2+a_3)/2\), the two numbers must be placed
between the two \(9\)’s in the sorted order.  
The sorted list will therefore be  

\[
[a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4]=[\text{first number},\text{second number},9,9].
\]

So the median is \((a_2+9)/2\).  
For this to be an integer, the second number must be odd.  
Moreover the median must **not** be in the list, so it cannot equal \(9\) or the second number.

### 4.  Show that no larger even \(n\) works

For \(n=6\) we require at least two \(9\)’s, leaving \(12\) to be shared by four other distinct positive integers.  
No four distinct positive integers sum to \(12\) (minimum sum is \(1+2+3+4=10\), but then the median would not be an integer).  
If we try three \(9\)’s, the remaining three numbers must sum to \(3\), forcing them to be \(1,1,1\), which would give a non‑unique mode.  
Thus \(n=6\) is impossible.

For \(n\ge 8\) the remaining sum would be even smaller and cannot be split into distinct positive integers, so those cases are impossible as well.
