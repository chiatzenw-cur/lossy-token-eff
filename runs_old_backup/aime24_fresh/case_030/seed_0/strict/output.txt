column, i.e.

\[
a>0 \;\Longrightarrow\; b>0 .
\tag{1}
\]

Similarly all black rows must meet at least one black column:

\[
5-a>0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 5-b>0 ,
\tag{2}
\]

or, equivalently, \(b>0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a>0\).

Combining (1) and (2) we see that **the numbers of white rows and white
columns are equal**:

\[
a=b \quad\text{or}\quad (a,b)=(0,0).
\]

If \(a = 5\) then \(b\) also must be \(5\);  
if \(a = 0\) then \(b = 0\);  
and for \(1\le a,b\le 4\) we must have \(a=b\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  Counting the colourings

For a fixed \(k=a=b\) (\(k=0,1,\dots ,5\)) choose

* the \(k\) white rows out of 5 – \(\binom{5}{k}\) ways,
* the \(k\) white columns out of 5 – \(\binom{5}{k}\) ways.

Thus for a particular \(k\) we obtain  

\[
\binom{5}{k}^{\,2}
\]

different colourings, each of which gives a unique grid of chips
(because a chip is present exactly at the intersection of a white
row and a white column, or of a black row and a black column).

Summing over all admissible \(k\),

\[
N=\sum_{k=0}^{5}\binom{5}{k}^{2}
= \binom{10}{5}=252 .
\]

The identity \(\sum_{k}\binom{n}{k}^{2}=\binom{2n}{n}\) has been used.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4.  Conclusion

Every valid placement of chips corresponds uniquely to a choice of a
subset of rows and the same-sized subset of columns to be coloured
white; all other rows and columns are black.  
All such choices give maximal configurations, because any empty cell
lies at the intersection of a white and a black line, and placing a
chip there would violate the colour‑uniformity constraints.

Hence the number of possible configurations is  

\[
\boxed{252}.
\]
