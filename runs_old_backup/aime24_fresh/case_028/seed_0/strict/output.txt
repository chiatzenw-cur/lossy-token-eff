\[
N=\overline{abcd}=1000a+100b+10c+d \qquad (a\neq 0).
\]

The condition that changing any one digit to \(1\) gives a multiple of \(7\) gives the following four congruences:

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{replace }a:& 100b+10c+d \equiv 1 \pmod 7 \\
\text{replace }b:& 6a+2+3c+d \equiv 0 \pmod 7\\
\text{replace }c:& 6a+2b+3+d \equiv 0 \pmod 7\\
\text{replace }d:& 6a+2b+3c+1\equiv 0 \pmod 7
\end{array}
\]

(All arithmetic is mod \(7\); note \(100\equiv2,\;10\equiv3,\;1000\equiv6\pmod7\).)

---

### Simplifying

1. From the first and second equations  

   \(1-2b-3c \equiv -6a-2-3c\Rightarrow 6a-2b+3\equiv0\Rightarrow 3a-b\equiv2\pmod7\)

   \[
   \boxed{b\equiv3a-2\pmod 7}.
   \]

2. From the first and third equations  

   \(1-2b-3c \equiv -6a-2b-3\Rightarrow 6a-3c+4\equiv0\Rightarrow 2a-c\equiv1\pmod7\)

   \[
   \boxed{c\equiv2a-1\pmod 7}.
   \]

3. Insert \(b,c\) into the fourth equation  

   \(6a+2(3a-2)+3(2a-1)\equiv6\).   
   This simplifies to \(4a\equiv6\pmod7\).  

   The inverse of \(4\) modulo \(7\) is \(2\), so  

   \[
   a\equiv6\cdot2\equiv12\equiv5\pmod7 .
   \]

   Hence  
   \[
   a=5 \quad (a\text{ is a single decimal digit}).
   \]

Using \(a=5\):

\[
b\equiv3(5)-2=13\equiv6\pmod7\;\Rightarrow\;b=6,
\]
\[
c\equiv2(5)-1=9\equiv2\pmod7\;\Rightarrow\;c=2\text{ or }9 .
\]

Finally \(d\) follows from the first equation:

\[
d\equiv1-2b-3c\equiv1-5-6\equiv4\pmod7,
\]

and \(0\le d\le9\) gives \(d=4\).

Thus the possible numbers are  

\[
N_1=5624,\qquad N_2=5694.
\]

Checking each, changing any single digit to \(1\) yields a multiple of \(7\); hence both are valid.  
The greatest such four‑digit integer is  

\[
N=5694.
\]
