\[
\log_x(y^x)=10,\qquad \log_y(x^{4y})=10
\]

we can rewrite them in exponential form:

\[
y^x = x^{10}\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^{4y}=y^{10}.
\]

---

### 1.  Express the equations in logarithmic form

Let  

\[
A=\log x,\qquad B=\log y \quad(\text{logarithm to any base, e.g. natural log}).
\]

Then  

\[
x = e^A,\qquad y = e^B .
\]

Taking logarithms of the two equations we get

\[
x B = 10 A \quad\text{(1)}
\]
\[
4 y A = 10 B \quad\text{(2)}
\]

because  
\(\log_x(y^x)=x\log_x y =x\frac{\log y}{\log x}=xB/A\).

---

### 2.  Multiply the two equations

Multiply (1) and (2):

\[
(xB)(4yA)= (10A)(10B).
\]

Simplify:

\[
4xy\,AB = 100\,AB.
\]

Since \(x>1\) and \(y>1\), we have \(A>0\) and \(B>0\), so \(AB\neq 0\).  
Divide both sides by \(AB\):

\[
4xy = 100 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad xy = 25.
\]

Thus the product \(xy\) is fixed regardless of the particular values of \(x\) and \(y\).

---

### 3.  Verify existence of solutions

Solving (1) and (2) explicitly gives a specific pair 

\[
x\approx 1.53,\qquad y\approx 16.30,
\]

which indeed satisfies both original logarithmic equations.  
Therefore such real numbers exist and \(xy\) is well‑defined.
