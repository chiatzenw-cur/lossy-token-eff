A number \(n\) is **\(b\)-beautiful** iff  

* \(n\) has exactly two digits in base \(b\) – its digits are \(a\) and \(b\)  
  \(\;n=a b+b\);  
* the sum of these two digits equals \(\sqrt n\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  The equation for a \(b\)-beautiful number

Let \(a,b\;(a,b)\) be the digits.  
Since \(n=a b+b\),

\[
\sqrt n = a+b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (a+b)^2 = a b + b .
\]

Rearranging gives

\[
b = \frac{a^2- a b + b^2 - b}{b-1} = \frac{a^2-a+b^2-b}{b-1}.
\]

But the system is easier, once we give the number \(n\) a name.
Let the square root be \(s\) (so \(n=s^2\)).  
Then \(s\) is a non‑negative integer and

\[
s^2 = a\,(b-b)+s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a(b-1)=s^{2}-s .
\]

Hence

\[
a=\frac{s^2-s}{\,b-1\,}\qquad
s\ge 1,\; s\le b-1 . \tag{1}
\]

For \(a\) to be a digit we need \(1\le a\le b-1\).  
Notice that if \(s=1\) then \(a=0\) and the number would not be a 2-digit
number; therefore \(s>1\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Counting the admissible \(s\)

Equation (1) tells us that \(b-1\) must divide \(s^2-s=s(s-1)\),
i.e. \(s\) is a solution of

\[
s(s-1)\equiv 0\pmod{b-1}. \tag{2}
\]

Write \(b-1=G\) and let \(G\) be the product of its distinct prime powers.

For any prime \(p\mid G\) we have a requirement:  
\(p\mid s\) or \(p\mid (p-1)\).

Thus for each prime \(p\mid G\) we have two possibilities for the residue,
\(s\equiv 0\pmod{p}\) or \(s\equiv 1\pmod{p}\).
Using the Chinese remainder theorem, we obtain

* distinct solutions modulo \(g\) with residue choices \(0\) or \(1\).
* In total \(\;2^{\omega(G)}\) distinct solutions, where \( \omega(G)\) is

Every solution appears once as an integer \(s\) with \(1\le s\le g\).
The residue \(s=1\) gives \(a=0\) and therefore does **not** give a
two‑digit number.  
Hence the number of full \(b\)-beautiful numbers is

\[
\boxed{\,2^{\omega(G)}-1\,}.
