### 1.  Which primes allow a solution modulo \(p^{2}\)?

If \(p^{2}\mid n^{4}+1\) then certainly \(p\mid n^{4}+1\).  
So we first need \(n^{4}\equiv -1 \pmod p\).

For an odd prime \(p\), the multiplicative group \((\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^{\times}\) is cyclic of order \(p-1\).
A primitive root \(g\) gives

\[
n\equiv g^{k}\pmod p,\qquad
n^{4}\equiv g^{4k}\equiv -1\equiv g^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p .
\]

Thus we need an integer \(k\) such that  

\[
4k\equiv \frac{p-1}{2}\pmod{p-1}.   \tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) is solvable iff \(\gcd(4,p-1)\mid \frac{p-1}{2}\).
Since \(p-1\) is even, write \(p-1=2^{a}m\) with \(m\) odd.

* \(a=1\):  \(\gcd(4,p-1)=2\) and the right–hand side is odd → no solution.  
* \(a=2\):  \(\gcd(4,p-1)=4\) and the right–hand side is \(2m\) (odd) → no solution.  
* \(a\ge 3\):  \(\gcd(4,p-1)=4\) and \(\frac{p-1}{2}=2^{\,a-1}m\) is a multiple of 4 → solution exists.

Hence a prime \(p\) must satisfy \(p-1\equiv0\pmod 8\), i.e. \(p\equiv1\pmod 8\).

The smallest odd prime with this property is  

\[
p=17 .
\]

(Primes \(2,3,5,7,11,13\) are all \( \not\equiv1\pmod8\) and fail to satisfy \(n^{4}\equiv-1\pmod p\).)

---

### 2.  Lifting the solution from \(p\) to \(p^{2}\)

Because \(p\nmid 4n^{3}\) whenever \(n\not\equiv0\pmod p\), Hensel’s lemma guarantees a unique lift from
\(n^{4}\equiv-1\pmod{p}\) to \(n^{4}\equiv-1\pmod{p^{2}}\).

For \(p=17\) we have a root modulo \(17\):  

\[
2^{4}=16\equiv-1\pmod{17}.
\]

Let \(n=2+17k\).  For \(p^{2}=289\) we compute, keeping only terms that survive modulo \(289\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
n^{4}&=(2+17k)^{4}
      =2^{4}+4\cdot 2^{3}\cdot 17k\pmod{289}   \\
      &=16+544k\equiv 16+255k\pmod{289}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence

\[
n^{4}+1\equiv 16+255k+1\equiv 0\pmod{289}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
255k+17\equiv0\pmod{289}.
\]

Since \(\gcd(255,289)=17\) and \(289=17^{2}\),

\[
255k\equiv-17\equiv272\pmod{289}
         \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
15k\equiv16\pmod{17}.
\]

The inverse of \(15\) modulo \(17\) is \(8\) (because \(-2\cdot(-9)=1\)), so  

\[
k\equiv 16\cdot 8\equiv128\equiv9 \pmod{17}.
\]

Thus the smallest non‑negative \(k\) satisfying the congruence is \(k=9\).

Therefore

\[
n = 2+17k = 2+17\cdot9 = 155 .
\]
