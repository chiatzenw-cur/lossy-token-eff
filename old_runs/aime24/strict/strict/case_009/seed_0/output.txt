A position is **\(P\)** (previous player wins, i.e. a losing position for the player to move) if every legal move takes the game to an \(N\)–position, and **\(N\)** (next player wins) otherwise.

The legal moves are to remove \(1\) or \(4\) tokens.

---

### 1.  Compute the first few positions

| \(n\) | legal moves | resulting positions | classification |
|------|--------------|---------------------|----------------|
| 0 | — | (terminal) | \(P\) |
| 1 | \(1\to0\) | \(P\) | \(N\) |
| 2 | \(1\to1\) | \(N\) | \(P\) |
| 3 | \(1\to2\) | \(P\) | \(N\) |
| 4 | \(1\to3\) (\(N\)), \(4\to0\) (\(P\)) | \(N\) |
| 5 | \(1\to4\) (\(N\)), \(4\to1\) (\(N\)) | \(P\) |

Continuing in this way gives

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
n & \text{type} \\
\hline
0 & P\\
1 & N\\
2 & P\\
3 & N\\
4 & N\\
5 & P\\
6 & N\\
7 & P\\
8 & N\\
9 & N\\
10& P\\
11& N\\
12& P\\
13& N\\
14& N\\
15& P\\
\vdots
\end{array}
\]

The pattern of \(P\)-positions is easy to see: after each \(P\) the next move goes alternately two steps, then three steps, then two, then three, and so on.

---

### 2.  Find a closed description

Observing the list, the \(P\)-positions satisfy  

\[
n\equiv 0 \pmod 5 \quad\text{or}\quad n\equiv 2 \pmod 5 .
\]

Proof by induction:  
Assume all numbers \(< n\) that are \(0\) or \(2\) mod \(5\) are \(P\)-positions, and that all others are \(N\).  
If \(n\equiv0\pmod5\) or \(n\equiv2\pmod5\) then

* \(n-1\equiv 4\) or \(1 \pmod5\) (an \(N\)-position),
* \(n-4\equiv 1\) or \(3 \pmod5\) (an \(N\)-position).

Thus all moves from \(n\) go to \(N\)-positions, so \(n\) is \(P\).

Conversely, if \(n\equiv 1,3,4 \pmod5\) then one of the moves (either \(n-1\) or \(n-4\)) lands on a number that is \(0\) or \(2 \pmod5\), a \(P\)-position. Hence those \(n\) are \(N\)-positions.

So the set of *losing* positions for the player to move is exactly
\[
\{\,n>0: n\bmod5\in\{0,2\}\,\}.
\]

---

### 3.  When does Bob have a guaranteed win?

Bob moves second.  
If the initial pile size \(n\) is a \(P\)-position, the first player (Alice) is in trouble: whatever she does will leave Bob in an \(N\)-position, from which Bob can move to a \(P\)-position, and ultimately force the last move.  
Hence **Bob can guarantee a win exactly when \(n\equiv0\) or \(2\pmod5\).**
