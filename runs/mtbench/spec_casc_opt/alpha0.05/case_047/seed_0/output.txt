<|end|><|start|>assistant<|channel|>final<|message|>### 1. What the two terms mean

| Term | What it means in the language of the system | What it means for the surroundings |
|------|--------------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| **Exothermic** | The reaction *releases* energy into the surroundings. | The surroundings get warmer. |
| **Endothermic** | The reaction *absorbs* energy from the surroundings. | The surroundings get cooler. |

In everyday language you might say *“the reaction is hot”* (exothermic) or *“the reaction takes in heat”* (endothermic).

---

### 2. The scientific criterion

The smallest‑scale tool that lets us decide whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic is the **enthalpy change** (ΔH):

\[
\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}
\]

* **ΔH < 0**  → **Exothermic**.  The system’s enthalpy drops; the missing energy is supplied up to the surroundings as heat.
* **ΔH > 0**  → **Endothermic**.  The system’s enthalpy rises; the excess energy is taken from the surroundings as heat.

In a constant‑pressure process (most chemistry labs, everyday burners, solar panels), this is the exact criterion you need to know. (In practice, you use a calorimeter or a thermocouple to measure the heat flow and record the sign of ΔH.)

---

### 3. Why the sign can flip (or stay the same)

The sign of ΔH is the *difference in net bond‑energy* between the nucleate (reactants) and product (reactants).  
* \( \text{C–H bonds} + \text{O–O bonds}\)   →   \(\text{C–O bonds} + \text{O–H}\) (CH₄ combustion).  
  Breaking bonds → *more energy* required.  
  Forming bonds → *greater energy* released.  
  The net of the two is a *net* release → ΔH < 0.

---

### 4. A real‑world example

**Exothermic combustion of a candle**  
\[
\boxed{\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{BF}_2 → \text{BF}_3 \text{ (candle)}}
\]

(When a candle burns, the wax, a coal‑derivative, reacts with atmospheric oxygen to produce a mixture of CO₂, H₂O, and various by‑products. The difference in enthalpy is about **-4.5 kJ mol⁻¹** of wax consumed.)

* **What you see:** The flame produces a warm glow.  
* **What you feel:** The air around the flame is hotter than the surrounding room.  
* **ΔH:** Negative (≈ –4.5 kJ mol⁻¹); fast, spontaneous.

---

### 5. A quick “golden rule” to keep in mind

| Question | Rule of thumb |
|----------|----------------|
| “Does the system give heat to it?” | If yes → exothermic (ΔH < 0). |
| “Does the system take heat from it?” | If yes → endothermic (ΔH > 0). |

> **Why it works** – In a constant‑pressure, food‑scale process:  
> \[
> H_{\text{total}} = U + PV
> \]
> The difference in *U* (internal energy) and *PV* work matches the heat exchanged, which is precisely what ΔH tells us.

### 6. Quick cheat‑sheet

| Strong property | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|-----------------|------------|-------------|
| ΔH | < 0 | > 0 |
| Surroundings | Warm (heat) | Cool (heat) |
| Temperature of system | Tends to drop, but can rise if heat is removed | Tends to rise if heat is absorbed |
| Main driving force | Formation of bonds that are more stable | Breaking bonds that are more stable |
| Example | Burning wood, combustion of gasoline | Melting ice, photosynthesis, cooking an egg |

---

### 7. Summary

1. **Exothermic reactions** are produced by a net release of energy: ΔH < 0.  
2. **Endothermic reactions** absorb energy: ΔH > 0.  
3. The measure of *heat* you encounter (warmth is exothermic, cool feeling is endothermic) is a direct reflection of the enthalpy change.  
4. **Real‑world example** – a candle: the wax combusts, release heat, and the room feels warm, making the reaction exothermic.

Use the sign of ΔH (or the direction of heat flow) to decide whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic. That’s the reliable, textbook‑approved criterion.
