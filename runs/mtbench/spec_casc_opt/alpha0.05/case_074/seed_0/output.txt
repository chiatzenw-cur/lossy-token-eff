When solid calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) is mixed with aqueous hydrochloric acid (HCl) the carbonate reacts with the carbonate ions to give a soluble salt, a liquid and a gas.  The reaction is a classic acid–base reaction (double‑displacement reaction) that proceeds in two phases:

1. **Solubilization of the carbonate** – the solid CaCO₃ slowly dissolves into the acid producing a “carbonate” solution that is then consumed by the acid.
2. **Neutralisation & CO₂ release** – HCl is consumed by the carbonate, yielding water and CO₂ gas. As the solid dissolves, the rate of gas evolution is steady.

**Balanced equation**

\[
\boxed{\text{CaCO}_{3(s)} + 2\,\text{HCl}_{(aq)} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{CaCl}_{2(aq)} + \text{CO}_{2(g)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O}_{(l)}}
\]

- Numbers of atoms are balanced:  
  – Ca: 1 on each side  
  – C: 1 on each side  
  – O: 3 (from CaCO₃) + 2 (from 2 HCl) = 5 (from CaCO₃) + 3 (from CO₂) + 1 (from H₂O) = 5  
  – H: 2 and 2 = 2.

**Type of reaction**

- **Acid–base (or neutralisation) reaction**  
- It is also a **double displacement** (exchange) reaction: the carbonate anion of CaCO₃ is exchanged with chloride in HCl.
- Because it produces a gas, it is often called “acid–base” or “carbonate‑acid” reaction.

**Observations that that the reaction is happening**

| Observation | What it tells you |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Effervescence** (bubbles coming out of the liquid) | CO₂ gas is being released. |
| **Fast, audible fizzing** | The reaction is rapid and vigorous. |
| **No solid residue left** | The CaCO₃ has been consumed. |
| **Heat** (the mixture may feel warm) | Reactions are largely exothermic (though the acid and carbonate solution may be slightly endothermic at the surface, but it feels warmer overall). |
