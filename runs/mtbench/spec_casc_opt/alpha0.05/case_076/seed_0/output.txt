| Macro‑Circuits | Easter‑egg model |
|----------------|----------------|

**2. Fiscal policy – the “in‑hand” side of the economy**

1. **Fiscal expansion** *(acting on the government’s fiscal balance)*  
   * **Tools**: increased spending, tax cuts, transfer payments.  
   * **Short‑run effect**: ↑ national income → ↑ GDP, ↑ employment → ↑ inflation.  
   * **Expectations**: If the government runs a long‑run debt, the “crowding‑out” effect can push up real interest rates, potentially dragging GDP down.  
   * **Illustrative data**:  
     * 2020–2021 US COVID‑19 stimulus (+$1.9 trillion) and the Fed’s 0.25 % rate was rolled into an 8‑month GDP hit of +40% net growth. Inflation recorded a 13‑month spike in 2021, yet the *real* hourly rate stayed below 3 %.  
     * **Note**: The stimulus was *mostly* “exchange‑rate pass‑through” (common‑goods and services) that amplified domestic demand and pushed the price level upward.  

2. **Fiscal contraction** *(fiscal austerity)*  
   * **Tools**: tax cuts, spending cuts, debt‑to‑GDP changes.  
   * **Short‑run effect**: ↓ GDP, ↓ employment, ↓ inflation.  
   * **Common‑world effects**:  
     * *Stagflation risks* from Russia‑Ukraine war reduces commodity supplies → supply‑side shock + inflation.  
     * *Sovereign debt & fiscal discipline* (e.g., Greece in 2010–2015) can suppress inflation dramatically but push unemployment to 27 %.  

3. **Monetary‑fiscal interactions**  
   * **Fiscal multiplier**: 
     * **Demand‑side**: multiplier ≈ 1‑1.5 for a two‑thirds multiplier.  
     * **Supply‑side**: possible *backward‑looking* crowding‑out if higher fiscal deficits drive up high‑frequency rates.  
   * **Debt‑to‑GDP impact**: fiscal deficits that increase long‑term debt potential *raise* the expectation‑based inflation, especially in **low‑growth, high‑debt** scenarios.

**3. Monetary policy – the “hand‑off” side of the economy**

1. **Rate‑based policy**  
   * **Policy tool**: Fed’s federal funds rate (FRED), ECB’s Main Refinancing Operations).  
   * **Contractionary** → ↓~1 % of discretionary spending → ↑ inflation, ↑ unemployment, ↓GDP.  
   * **Expansionary** → ↓ rates → ↓ borrowing costs → ↑ consumption.  
   * **Key points**:  
     * **Lag**: 3–6 months per time the policy term equals the monetary **boom** (like the 2018 0.125 %‑to‑0.25 % policy).  
     * **Transmission**: Warm‑up — the central bank *synchronises* with *inflation expectations* and *long‑run credit*.

2. **Policy‑based inflation targeting**  
   * **Forward guidance**: The Fed’s “zoo” of 2021 publishing of $300 trillion “float” after Christmas is one of the most widely watched influence.  
   * **Outcome**: By “fixing” expectations, the central bank puts the target ladder into the central forecasting model, reducing the impetus for inflation.  

3. **Quantitative easing**  
   * **Place‑holding**: Buying volumes > $100 trillion in USD yields a ~10 % (almost all‑year) coefficient for the “risk‑adjusted yield” of the Treasury (shifted from 3.5 % to 2.0 %).  
   * **When applied**: A Fed can back‑stop the monetary policy to preserve the service‑use of the real economy—keeping the *coordinated* channel of inflation and employment alway well below the hyper‑inflation or recession.

4. **Monetary‑fiscal interplay** – *What happens when the policy house is shaken?*  
   * The Fed’s “data‑turbo” remarks can *force* the fiscal side to deliver more prudent spending.  
   * In the *diagonal* (fiscal exhaustion, debt‑broadening) the Fed raises rates expecting *higher* imports of borrowing power in the *risk‑sensitive* sector (the *Eisenberg channel*).
