1. **Identify the Claim (What the Argument Is Trying to Establish)**  
   *Why it matters:* Every argument is built around a central claim or thesis. If the claim is vague, contradictory, or poorly defined, the reader will have to chase them themselves.  
   *How to evaluate:* Ask: *What is the writer’s main claim?* Is it articulated in a single sentence or spread across several paragraphs? Is the claim testable—does it make a **predicative statement** that can be tested or contested?  

2. **Assess the Evidence (Transparency, Relevance, and Credibility)**  
   *Why it matters:* Evidence is the lifeblood of an argument. Weak or irrelevant evidence will not persuade the reader, even if the reasoning is sound.  
   *How to evaluate:*  
   - **Relevance:** Does each piece of evidence directly support the claim?  
   - **Credibility:** Are the sources reputable or do they come from a prior claim?  
   - **Rigor:** Are the data collected or cited properly (close‑caption, statistical significance, etc.)?  

3. **Analyze the Reasoning (Logical Structure and Coherence)**  
   *Why it matters:* Even if the evidence is good, the link between evidence and claim must be logical. Misleading or circular reasoning is a strong logical flaw.  
   *How to evaluate:*  
   - **Logical flow:** Does the argument follow a clear “premise ⇒ evidence → conclusion” structure?  
   - **Consistency:** Are there internal contradictions?  
   - **Fallacies:** Watch for eliminacies (e.g., hasty generalization, appeal to authority, straw‑man, or false dilemmas).  

4. **Uncover Hidden Assumptions and Biases**  
   *Why it matters:* An argument often depends on unstated premises that, if false, include a **weak point**.  
   *How to evaluate:*  
   - **Make assumptions explicit:** Identify which assumptions are needed for the argument to hold.  
   - **Test for bias:** Are the sources or viewpoints aligned with a particular ideology or economic stake?  
   - **Check for counter‑evidence:** Does the writer ignore data that contradicts the claim?  

5. **Consider Counterarguments and Alternative Interpretations**  
   *Why it matters:* A robust argument anticipates opposing views and either dismantles them or rebut them. Failure to handle counterpoints is a red flag that the argument is overly simplified.  
   *How to evaluate:*  
   - **Presence:** Does the writer acknowledge counterarguments?  
   - **Response:** Are the counterarguments acknowledged with substantive rebuttals?  
   - **Scope:** Does the argument allow for partial filters? If the claim is only “one plausible explanation,” it is preferable to an absolute claim.
