Think of probability as **the mind’s way of saying “how likely is this to happen?”**

| Probability | What it means | Everyday example |

## 1. The Building Blocks

| Term | What it is | How we write it |
|------|------------|-----------------|
| **Experiment** | A process or event that leads to a random outcome. | “Losing a coin” |
| **Sample Space** | All possible outcomes of an experiment. | “{Heads, Tails}” |
| **Event** | A subset of the sample space that we care about. | “Getting Heads” |
| **Outcome** | A single outcome. | “Heads” or “Tails” |

### Quick example  
**Experiment**: Draw a card from a standard deck.  
**Sample Space**: 52 cards (Ace, Queen, …, King of 4 suits).  
**Event**: Draw a heart.  
**Probability**: *Number of hearts* ÷ *Number of total cards* = 13 ÷ 52 = 0.25.

## 2. Calculating Basic Probability

### 2.1: Rule of Counting

\[
P(\text{Event}) = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Number of total outcomes}}
\]

### 2.2: Step‑by‑Step

1. **List the sample space** (or the size of it).  
2. **Identify favorable outcomes**.  
3. **Divide** (favorable ÷ total).

#### Example 1 – Rolling a die
- **What?** “What is the probability of rolling a 4?”  
- **Sample space**: {1,2,3,4,5,6}  
- **Favorable**: {4}  
- **P** = 4 ÷ 6 = **1/6 ≈ 0.1667** (16.7 %).

#### Example 2 – Picking a fruit
- **What?** “You have 3 apples, 2 oranges, 1 banana. How likely is it to pick an apple?”  
- **Sample space**: 6 fruits total.  
- **Favorable**: 3 apples.  
- **P** = 3 ÷ 6 = **0.5**.

## 3. Conditional Probability

When you know that something happened earlier, the probability of a later event can change.

\[
P(\text{B|A}) = \frac{P(\text{A and B})}{P(\text{A})}
\]

If we want to know:  

- **Question**: *What’s the probability that the sock was black, given that we saw a black sock?*  
- **Answer**: How many black socks are there?  
- **Answer**: `Black socks ÷ total socks`.  
  (If you already know a sock is black, the “given” part is just a factor.)

---

## 4. Binary Operations (Adding & Multiplying)

### 4.1: Additive Law (Mutually Exclusive)
- **If two events are mutually exclusive**, they can’t happen together.  
  \[
  P(A\cup B) = P(A) + P(B)
  \]
  *Example*: Probability of drawing a heart or a spade is 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5.

### 4.2: Multiplication Law (Independent)
- **If two events are independent**, the outcome of one doesn’t affect the other.  
  \[
  P(A\cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)
  \]
  *Example*: Toss a coin and roll a die.  
  P(Heads + D6) = 0.5 × 0.1667 = 0.0833.

## 6. Common Unexpected Traps

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| **“If it’s rare, it’s impossible.”** | Probability is not zero unless it is truly impossible. | Count the odds. |
| **“If one event is independent, everything else changes.”** | Independence means nothing else changes. | Test independence: \(P(A|B)=P(A)\). |
| **“Not adding up to 1.”** | Always double‑check for missing outcomes. | Sum all elementary probabilities to get 1. |

## 7. Why Is Probability Useful?

- **Games**: Weather prediction, card games, sports betting.  
- **Science**: Calculating chances of an event, assessing risk.  
- **Daily Life**: Deciding whether to bring an umbrella.
