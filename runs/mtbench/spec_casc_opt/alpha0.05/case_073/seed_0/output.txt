The central dogma is the foundational principle that describes how genetic information is transferred from the whole organism’s genome to the functional molecules that carry out cellular functions. It is best described as a one‑way flow:

```
DNA  →  RNA  →  Protein
```

In other words, **information is copied from DNA to RNA to protein**, but a protein cannot have a direct template for DNA or RNA (exceptions of reverse transcription such as retroviruses still apply but are NOT typical of vertebrate cellular processes).

---

### Processes Involved

| **Process** | **What Happens** | **Key Players** | **Why It Matters in the Canonical Flow** |
|-------------|------------------|-----------------|----------------------------------------|
| **DNA replication** | Copies the entire genome before cell division | DNA polymerase, helicase, ligase, primase | Ensures that the original genetic code is copied accurately for the next generation of cells. |
| **Transcription** (DNA → RNA) | RNA polymerase reads a DNA template, producing a complementary RNA strand | RNA polymerase (α, β, β′, ε, κ), σ‑factor, transcription factors | Generates the messenger RNA (mRNA) that carries the genetic information. |
| **RNA processing** (in eukaryotes) | Splicing, 5′‑capping, polyadenylation | Spliceosome, capsons, poly(A) polymerase, spliceosome | Produces a mature, stable mRNA ready for translation. |
| **Translation** (RNA → Protein) | Ribosomes read the mRNA codon, ribosomal RNA and tRNAs bring up amino acids | Ribosomal proteins, rRNA, tRNAs, aminoacyl‑tRNA synthetases, ribosomal enzymes | Builds the polypeptide chain that folds into a functional protein. |
| **Gene regulation** | Mechanisms controlling when/where a gene is transcribed | Transcription factors, enhancers, silencers, epigenetic marks | Determines which genes are expressed and the timing of the flow. |
| **Post‑translational processing** | Proteins attached to regulatory structures, cleaved, folded etc. | Chaperones, proteases, kinases, phosphatases | Converts the raw polypeptide into a functional protein. |
| **DNA repair & recombination** | Fixes mutations, cross‑links; also recombines genetic segments | DNA repair enzymes (BER, NER, HR, NHEJ), nucleases (e.g., RecA/Holcomb) | Protects the integrity of the genetic code and allows controlled evolution, but these steps ultimately still feed into the DNA‑to‑RNA‑to‑protein path. |

### Who Coined the Term?

The phrase **“central dogma”** was coined by **Francis Crick** in 1958. He introduced it in the paper *“On the origin of the genetic code”* (Published *Nature* in 1958) and in later writings such as *“The central dogma of molecular biology”* (Nature, 1958, 1959). Crick himself, of course, was part of the famous Watson–Crick partnership that described the double‑helix structure of DNA in 1953.

Although the term “central dogma” is often cited together with Crick’s 1958 biography, basic principles were already being discussed by the late 1950s by Watson, Crick, and others. The phrase “central dogma” entered popular scientific literature as an elegant way to encapsulate gene‑expression fundamentals.

---

### Legacy & Exceptions

| **Promise** | **Misinterpretation** | **Clarification** |
|-------------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| DNA → RNA → Protein only | No reverse transcription or reverse transcription | Reverse transcription (RNA → DNA) is a *violation* in the classic sense, but it is not part of the canonical flow in most eukaryote cells. |
| “One‑way” | Animals can't change proteins back to DNA | Some modern viruses and transposons do reverse transcription (e.g., retroviruses, EER); Crick used “dogma” to emphasize the flow’s directionality in biology. |

Thus, the **central dogma** describes the regulatory circuitry of molecular biology and how life‑history information is stored, transferred, and interpreted. It is the language that connects genetics, transcriptomics, and proteomics in a coherent, linear framework.
