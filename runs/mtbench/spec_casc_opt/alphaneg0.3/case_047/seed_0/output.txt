| Feature | **Exothermic** | **Endothermic** |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| What happens to the surroundings? | **Heat is released** into the surroundings. | **Heat is absorbed** from the surroundings. |
| Enthalpy change (ΔH) | **ΔH** < 0 (negative) | **ΔH** > 0 (positive) |
| Energy of products vs. reactants | Products carry **less** internal energy than reactants. | Products carry **more** internal energy than reactants. |
| Temperature of a closed system | Rises (unless heat is being siphoned elsewhere). | Falls (unless heat is supplied from outside). |
| Common thermodynamic diagram | ΔH plot shows a downward slope (the system “drops” energy). | ΔH plot shows an upward slope (the system “climbs” energy). |

**Criteria I used to distinguish the two**

1. **Sign of ΔH**  
   * ΔH is the standard enthalpy change of the reaction.  
   * A negative ΔH means the system lost energy (exothermic).  
   * A positive ΔH means the system gained energy (endothermic).

2. **Heat flow measured by calorimetry**  
   * In a bomb calorimeter or a coffee‑cup calorimeter, you watch the temperature rise or fall.  
   * A rise in the temperature of the calorimeter fluid indicates that the reaction released heat → exothermic.  
   * A fall in the temperature of the fluid indicates that the reaction absorbed heat → endothermic.

3. **Temperature change of the surroundings**  
   * In a sealed container, the air surrounding the reaction gets hotter (exothermic) or colder (endothermic).  
   * In a laboratory, you can place a thermocouple on the reaction vessel or measure the temperature of the surrounding water bath.

4. **The usual energy flow in reaction mechanisms**  
   * Exothermic reactions often involve the formation of bonds that are **stronger** than the bonds broken.  
   * Endothermic reactions often involve the breaking of bonds that are **stronger** than the ones formed, leading to a net energy absorption.

5. **Enthalpy of formation (ΔHf)**  
   * Comparing the sum of ΔHf of products vs. reactants lets you calculate ΔH directly.

### Real‑World Example: *Instant Cold Pack* vs. *Combustion of Wood*

| Reaction | Type | ΔH (approx.) | How it shows up in everyday life |
|----------|------|--------------|---------------------------------|
| **Dissolution of NH₄NO₃ in water (instant cold pack)** | Endothermic | +15.5 kJ mol⁻¹ | When you press the pack, the surface feels cold because the internal energy of the compact increases, pulling heat from your hand. |
| **Combustion of wood (CH₂O + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O)** | Exothermic | –285 kJ mol⁻¹ | The fire gets hot; the air and surrounding objects warm up. The released heat is what keeps the flames going. |

**Why the cold pack feels cold**

1. **Energy is absorbed:** The solute (NH₄NO₃) absorbs heat from the surrounding water to break its lattice and dissolve.  
2. **Heat is removed from surroundings:** The surrounding water and your hand lose heat, thus the sensation of cold.  
3. **Positive ΔH:** The enthalpy of the products (aqueous NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻) is higher than the system’s initial state.  

**Why the fire feels hot**

1. **Energy is released:** The breaking of C–H bonds in wood is coupled with the formation of stronger C–O bonds during combustion.  
2. **Heat is added to surroundings:** The surrounding air and objects absorb this energy, raising their temperature.  
3. **Negative ΔH:** The overall energy drops, reflecting heat flowing out of the system.

#### Bottom Line

- **Exothermic** = the system *loses* energy as heat → ΔH < 0.  
- **Endothermic** = the system *gains* energy as heat → ΔH > 0.  

You can spot the difference by watching the temperature of the surroundings or by calculating ΔH from standard enthalpies. The instant cold pack and a small campfire are everyday, tangible demonstrations of both processes.
