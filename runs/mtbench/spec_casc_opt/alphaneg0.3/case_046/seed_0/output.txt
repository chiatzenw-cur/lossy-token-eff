If the instantaneous radius of a satellite remains fixed at the point where its speed is lowered, the orbit can no longer stay circular – it becomes an ellipse whose perigee is that point.  
The satellite will move out to larger distances on average, and the mean orbital period will increase.  

Below is a step‑by‑step reasoning that shows why the radius grows and why the period becomes longer.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1.  Energy of a circular orbit

For a circular orbit of radius \(r\) around an Earth‑mass point \(M\)

\[
v_{\text{circ}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
\]

The specific orbital energy (energy per unit mass) is

\[
\varepsilon_{\text{circ}} = \frac{v_{\text{circ}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM}{r}
       =-\frac{GM}{2r} .
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2.  What happens when the speed is reduced

Suppose the satellite’s speed is instantaneously lowered from \(v_{\text{circ}}\) to \(v_{\text{new}}\) while it is at the same radial distance \(r\).

The new specific energy is

\[
\varepsilon_{\text{new}}
   = \frac{v_{\text{new}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM}{r}.
\]

Because \(v_{\text{new}}<v_{\text{circ}}\),

\[
\varepsilon_{\text{new}}
   > \frac{v_{\text{circ}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM}{r}
   = -\frac{GM}{2r},
\]

i.e. the energy is **less negative** (the satellite is less bound).

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3.  Semi‑major axis of the new orbit

For an elliptical orbit the specific energy is

\[
\varepsilon = -\frac{GM}{2\,a} ,
\]

where \(a\) is the semi‑major axis.  
Re‑arranging gives

\[
a =-\frac{GM}{2\varepsilon_{\text{new}}} .
\]

Because \(\varepsilon_{\text{new}}\) is less negative than \(\varepsilon_{\text{circ}}\),
the denominator \(2|\varepsilon_{\text{new}}|\) is **smaller**; hence

\[
a_{\text{new}} > a_{\text{circ}} = r .
\]

Thus the satellite’s average orbital radius (given by the semi‑major axis) **increases**.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4.  Perigee and apogee

At the point where the speed was reduced the particle is at the **perigee** of the new ellipse:

\[
r_{\text{perigee}} = r .
\]

The apogee \(r_{\text{apo}}\) is found from the conservation of angular momentum:

\[
h = r\,v_{\text{new}} = \sqrt{GM\, a_{\text{new}}(1-e^{2})} ,
\]

with \(e\) the orbital eccentricity.  Solving for \(r_{\text{apo}}\) gives

\[
r_{\text{apo}} = a_{\text{new}}\,(1+e) > r .
\]

So the orbit is elongated: the satellite swings out farther from the Earth between perigee and apogee.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5.  Period of revolution

Kepler’s third law for any ellipse (or any orbit)

\[
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{GM}}
\]

shows that the orbital period depends on the semi‑major axis.  
Since \(a_{\text{new}}>a_{\text{circ}}\),

\[
T_{\text{new}} > T_{\text{circ}} .
\]

Thus the satellite takes longer to complete one revolution.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6.  Summary

| Quantity | Before speed reduction | After speed reduction |
|---------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Radius (instantaneous at the point of speed reduction) | \(r\) | \(r\) (perigee) |
| Semi‑major axis | \(a = r\) | \(a > r\) |
| Orbital period | \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^{3}/GM}\) | \(T' > T\) |
| Orbital shape | Circular | Elliptical, with perigee at \(r\) and apogee \(> r\) |

**Conclusion:**  
When the speed of a satellite in a circular orbit is deliberately reduced, the satellite can no longer stay circular; it enters an ellipse that has a **larger semi‑major axis**. Consequently, the apogee radius is greater than before and the orbital period increases. This follows directly from the vis‑civae (vis‑civae) equations and Kepler’s laws.
