The line “Thomas is very healthy” normally means he isn’t physically ill in the ordinary sense—he’s fit, he can run, he has no chronic symptoms. | **Scenario** | **Why it happens** | **Typical signs you might see** |
|--------------|---------------------|--------------------------------|

### Why the phrasing “very healthy”

The clue is a misdirection.  In a riddle, “healthy” could be a red herring – the person may be a *clinical* healthy man in the sense that he has 100 % normal physiology, but he still has an extraordinary need for the hospital’s daily service because of the selected treatment protocol or because it’s his job.

### The most common “real world” answers

1. **Diabetes – daily insulin and blood‑sugar monitoring**  
   – He’s a type‑1 diabetic with a meticulously scheduled insulin dose that must be checked continuously to avoid hypoglycemia.

2. **Dialysis – a daily infusion of renal replacement**  
   – Here “visible” health concerns appear only once a week, but the patient is healthy enough to live independently.

3. **Post‑surgery / heart‑failure rehab**  
   – He was operated for a heart transplant and has to stay on a daily in‑hospital rehab regimen.

4. **Medical staff shift**  
   – He earns his living in the hospital, so he is physically healthy but is physically in the hospital every day.

5. **Clinical trial**  
   – He is a highly motivated volunteer giving a drug daily for research, so he is "healthy" but obligated to come to the hospital each day.

When you read the question, the simplest answer is usually the one that fits the wording: **Thomas is technology‑heavy (e.g., a pacemaker or a transplanted organ) that requires daily monitoring, or he has a daily therapy that is only useful if he’s in the hospital**. The alternative (with medical staff) is a clever twist that can be made into a great joke or a play on words.
