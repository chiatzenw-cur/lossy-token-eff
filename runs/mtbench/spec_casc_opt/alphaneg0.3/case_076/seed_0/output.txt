### 2.  The *Phillips Curve* – Short‑run trade‑off

- **Classic (1950s–1960s)**:  
  *Higher unemployment ↔ Lower inflation*  
  This positive relationship was deemed a “safe‑haven” for policymakers: employers bid up wages → costs rise → inflation, while in a downturn wage growth slows.  

- **Modern (1980s–present)**:  
  The curve is shallow; *inflation expectations* dominate.  
  - *When expectations are anchored* (e.g., a credible central bank), the gap between unemployment and the *natural rate* (NAIRU) has very little effect on inflation.  
  - *When expectations are unanchored* (e.g., a sudden supply shock), a higher unemployment can indeed dampen inflation, but the cost is prolonged joblessness.

**Key insight for policy:**  
- **Monetary easing** (lower rates, higher money supply) is typically aimed at cutting unemployment but can (if expectations shift) lead to higher inflation once the economy *re‑reaches* its potential output.

### 3.  Empirical correlations over the last 70 years

| Period | GDP growth | Inflation | Unemployment | Observed pattern |
|--------|------------|-----------|--------------|------------------|
| 1950–60s (post‑war boom) | +4–5 % | 2–4 % | 3–4 % | Positive GDP–inflation trade‑off, low unemployment. |
| 1970s (stagflation) | 0–1 % | 5–12 % | 6–9 % | Negative GDP–inflation link; unemployment spiked while inflation surged. |
| 1990s–2000s (expansion) | 2–3 % | 1–3 % | 4–5 % | Negative GDP–inflation trend; unemployment fell while inflation remained flat. |
| 2008–2010 (crisis) | –0.1 % | 1 % | 9–10 % | GDP contraction → inflation fell (deflationary pressure), unemployment skyrocketed. |

**Takeaway:**  
The relationship is **time‑variant**; in “normal” times care usually focuses on maintaining low unemployment while keeping inflation near the 2 % target. In shocks (supply or demand), the link can reverse.

### 4.  Fiscal policy: The *Finances‑to‑Product* channel

| Tool | What the policy does | Typical effect on GDP → Inflation → Unemployment |
|------|----------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| **Government spending (automatic/ discretionary)** | ↑ G. Cuts taxes or increases expenditures directly. | **↑ GDP** (shift AD right). If economy is below potential, often keeps inflation low. **↓ Unemployment** (job creation). At full‑capacity, can **↑ inflation**. |
| **Tax policy** | ↓ taxes → ↑ Disposable income. | Similar to spending: ↑ GDP, ↑ inflation potential, ↓ unemployment. |
| **Tax‑on‑investment credits** | Encourages new business capital. | Long‑term boost to potential output (↑ A in AD‑AS), potentially lowering inflation via productivity gains. |
| **Budget deficits** | Financing swings the money supply when backed by central bank. | If financed by banking (not monetized), adds credit into system; if monetized, can spark inflation. |

**Illustrations**

- **Great Recession** – 2010–2013 US fiscal stimulus (TTIP, cuts) increased GDP growth by 1–2 pp, slashed unemployment from 10 % to 6 %, but limited the rise in inflation.  
- **COVID‑19 stimulus** – In the U.S., the CARES Act ($2.2 trillion) pushed spending $1.5–2 trillion, bankrollting GDP growth of 13 % in Q2 2020 while unemployment fell from 11 % to 3.5 % by 2021.

**Key rules of thumb**

1. **Multiplier ≈ 1.5–2** on *stimulus* when the economy is below its potential, lower when at/above potential.  
2. **Time lag** – 6–12 months for fiscal stimulus to fully affect GDP, 12–18 months for unemployment.  
3. **Fiscal space** – Over‑financing can distort real interest rates and crowd out private investment if the central bank intervenes.

### 5.  Monetary policy: Ramping the *Demand‑side* multiplier

| Tool | What the policy do | Impact on GDP/Inflation/Unemployment |
|------|--------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Short‑term nominal interest rate**   |  Cuts lower rates → borrowing cheaper. | ↑ GDP through higher consumption & investment; ↓ unemployment; ↑ inflation if expectations rise. |
| **Quantitative easing (QE)** | Central bank buys long‑term securities → compresses long‑term rates → credit‑expansion. | ↓ treasury yields → lower corporate & mortgage rates, supporting AD. |
| **Forward guidance** | Communication of future policy path. | Lowers expectations for rates → boosts current demand. |
| **Inflation targeting** | Credible 2 % target. | Anchors expectations → deflates hysteresis. |

**How it works in the AD‑AS framework**

- **Lower rates** ↔ *Lower discount rate* for firms → higher investment → **AD shift right**.  
- **Elasticity of demand** determines breadth: if firms spend more, AD shift wide; if they are idle, shift narrow.  
- **Potential output** (vertical AS curve) stays constant in the short run.  
- **Inflation** rises when AD moves above potential output.

**Historical examples**

| Period | Action | Outcome |
|--------|--------|---------|
| 2007–2010 (great crisis) | 2‑3 % RRP; QE1 | GDP contraction slowed to 0.3 %/yr; unemployment rose to 10 %; inflation fell to 2 %. |
| 2019–2020 (pre‑pandemic) | 0‑0.25 % policy; QE2 | GDP stable; inflation around 2 %; unemployment ~3.5 %. |
| 2023–2024 (post‑pandemic) | Rising rates → 3.5 %. | GDP slowed 1–2 %; unemployment climbs from 3.5 % to 4.5 %; inflation moves toward 2 % from 7 %. |

*Trade‑offs:*  
- **Monetary tightening** slows GDP growth, lifts unemployment, but **curbs inflation**.  
- **Monetary easing** has opposite effects.

### 6.  Macro‑policy “menu” – Matching tools to conditions

| Situation | Preferred policy mix & why |
|-----------|----------------------------|
| **Recession, low output** | **Fiscal stimulus + monetary easing** or **QE** – expand AD, lower costs, keep inflation low. |
| **Inflationary overshoot** | **Monetary tightening (raise rates)** – tighten liquidity, lower cost of credit. **Fiscal tightening** is usually delayed due to election cycles. |
| **Stagflation (high unemployment + high inflation)** | **Supply‑side policy** (tax cuts, deregulation), accompanied by **monetary tightening**. |
| **Re‑expansion, overheating** | **Monetary tightening** first; fiscal stimulus if growth still needed; monitor inflation expectations. |

---

### 7.  Important nuances

| Nuance | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Expectations matter** | If expectations for inflation rise, even modest AD shifts will perpetuate inflation; central banks use forward guidance to anchor. |
| **Inflation expectations can be self‑fulfilling** | In markets, if price‑setting workers expect higher future CPI, wages rise → even if inflation remains low, a wage‑price spiral can materialize. |
| **Time lag** | Fiscal policy lags GDP effects by 6–12 months, fiscal policy lags inflation/Unemployment by 12–18 months. Monetary policy actions have a lag of 12–18 months. |
| **Crowding‑out** | Large fiscal deficits can raise short‑term rates (if not offset by monetary easing), partially offseting investment. |
| **Potential output** | Long‑run growth is driven by technology (A, TFP) and investment; policy can’t change the slope of the AS curve, only shift the economy left/right. |

### 8.  Bottom line for a policy‑oriented analyst

| Indicator | What it tells you | Policy lever | Immediate direction ↑/↓ |
|-----------|------------------|-------------|------------------------|
| **GDP growth** | Real capacity utilization | Fiscal spending; monetary easing | ↑ |
| **Inflation** | Price dynamics | Interest rate hikes; expectation‑anchoring | ↓ (if above target) |
| **Unemployment** | Labour market slack | Fiscal stimulus; fiscal‑monetary coordination | ↓ (if above natural rate) |

**Correlation rule:**  
- High‑growth periods → **(High GDP)** → **reducing unemployment** → **potential inflation**.  
- Policy responds by *tightening* to keep inflation within target while *supporting* the labour market.  

**Policy toolbox:**  
- *Debt‑over‑the‑counter*: Fiscal stimulus during sufficiency gaps.  
- *Rate‑all‑the‑time*: Raise rates to keep inflation from spiralling, especially when output approaches or exceeds potential.  
- *Quantitative easing*: Credit‑expansion when short‑term rates hit the zero‑lower bound.

Takeaway: GDP, inflation, and unemployment are jointly determined. They co‑migrate when the economy is in a demand‑dominated regime, while supply shocks can invert this relationship. Fiscal and monetary tools are the levers they ‘engine of the economy’. Balancing them in the right sequence and with the right magnitude is key to sustaining growth, keeping prices stable, and safeguarding full employment.
