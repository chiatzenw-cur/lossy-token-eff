The central dogma is the fundamental framework that explains how genetic information is transferred from the hereditary material of a cell to the functional molecules that perform the cell’s work.  In its simplest, most widely‑accepted form it states:

> **DNA → RNA → Protein**

In other words, the sequence of nucleotides in DNA is first transcribed into RNA, and that RNA is then translated into a polypeptide chain (protein).  The dogma is not a single reaction but a chain of interconnected biological processes that together ensure that the genetic blueprint encoded in a cell’s DNA is faithfully expressed as functional proteins.

**Other relevant flows**

* **Replication (DNA → DNA)** – not part of the “information flow” but crucial for copying the genome.
* **Transcriptional regulation** – promoter/enhancer elements, transcription factors, chromatin state – modulate how much and when RNA is made.
* **RNA‑based regulation** – miRNAs, siRNAs, long non‑coding RNAs reduce or modify the flow of information.
* **Retrotranscription (RNA → DNA)** – occurs in retroviruses and some transposons, but is generally an exception rather than a rule.

### Who Named the Concept?

- **Francis Crick** (British physicist‑biologist, co‑discoverer of the DNA double helix) first articulated the central dogma in **1958**.  
  - He introduced it in his paper **“On Protein Synthesis”** (Scientific American, 1958) and later in formal academic papers.
  - The phrase “central dogma” was coined to describe the unidirectional flow of hereditary information (“DNA → RNA → Protein”) that he believed characterized the core of genetic information transfer.

Crick’s original statement notes that while some processes (e.g., reverse transcription in retroviruses) are exceptions, the “core” describes how hereditary information is encoded in DNA and comes to bear as functional macromolecules.  Subsequent refinements (e.g., the RNA world hypothesis, non‑coding RNAs) have expanded the dogma, but the core tenet remains the best‑known explanation of the flow of genetic information.
