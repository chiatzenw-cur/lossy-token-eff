### Applying the Methods: A Mini‑Case Study

Take, for example, the **Socratic debate on “justice”** (replicated in Plato’s *Republic*).  
1. **Socratic Questioning**: Socrates asks “What is justice?”  
2. **Irony**: He “knew” none of the answers he receives.  
3. **Dialectic**: He systematically deconstructs each answer, asking “Why…?”  
4. **Dismissal of Metaphors**: Large, sweeping metaphors (“justice is a good idea”) are collapsed instead of accepted.  
5. **Hypothesis**: “If we were to live in a city where justice is something, what would it look like?”  
6. **Refutation**: Socrates shows any attempt to define justice in military terms, or as a function of pleasure, leads to contradictions.  
7. **Exemplary Figures**: He uses the characteristics of corrupt politicians or examples of the Spartans.  
8. **Moral Inquiry**: Shifts to “being just” in everyday life.  
9. **Public Forum**: Questions Thrasymachus—claimed that justice is the advantage of the stronger—on the floor of the Athenian courts.  
10. **Self‑Critique**: When he is charged with corrupting youth, he again asks what the evidence of that is, exposing the lack of a solid foundation for the charge.

After each iteration, the previously natural‑sounding assertion of “justice” becomes a contested, definable concept.
