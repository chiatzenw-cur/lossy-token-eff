> **Goal:** Understand what probability really means, how we calculate it, and why it matters in everyday life.

### 1️⃣ The Intuition Behind Probability

Think of probability as *“how likely is something to happen?”* It’s a number between **0** (impossible) and **1** (certain).  
- **0.5** = 50 % → it’s a coin flip: heads or tails, each is equally likely.  
- **0.01** = 1 % → a rare event (like winning a lottery).  
- **1** = 100 % → a sure thing (your birthday occurs every year).

---

### 2️⃣ The Basic Formula

```
Probability (P) = (Number of favorable outcomes) / (Total possible outcomes)
```

- **Favorable outcomes** are the ways the event you care about can happen.
- **Total possible outcomes** are all ways the experiment can go, regardless of your event.

> **Example 1 – Tossing a coin**
> - Favorable: 1 (heads)
> - Total: 2 (heads or tails)
> - P(heads) = 1/2 = 0.5 or 50 %

> **Example 2 – Rolling a six‑sided die (looking for an even number)**
> - Favorable: 2, 4, 6 → 3 outcomes
> - Total: 6 (1–6)
> - P(even) = 3/6 = 0.5

---

### 3️⃣ Everyday Scenarios

| Scenario | Question | Possible Outcomes | Favorable | Probability |
|----------|----------|-------------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Choosing a snack** from a box containing 4 chips, 3 chocolates, 2 cookies | What's the chance you pick a chocolate? | 4+3+2 = 9 pieces | 3 chocolates | 3/9 = 1/3 ≈ 33 % |
| **Stormy weather in Seattle** on any given day | Will it rain? | Roughly 50/50 (historical data) | Rain | 0.5 |
| **Drawing a card** from a standard deck, looking for an Ace | Will you draw an Ace? | 52 cards | 4 Aces | 4/52 = 1/13 ≈ 7.7 % |

These simple counts help you see probability as **“counting”** — which fits perfectly with kids’ math learning, dice games, and trivia.

---

### 4️⃣ Two Essential Rules

1. **Complement Rule**  
   *If you know the probability of something happening, you automatically know the probability of it **not** happening.*  
   - `P(not A) = 1 – P(A)`
   - **Example:** `P(not even number on a die) = 1 – 0.5 = 0.5`

2. **Addition Rule (for mutually exclusive events)**  
   *If two events cannot happen at the same time, the probability of either happening equals the sum of their probabilities.*  
   - `P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)`
   - **Example:** Rolling a “1” or a “2” on a die:  
     `P(1) = 1/6`, `P(2) = 1/6` → `P(1 or 2) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3`

> **Tip:** Check “mutually exclusive” first. If they can happen together, you’ll need to use the **inclusion–exclusion principle**.

---

### 5️⃣ Independence & Conditional Probability

- **Independent events**: One event doesn’t affect the other.  
  *Example:* Rolling a die once, then again. The first roll does not change the odds of the second roll.

- **Conditional probability**: Probability of *B* given that *A* has occurred.  
  `P(B | A) = P(A and B) / P(A)`  
  *Example:* After drawing two cards from a deck (without replacement), what's the probability the second card is a King given the first was not?
  
> **Why it matters:** In real life, many events are *not* independent (e.g., the weather today influences tomorrow’s weather). Probability helps us model those dependencies.

---

### 6️⃣ A Fun “Real‑World” Example: The Birthday Paradox

> **Question:** In a group of 23 people, what's the chance that *at least two* share a birthday?  
> **Answer:** ~50.7 %.  
> It looks counterintuitive because there are 365 possible birthdays, but the math shows a surprisingly high chance!

*Why it’s useful:* This demonstrates how “combinatorial counting” (arranging numbers/people) can produce counterintuitive probabilities.

### 7️⃣ Quick “Probability in Action” Mini‑Exercise

**Problem:** You have a jar with 5 red marbles, 3 blue marbles, and 2 green marbles. You pull one marble out.

a) What’s the probability it's **red**?  
b) What’s the probability it's **not blue**?  
c) If you replace the marble and pull again, what’s the probability you get a red and then a green in that order? (Assume replacement, so the counts stay the same.)

**Solve:**

- a) `P(red) = 5/(5+3+2) = 5/10 = 0.5` (50 %)
- b) `P(not blue) = 1 – P(blue)`.  
  `P(blue) = 3/10 = 0.3` → `P(not blue) = 0.7` (70 %)
- c) `P(red then green) = P(red) × P(green)` (independent events because of replacement)  
  `= 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1` (10 %)

Test yourself! If you had substitution (no replacement), the probability would be different (i.e., 5/10 × 2/9 = 10 %).
