## 2.  United States Antitrust Framework  

### 2.1  Statutory Foundations  

| Statute | Year | Key Provisions |
|---------|------|----------------|
| **Sherman Act** | 1890 (Section 1: prohibits conspiracies; §2: tackles monopolization) | Provides a **consumer‑welfare width**: any conduct that “may affect competition” is unlawful. |
| **Clayton Act** | 1914 (Sections 2–8: price‑discrimination, exclusive dealing, tying, mergers, etc.) | Adds more granular prohibitions and **merger‑rules** (Section 7). |
| **Federal Trade Commission Act** | 1914 | Creates the FTC, empowers it to issue orders, and enables civil remedies. |
| **Hart‑Scott‑Rodino Antitrust Improvements Act** | 1976 | Mandates pre‑merger notification and waiting periods for large transactions. |

### 2.2  Legal Tests  

| Test | When it applies | What it looks for |
|------|-----------------|--------------------|
| **“Per‑se”** | Typically for clear, obvious monopolistic conduct (e.g., predatory pricing, exclusive dealing that forecloses competitors). | The conduct is illegal in itself, no need for economic analysis. |
| **Rule of Reason** | For "moderately restrictive" behavior not clearly “per‑se.” | Must establish that the conduct has a *predatory* or *anti‑competitive* effect *over* its pro‑competitive benefits. Economic evidence is required. |
| **MERIT‑Test (in the context of vertical restraints)** | Applications to vertical agreements. | Assesses whether the restraint defers competition after reasonable without anticompetitive effects. |

### 2.3  Enforcement Doctrine Highlights  

* **Consumer welfare** is the most widely adopted yardstick.  
* **De‑mergers** are rarely used as policy instruments.  
* **Private litigation** coexists with federal action; individuals or entities can sue under the Sherman Act.  
* The **“economies of scale”** argument is a common defense for large firms.

## 3.  China’s Anti‑Monopoly Law (AML)  

### 3.1  Legislative Milestones  

| Year | Legislative act | Main changes |
|------|-----------------|--------------|
| 1993 | First AML (Weak, mainly a green‑field legal window) | Roughly an amending tool, no enforcement body. |
| 2008 | Revised AML and “Competition Law” (still very limited in scope) | Created the *State Administration for Market Regulation* (SAMR). |
| 2018 | Further amendments: broader definitions, higher penalties, more judicial autonomy | Established the *Administration of Market Regulation* (AMR) and the *National Administration for Market Regulation* (NAMR). |

### 3.2  Key Provisions  

| Section | Focus | Notable points |
|---------|-------|----------------|
| **Section 2** | Defines monopoly | Holding a **“significant influence”** (e.g., >20% market share or “titular power”) over “the industry or market.” |
| **Section 4** | Restrictions on “unreasonable restrictions of competition” | Focuses on *coercive* conduct, independent from a “rule‑of‑reason” analysis. |
| **Section 9** | Mergers & acquisitions | *Pre‑approval* and *post‑approval* review; thresholds > 2 billion RMB (≈$300 M) trigger it. |
| **Section 15** | Enforcement | Penalties up to **10% of annual revenue** for violations. |

### 3.3  Enforcement & Institutional Context  

* **Administrative** rather than judicial: **CCIC** (Competition, Consumer Affairs Commission) investigates and levies penalties.  
* **Ongoing Supreme Party & State oversight** –  the Communist Party maintains a policy platform that requires compliance with broader socio‑economic goals.  
* **“Social responsibility”** is embedded: the law cites not only economic harm but also “social interest” violations (e.g., “fair allegations” of “discriminatory pricing”).  
* **Private suits** exist but are “secondary” and usually “negligence‑based,” rarely invoked as the primary tool.

## 6.  Comparative Analysis of Key Differences  

| Dimension | US Law | China AML |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| **Legal Doctrine** | *Consumer welfare*, “rule‑of‑reason” & “per‑se” analysis. | *Market‑efficiency* + *social interest*; emphasis on *coercive* conduct. |
| **Regulatory Body** | FTC, DOJ, and U.S. Courts. | SAMR/CCIC/NAMR. |
| **Enforcement Process** | **Litigations** by private parties + **administrative orders**. | **Administrative investigations** → sanctions → judicial review limited. |
| **Penalty Structure** | Monetary, structural remedies, injunctive relief. | Commission‑based fines (up to 10% of revenue); may order structural change; limited step‑by‑step eviction. |
| **Subsidiary/Joint Ventures** | Firm‑level analysis; stakeholder side deals possible. | Often penalize *parent* company if rival parties unconstrained. |
| **Global Footprint** | US firms adapt; cross‑border mergers reviewed in 2‑stage “pre‑approval.” | Chinese firms more likely to face domestic‑only review but can also hit cross‑border relations (e.g., Ant Group). |
| **Political Control** | Operates within a non‑party logic. | Party’s Secretary attaches policy expectations (national security, digitisation strategy). |
| **Industry Focus** | Great for fintech, pharmaceuticals, telecommunications. | In recent years, heavy focus on **tech giants**, **e‑commerce**, **payments**, and **online media**. |

**Underlying Reason for Differences**  
- **Historical roots**: The U.S. began antitrust as a reaction to *railroad monopolies* of the Gilded Age, where the focus was on *private* economic interest.  
- **Policy context**: China’s modernisation and the goals of “socialist market economy” emphasize *state strategic goals*, which the AML treats as a part of competition enforcement.
