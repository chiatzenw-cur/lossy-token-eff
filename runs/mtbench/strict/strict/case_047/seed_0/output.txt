### 1. What I Used to Distinguish Between Them

1. **Heat Flow Direction** – The most intuitive criterion: if the reaction **releases** heat to the environment, it is exothermic; if it **takes in** heat, it is endothermic.
2. **Enthalpy Sign (ΔH)** – In practice the enthalpy change is the quantitative descriptor:  
   - ΔH < 0 → exothermic (heat produced).  
   - ΔH > 0 → endothermic (heat required).
3. **Internal Energy Change (ΔU)** – Same sign as ΔH for reactions conducted at constant volume.  
4. **Thermodynamic Implications** – The Gibbs free‑energy equation (ΔG = ΔH – TΔS) indicates that even endothermic reactions can be spontaneous if the entropy increase (TΔS) is large enough, but for the purpose of distinguishing exo/endo we focus purely on the enthalpy sign and heat flow.

> **Bottom line for classification:**  
> **Exothermic** ⇔ |system → surroundings| & ΔH < 0.  
> **Endothermic** ⇔ |surroundings → system| & ΔH > 0.

### 2. Real‑World Examples

| Process | Classification | Why it's exo/endo | Practical consequence |
|--------|----------------|------------------|----------------------|
| **Burning of a candle (wax → CO₂ + H₂O)** | **Exothermic** | Combustion releases a large amount of heat (ΔH ≈ –286 kJ mol⁻¹ for the reaction of methane; wax behaves similarly). | The flame stays lit and radiates heat. |
| **Freezing of water in a freezer** | **Endothermic** | The water absorbs heat from the freezer cabinet to change from liquid to solid (ΔH ≈ +6 kJ mol⁻¹ for freezing). | The freezer’s thermostat cycles harder to keep the temperature down. |
| **Ice pack “cold pack” (ammonium nitrate + water)** | **Endothermic** | Dissolving ammonium nitrate absorbs heat (ΔH ≈ +26 kJ mol⁻¹). | The pack gets cold; useful for first aid or keeping drinks chilled. |
| **Photosynthesis in plants** | **Endothermic** (overall) | The assimilation of CO₂ and H₂O into glucose requires energy (~+2800 kJ mol⁻¹ for six CO₂ to one glucose). | Plants grow; energy stored in biomass. |
| **Melting of ice (physical change, not chemical)** | **Endothermic** | Requires latent heat (~333 kJ kg⁻¹) to break hydrogen bonds. | Ice melts, cooling the surrounding air or liquid. |

### 3. A Concrete “Day‑to‑Day” Illustration

**Scenario:** You have a bag of “instant cold pack” (endothermic) and a tea kettle (exothermic).

- **Instant Cold Pack:** You crush the inner pouch; ammonium nitrate dissolves in water, absorbing about 10 kJ of heat from the surrounding air → the pack feels cold and helps relieve swelling or bruising.  
- **Tea Kettle Heating Water:** The burner supplies energy; as water boils, steam carries away the heat. Once the heat source is turned off, the kettle and the water inside start cooling, but in the process of heating the water the *kettle’s* cold metal was warmed up, *outside* the reaction itself the energy flow was *from* the flame to the water (exothermic relative to the flame).

---

### 4. Quick Decision Flow

1. **Ask:** “Does the reaction give heat to the environment or take heat from it?”  
2. **Check ΔH** (if available):  
   - Negative → exothermic.  
   - Positive → endothermic.  
3. **Observe surroundings** (temperature rise/fall) **– optional confirmation**.

By comparing these two pieces of information—heat flow direction and sign of ΔH—you can reliably categorize any reaction as exothermic or endothermic.
