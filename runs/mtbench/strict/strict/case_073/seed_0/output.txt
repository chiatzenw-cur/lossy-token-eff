> **Brief statement:** *“Information flows from DNA → RNA → protein.”*  

This was first formalized by **Francis Crick** in 1958, and he famously presented it as the “central dogma” in a lecture to the Royal Society of London. The notion has since been refined to include additional nuances—such as reverse transcription in retroviruses—but the core concept remains the same.

---

### 1. The Three Core Processes

| Process | Function | Key Enzymes/Complexes | Typical Outcome |
|---------|----------|-----------------------|---------------- |
| **DNA Replication** | Generates a chromatids identical to the original, ensuring faithful inheritance. | **DNA polymerases** (α, δ, ε, etc.), helicase, ligase, primase, single‑strand binding proteins | Double‑stranded DNA copies |
| **Transcription** | Converts a stretch of DNA into a complementary RNA copy (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, or regulatory RNAs). | **RNA polymerases** (RNAP II for mRNA in eukaryotes, RNAP I & III for rRNA & tRNA, bacterial RNAP), transcription factors | Single‑stranded RNA transcript |
| **Translation** | Interprets the mRNA codon sequence to assemble a specific amino‑acid chain (protein). | **Ribosome** (large and small subunits), **tRNA** carrying amino acids, **initiation, elongation, termination factors** | Polypeptide chain → folded protein |

---

### 2. Who Named It?

- **Francis Crick** (1926‑2004), co‑discoverer of the DNA double helix (with James Watson) and a Nobel laureate in Physiology or Medicine (1962).
- He introduced the phrase *“central dogma”* in his 1958 Royal Society lecture: “A quantitative theory of the lac operon”. The formal paper, **Crick, F. 1958. *The Central Dogma of Molecular Biology*. *Nature* 222, 564‑567**, became the definitive statement.
- Crick used the term *dogma* to convey the idea that information flow is largely one‑way—but he also acknowledged that there are exceptions (e.g., reverse transcriptase).

---

### 3. Why It Matters

| Reason | Significance |
|--------|--------------|
| **Unifying principle** | Provides a concise framework for molecular biology, genetics, and biochemistry. |
| **Predictive power** | Helps scientists infer what proteins a gene can produce and how mutations might affect protein function. |
| **Evolutionary insight** | Explains the constraints and flexibility in genome evolution and virus replication strategies. |
| **Biotechnological application** | Guides the design of recombinant DNA, gene therapies, mRNA vaccines, synthetic biology, etc. |

---

### 4. Caveats & Extensions

- **Reverse Transcription** – RNA viruses (e.g., HIV) use **reverse transcriptase** to convert RNA back into DNA.  
- **Regulatory RNAs** – non‑coding RNAs (miRNA, siRNA, lncRNA) influence transcription, translation, and epigenetic states.  
- **Alternative Splicing** – one gene can give rise to multiple mRNA variants; thus, the information flow is often more complex than a strict linear chain.  
- **Protein‑Repair/Modification** – post‑translational modifications (phosphorylation, glycosylation) add further regulatory layers.

---

### 5. Quick Take‑away

- **Central Dogma:** DNA copies → DNA → RNA → Protein.  
- **Processes:** Replication (DNA ➜ DNA), Transcription (DNA ➜ RNA), Translation (RNA ➜ Protein).  
- **Named by:** **Francis Crick** in 1958.  

This framework is foundational to any work involving genes, gene expression, and molecular biotechnology.
