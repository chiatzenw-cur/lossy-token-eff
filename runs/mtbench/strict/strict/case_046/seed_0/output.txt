If the satellite’s speed is reduced while it is still on the same circular radius, the new orbit will be an **elliptical** one whose perigee is the present radius.  
From the orbital–energy equations we can see that both the size of the orbit and the period of revolution become **smaller**.

---

### 1.  Orbital energy and new radius

For any bound orbit the specific orbital energy (energy per unit mass) is

\[
\varepsilon = \frac{v^{2}}{2} - \frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r},
\]

and for a Keplerian orbit it can also be written as  

\[
\varepsilon = -\,\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2a},
\]

where \(a\) is the semi‑major axis.  
For the original circular orbit let \(r_0\) be the radius and  

\[
v_{0}= \sqrt{\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r_0}}\; .
\]

Thus the initial specific energy is  

\[
\varepsilon_{0} = \frac{v_0^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r_0}
               = -\,\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2r_0}.
\]

Now the speed is reduced to \(v'<v_0\) while the satellite is still at radius \(r_0\).  
Its new specific energy is

\[
\varepsilon' = \frac{{v'}^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r_0}.
\]

Because \(v' < v_0\), the kinetic term is smaller and

\[
\varepsilon'  <  \varepsilon_0 .
\]

Hence the orbit is now **more tightly bound**; the semi‑major axis implied by  
\(\varepsilon' = -GM_{\oplus}/(2a')\) is

\[
a' = -\,\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2\varepsilon'}  \;<\; r_0 .
\]

Thus the new orbit has a **smaller size** than the original circular orbit.  
At the instant the speed is reduced the satellite is at the **perigee** of that ellipse, which is \(r_{\rm peri}=r_0\); the apogee is larger than the perigee but the semi‑major axis (average distance) is still less than \(r_0\).

---

### 2.  Period of revolution

Kepler’s third law for the period \(T\) of a bound orbit around Earth reads

\[
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{GM_{\oplus}}}\; .
\]

Since \(a' < r_0\), the new period satisfies

\[
T' = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a'^{3}}{GM_{\oplus}}}
    \;<\; 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r_0^{3}}{GM_{\oplus}}}
    = T_0 .
\]

So the satellite’s revolution time **decreases**.

---

### 3.  Physical picture

Think of the satellite on a circular track.  
If its speed is suddenly lowered, the gravitational “pull” toward the Earth is unchanged, but the required centripetal acceleration is smaller.  
Because the real centripetal force is larger, the satellite experiences a net **inward** acceleration: it moves to a lower orbit.  
In a lower Keplerian orbit the mean motion is faster, so the period shortens.

---

### Summary

- **Orbital radius (size)**: **decreases** – the satellite moves to an ellipse whose average distance \(a\) is smaller than the original radius.
- **Period of revolution**: **decreases** – from \(T \propto a^{3/2}\), a smaller \(a\) gives a smaller \(T\).

This conclusion follows directly from the vis‑viva equation, conservation of energy, and Kepler’s third law.
