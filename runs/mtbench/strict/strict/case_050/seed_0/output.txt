#### How these methods interacted

1. **Elenchus + Paradox** – A dialogue would start with a simple question. As the interlocutor supplied a definition, Socrates would insert a paradox and then elenchus to reveal that something could not hold up. The pattern of inquiry rendered the elder’s or the politician’s answers unstable.

2. **Irony + Public Debate** – By pretending he didn’t know, Socrates turned the table in public hearings. A public audience, accustomed to declarative certainty, found it unsettling that the "wise man" could actually be shy and uncertain. The audience had to confront their reliance on surface-level talk.

3. **Confessional Method + Dialectics** – His lifestyle served as a living model for the dialectics he “taught.” The personas swapped from "teacher" to "student" to "practitioner," flipping the societal roles expected of a philosopher into a concrete narrative.  

#### The philosophical impact

These methods harmed the status quo in two chief ways:

* **Knowledge Friction** – They upset the mental models of the Athenian elite.  The “closed” world of a text or a definitive statement was replaced by a world of doubt, counter‑examples, and endless questioning.  The elite could no longer merely cite authority.

* **Moral Introspection** – By constantly testing definitions of virtue, honesty, and justice, Socrates made Athenian society re‑examine its own ethics.  His statement “the unexamined life is not worth living” forces a citizen to either continue consistently or redefine life’s worth.

These same tools also contributed directly to Plato’s education of his own students, the spread of the method across the Hellenistic world, and ultimately the Reformation of Western thought.

In short, *Socratic)* “method” was a practical toolbox that could be applied **in conversation, in public debate, and in lived practice**.
