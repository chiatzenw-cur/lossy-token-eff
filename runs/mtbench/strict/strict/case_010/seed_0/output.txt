In quantum mechanics the state of an isolated system is represented by a vector \(|\Psi\rangle\) in a Hilbert space.  
Because that space is *linear*, any weighted sum of two (or more) allowed states is itself an allowed state. This gives rise to the principle of **superposition**:

\[
| \Psi \rangle = \sum_{i} c_i\, | \phi_i \rangle ,
\]

where \(|\phi_i\rangle\) are basis states (eigenstates of some observable) and the complex numbers \(c_i\) are probability amplitudes.  
Only when a measurement is performed does the state “collapse” to one of the basis states \(|\phi_k\rangle\); the probability of outcome \(k\) is \(|c_k|^2\).

*Classic example* – a spin‑½ particle.  
In the computational (up/down) basis the state

\[
|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\;| \uparrow\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\;| \downarrow\rangle
\]

is a coherent superposition of “spin up” and “spin down”. If we measure spin along the \(z\)-axis, a single measurement will give either \(\uparrow\) or \(\downarrow\), each with 50 % probability, and the post‑measurement state collapses to the corresponding basis vector.

**Entanglement – a special form of multi‑particle superposition**

Entanglement occurs when the composite state of two (or more) subsystems cannot be expressed as a simple product of individual states.  
Mathematically,

\[
|\Psi_{\text{AB}}\rangle \neq |\psi_A\rangle \otimes |\psi_B\rangle .
\]

Instead, the state is a *superposition* of product states that are mutually exclusive:

\[
|\Psi_{\text{AB}}\rangle = \sum_{i,j} c_{ij}\; |i\rangle_A \otimes |j\rangle_B .
\]

The simplest entangled state for two qubits is the Bell (or singlet) state

\[
|\Phi^-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\uparrow\rangle_A|\downarrow\rangle_B-|\downarrow\rangle_A|\uparrow\rangle_B\bigr).
\]

Here each subsystem is in a superposition of “up” and “down”, but the two subsystems are perfectly anti‑correlated. Prior to measurement, neither particle has a definite spin; the overall state is a *coherent superposition* of two distinct product states. When one particle is measured, the outcome instantaneously fixes the state of the far‑away partner – the hallmark of entanglement.

---

### How superposition → entanglement

1. **Superposition is the foundational principle:** any linear combination of allowed states is valid.
2. **Entanglement is a subclass of superpositions** for composite systems where the coefficients cannot be factorized into separate amplitudes for each subsystem.
3. **Necessity but not sufficiency**: superposition alone does not guarantee entanglement. A product state \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\otimes |0\rangle\) is a superposition but not entangled. Only when the many‑body superposition contains correlations that couple the subsystems do we get entanglement.

### Physical consequences

| Feature | Superposition | Entanglement |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| **Definition** | Linear combination of basis states of *one* system | Linear combination of *product* states of *multiple* systems |
| **Reduced density matrix** | Pure state (unless mixed by decoherence) | Each subsystem’s reduced density matrix is *mixed* (e.g., maximally mixed for Bell state). |
| **Correlations** | Only within a single system | Correlations across spatially separated subsystems; violate classical Bell inequalities |
| **Use in quantum tech** | Enables qubits, quantum interference, superdense coding | Key resource for teleportation, quantum cryptography, fault‑tolerant quantum computing |
| **Experimental signatures** | Interference fringes (double‑slit, Ramsey, etc.) | Bell‑test violations, violation of CHSH inequality, entanglement witnesses |

### Take‑home summary

* **Superposition** is the statement that any linear combination of allowed quantum states is again a valid state.  
* **Entanglement** is the remarkable situation where a composite system’s state is a superposition of product states that cannot be factorized into separate parts—leading to correlations that defy classical explanation.  
* Entanglement *uses* the superposition principle; it is an emergent phenomenon that appears only when that superposition straddles several subsystems in a way that preserves coherence across them.  

Both superposition and entanglement sit at the heart of quantum mechanics, and together they unlock the power of quantum information science.
