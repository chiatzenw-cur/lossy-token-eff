Life is a sequence of rooms we walk through, each room polished by a different set of rules about “when” and “what.”  As we move from one room to the next—infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, old age—our internal clock and our death‑ledger are rewritten.  Below is a loose map of how those stages reshape the two most universal human concerns: *time* and *mortality*.

---

## 1. Infancy – The “eternal now”

| What the infant experiences | How this shapes time | How this shapes mortality |
|---|---|---|
| **Only the present exists.**  No future awareness; the world is a series of sights, sounds, and touch. | *Shallow temporal horizon:* experiences are serialized, each moment a packet of immediate relevance. Time feels immense—moments stretch. | *No concept of death.*  Babies cannot yet label “die.”  Mortality is an invisible backdrop, not a conversation. |

### Takeaway
The infant’s cognitive architecture has no future or past symbols, so “”later“ or “days“ are abstract.  Their perception is “now‑or‑never.”  The feeling that “time has no edges” is inherent to childhood until the brain patches up an executive‑function map.

## 2. Childhood – First temporal maps are drawn

| What the child learns | How this reshapes time | How this reshapes mortality |
|---|---|---|
| **Days, weeks, seasons.**  School calendars, holidays, birthday parties. | *Past–future loop appears.*  Children start seeing a “sequence”—one day leads to the next.  They learn that actions can be planned. | *Death is a concept:* “my grandmother died.”  The word “dead” appears in vocabulary; agency and inevitability become contested. |
| **Relative duration has meaning.**  “A long winter” feels different than “a long summer.” | *Point‑to‑point* suffices; events get judged by their relative sequence, not by how long the world has been spinning. | *Mortality feels distant but present.*  It is weird—puzzles life’s sudden endings, leading to the first existential mini‑crisis. |

### Takeaway
The child’s mental model begins to incorporate a linear script.  Time is not a unit of calculus but a *narrative* with cause and effect.  Mortality enters the conversation, but because it lies *outside* the now, it is mostly a label—“it’s bad that it happened.”

### Takeaway
Adolescence transforms *time* from subjective outlines into a spreadsheet of future opportunities.  *Mortality* becomes a deadline rather than a black wall.  This is the period where many people develop a philosophy: “Time is limited; spend it wisely.”

## Cross‑cutting Themes

1. **Proportion vs. quantity**  
   - *Children:* 1/2 of life is “immediate.”  1 year is large.

   - *Elders:* 1/50 or 1/100 harvests their entire existence.

2. **Time Perception vs. Time Reality**  
   - Hormonal shifts, dopamine levels, and memory consolidation changes brain’s sense of length.

3. **Mortality as a “Time Driver”**  
   - The fear of missing out accelerates learning.  
   - The awareness of death intensifies meaning–seeking.  

4. **Culture‑Shaped Stage Narratives**  
   - **Western**: “Future focus” narrative, maturing in a 4‑stage life system (young, mid, late, old).  
   - **Eastern**: Stageborne order conveyed in Buddhist “vipassana” or Chinese “xìng” (character) development.  
   - **Anti‑linear cultures**: Indigenous chronologies—nature cycles, generational responsibilities—emphasise a circular view.

5. **The “Present” as the fulcrum**  
   - **Infants:** the only maintainable point.  
   - **Elders:** consciousness of the present lets them accept the death that is constant.
