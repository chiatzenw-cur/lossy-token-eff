Probability is the math of **“what’s the chance it’ll happen?”**  
Think of it as a way to turn your curiosity about a random event into a number.

---

## 1. The Basic Idea

- **Probability (P)** = **Favorable outcomes ÷ Total possible outcomes**  
  \( P = \frac{\text{# ways it can happen}}{\text{# possible things that could happen}} \)

- It’s always a **number between 0 and 1**  
  - 0 = impossible  
  - 1 = guaranteed  
  - 0.5 = 50‑50 (even odds)

---

## 2. Step‑by‑Step Example: Flipping a Coin

| Step | What to do | Result |
|------|------------|--------|
| 1 | Roll a fair coin (two sides) | 2 possible outcomes |
| 2 | Count favorable outcomes for “heads” | 1 head |
| 3 | Compute probability | \(P(\text{Heads}) = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5\) |

**Interpretation:** If you flip that coin a lot, about half of the flips will land heads.

---

## 3. Everyday-Life Example: Picking a Fruit

Imagine a bowl with 3 apples, 2 bananas, and 5 cherries (total 10 fruits).

- What’s the probability of picking a cherry?

  \[
  P(\text{Cherry}) = \frac{5}{10} = 0.5
  \]

- What’s the probability of picking a **fruit that’s not an apple**?

  \[
  P(\text{Not Apple}) = \frac{2+5}{10} = \frac{7}{10} = 0.7
  \]

---

## 4. Common Pitfalls

| Mistake | What it actually means |
|---------|------------------------|
| Thinking “more chances” = “certain” | Having more favorable outcomes doesn’t guarantee it unless you have all the outcomes (probability = 1). |
| Ignoring “total outcomes” | Even if you know favorable outcomes, you must know the size of the whole universe of outcomes. |
| Assuming events are independent when they’re not | Two events can affect each other (e.g., drawing two cards without replacement). |

---

## 5. Quick “Rule of Thumb” for Common Events

| Event | Probability |
|-------|-------------|
| Rolling a **6** on a fair die | \( \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.17 \) |
| Drawing an **ace** from a standard deck (52 cards) | \( \frac{4}{52} \approx 0.077 \) |
| Tossing a coin and getting **heads** twice in a row | \( \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4} \) |

---

## 6. “What if” Deep Dive: Drawing Cards

Suppose you pick **two cards** from a standard deck **without replacement**.

- **Probability of both being hearts**  
  1st heart: \( \frac{13}{52} = \frac{1}{4} \)  
  2nd heart: \( \frac{12}{51} \) (only 12 hearts left, 51 cards left)  
  Multiply: \( \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{12}{51} = \frac{12}{204} = \frac{1}{17} \approx 0.0588 \)

Notice the change after the first draw – that’s what makes *dependent* events tricky.

---

## 7. Probability “Space” – A Visual Trick

A useful way to see probabilities is to think of a **pie chart** or a **bar graph**:

- **0.8** → 80% of the pie is the favorable area  
- **0.2** → 20% is the rest

You can even draw a quick sketched diagram for each situation – it helps the brain “see” the math.

## 8. Quick Practice Problems

1. **Dice**: What’s the probability of rolling a number greater than 4?  
   *Answer:* \( \frac{5}{6} \) (since 5, 6, etc.)

2. **Marbles**: A bag has 4 red, 6 blue, 10 green marbles. Chance of drawing a blue or green?  
   *Answer:* \( \frac{6+10}{4+6+10} = \frac{16}{20} = 0.8 \)

3. **Two events**: It rains (0.3 chance) and the coffee shop is open (0.7 chance). If these are independent, chance both true?  
   *Answer:* \( 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21 \)
