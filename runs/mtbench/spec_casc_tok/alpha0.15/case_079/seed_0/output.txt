## 1.  First Impressions
| What to Do | Why It Matters | Quick Tips |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| **Punctuality** | Being on time (or even 5–10 min early) signals respect for everyone’s schedule. | Aim to arrive 10 min early. If you’re late, call/ email ASAP. |
| **Dress Code** | Business attire is still formal in most sectors. Dark suit, tie, and polished shoes for men; conservative suit or blazer for women. | Keep colours neutral, avoid overly bright patterns. |
| **Handshakes + Bow** | A brief, firm handshake is acceptable, but a bow is the true Japanese greeting. | A shy bow (around 15°) is enough for most business intro‑meetings. |
| **Use of Names** | Japanese names are written family‑name first. Address by family name + title (e.g., “Tanaka‑san”). | Never call a colleague “Mr./Ms.” for the first time; add the surname or “‑san” only after asking. |

## 2.  Business Cards (Meishi)
| What to Do | Why It Matters | Quick Tips |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| **Presentation** | Meishi are treated as a mark of respect. | Place the card on a small tray or padded holder. |
| **Exchange** | Hand the card with both hands, offer a slight bow. | Do not accept a card with one hand unless you’re in a very informal setting. |
| **Viewing** | Look at the card carefully before putting it away. | Never write on the card during the exchange; if you need to note something, do it later. |
| **Handling** | Place the card at eye level when you receive it; don’t pile it on the table. | Keep a reusable card holder on your desk. |
| **Quantity** | Couples of dozen cards are standard. | Have more than you think you’ll need—extra cards are appreciated. |

## 3.  Meetings & Communication
| What to Do | Why It Matters | Quick Tips |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| **Agenda** | Meetings start on time and usually stick to a set agenda. | Email the agenda beforehand in a concise format. |
| **Hierarchical Respect** | Junior staff may defer to senior staff; decisions are often made by the top. | Let senior staff speak first; avoid interrupting. |
| **Tone** | Use polite, formal language (keigo). Avoid slang or direct blame. | In written communication, end with “敬具” (Keigu) or “失礼します” (shitsurei shimasu). |
| **Body Language** | Avoid loud voices, hand gestures, or aggressive pointing. | Sit upright, keep hands visible on the table. |
| **Decision Making** | Arguably “Two‑heads are better” – group consensus is valued. | Pane about how you’ll finalize decisions; be patient. |
| **After‑meeting Follow‑up** | Send a thank‑you email and a summary of action items. | Mail within 24 hrs; email subject should be clear: “Thanks for today’s meeting – XYZ”. |

## 4.  Dining Etiquette
| What to Do | Why It Matters | Quick Tips |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| **Start/End** | Host usually starts the meal. Finish each course after everyone has finished. | Do not signal with a "to go" or sign off when you’re done. |
| **Sake** | Raise your glass to the host, then sip. | When you’re a guest, when you’re offered a glass, watch the host first. |
| **Utensils** | Use chopsticks properly; don’t point with them. | Don’t leave chopsticks sticking upright in a bowl of rice. |
| **Paper** | Don’t throw napkins on the table. | Keep napkins flat on or beside your plate. |
| **Conversations** | Avoid serious topics (politics, religion). | Praise partners, talk about business, culture, weather. |
| **Cool Down** | Keep your card light – the host will take care of the bill. | At the end, the host will offer to pay; politely decline if you insist. |

| What to Do | Why It Matters | Quick Tips |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| **Etiquette** | Small, high‑quality gifts are appreciated. | Avoid items that include alcohol if you’re unsure. |
| **Wrapping** | Wrap neatly in gift paper or wayaka (traditional paper). | Use a color pallet praised for business (e.g., green, blue). |
| **Presentation** | Offer the gift with both hands, slightly bow. | Don’t open the gift yourself; let the recipient open it. |
| **Timing** | Ideal to give as a thank‑you during a folding of a business card or at the end of a meeting. | In a multi‑party dinner, gifts are generally shared, not personal items. |
| **Typical Items** | Local specialty foods, high‑quality stationery, lacquerware, or a small art piece. | Avoid ‘souvenirs’ that only show off your country. |

|------------|----------------|------------|
| **First Contact** | Greet the senior person first. | Don’t engage the junior staff directly if a senior is present. |

| **Cultural Terms** | “Honne” (true feelings) vs. “Tatemae” (public façade). | Keep your statements polite and assume the public façade. |
| **Networking** | Trust-building is slow. | Small gestures (sharing a drink, sending a thank‑you note) help. |
