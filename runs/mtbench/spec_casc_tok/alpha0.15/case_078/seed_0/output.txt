In simpler terms, it means that instead of using the prior probability that a property or outcome exists in the general population, people act as if the probability is determined mainly by the specific evidence at hand.

*Why it matters in politics:* Politicians can exploit this bias by presenting isolated data, anecdotes, or targeted figures that look impressive in isolation, while obscuring the fact that the same data is highly unrepresentative of the whole population. The result is a distorted perception of a policy’s effectiveness, a candidate’s electability, or an issue’s importance.

### How to guard against the base‑rate fallacy in political discourse

1. **Ask for the denominator** – How many people or cases does the data represent?  
2. **Seek independent verification** – Look for studies that include the full population or multiple samples.  
3. **Look for “out‑of‑the‑box” numbers** – Are there other studies with different, maybe contradictory, results?  
4. **Beware of victory‑by‑story** – Emotional anecdotes don’t equal statistical evidence.  
5. **Note the source** – Is the data owned, selected, or filtered by those who benefit from the outcome?

By staying aware of how base‑rate neglect can distort the political narrative, voters can make more informed choices and politicians can be held accountable for presenting their data in full context.
