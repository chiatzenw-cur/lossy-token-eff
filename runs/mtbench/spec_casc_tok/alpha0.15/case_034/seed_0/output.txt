> **Red‑flag signs** that warrant immediate emergency care:  
> • Sudden, severe pain that worsens in the supine position  
> • Persistent vomiting (especially if bile‑colored)  
> • Fever >38.5 °C  
> • Signs of shock (tachycardia, hypotension, cold clammy skin)  
> • Jaundice or dark urine  
> • Peritonitis (rigidity, rebound tenderness)  
> • Melena or hematemesis  
> • Pregnancy‑related signs (amenorrhea, vaginal bleeding)

**Step 3 – Clinical examination priorities**

1. **Vital signs** – heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, oxygen saturation.  
2. **General appearance** – distress, pallor, diaphoresis.  
3. **Abdominal inspection** – distension, scars, scars, abdominal wall wall.  
4. **Palpation** – tenderness, guarding, rebound, organomegaly, masses.  
5. **Percussion** – tympany vs. dullness.  
6. **Murmer** – bowel sounds.  
7. **Special tests** – Rovsing’s sign (appendicitis), Murphy’s sign (gallbladder), McBurney’s point tenderness.  
8. **Rectal exam** – if indicated, to assess for blood or masses.  
9. **Pelvic exam** – if female and abdominal pain’s location is unclear.  

---

**Step 4 – Baseline investigations**

| Test | Why it’s useful | Typical findings |
|------|-----------------|------------------|
| CBC | Leukocytosis, anemia | ↑WBC, eosinophilia, anemia |
| CMP | Electrolytes, liver enzymes | ↑ALT/AST, bilirubin, renal function |
| LFTs | Check for gallbladder disease | ↑AST/ALT, GGT, bilirubin |
| Lipase/Amylase | Pancreatitis | ↑>3× ULN |
| Urinalysis | Pyelonephritis, stones | Protein, hematuria, bacteriuria |
| Pregnancy test (β‑hCG) | In women of childbearing age | Positive/negative |
| CRP/ESR | Inflammation | ↑CRP/ESR |
| Imaging | Depending on suspicion | Ultrasound, CT, MRI, X‑ray |

*Ultrasound* is first‑line for RUQ pain (gallstones) and for obstetric/gynecologic causes.  
*CT abdomen/pelvis* is the gold standard for suspected appendicitis, diverticulitis, perforation, or pancreatitis.  
*X‑ray* may be useful for bowel obstruction or perforation.

**Step 6 – When to seek urgent care**

If any of the following red‑flag signs present, the patient should *immediately* seek emergency medical attention (ambulance if necessary):

- Severe, sudden abdominal pain that worsens on lying flat or with movement.  
- Fever (≥38.5 °C) + vomiting.  
- Hematemesis or melena.  
- Evidence of shock (tachycardia >120 bpm, hypotension <90 mmHg systolic, pale, clammy skin).  
- Persistent vomiting for >24 h.  
- Severe abdominal rigidity or peritonitis.  
- Vaginal bleeding in a woman of reproductive age.  
- Suspected pregnancy (amenorrhea, abdominal pain).

### Bottom line

1. **Collect thorough demographic, symptom, and risk‑factor data.**  
2. **Perform a focused physical exam** and identify red‑flag signs.  
3. **Order baseline labs and imaging** based on the most likely differentials.  
4. **Initiate definitive care** (surgery, antibiotics, acid suppression) once an etiology is identified.  
5. **Provide targeted adjunctive therapy** with herbal, natural, or lifestyle approaches that complement primary treatment and are safe for the patient’s specific condition.  
6. **Re‑evaluate** regularly and adjust the plan as new data emerge.

> **Final reminder** – This information is educational and not a substitute for real‑time clinical judgment. If you’re the patient or a caregiver, please consult a qualified healthcare professional or go to the nearest emergency department if abdominal pain is severe or accompanied by any red‑flag symptoms.
