### Criteria I Used to Distinguish Them

1. **Sign of ΔH (Standard Enthalpy Change)** — The most direct thermodynamic criterion.  
   *If ΔH < 0 → exothermic; if ΔH > 0 → endothermic.*

2. **Direction of Heat Flow in a Calorimeter**  
   *Positive heat flow (calorimeter registers heat coming out) → exothermic; negative heat flow (heat is taken in) → endothermic.*

3. **Observable Temperature Change**  
   *An increase in temperature of the reaction vessel (or of the surrounding laboratory air) indicates an exothermic process; a decrease indicates an endothermic process.*

4. **Energy Profile Over the Reaction Coordinate**  
   *A downward slope (products lower in energy) → exothermic; an upward slope (products higher in energy) → endothermic.*

These criteria are mutually consistent: the sign of ΔH dictates the direction of heat flow, which in turn causes the temperature change you observe.

### Real‑World Example

**Endothermic: Dissolution of Ammonium Nitrate in Water**

- **Reaction**:  
  \[
  \text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3 (s) \;\xrightarrow{\text{in water}}\; \text{NH}_4^+(aq) + \text{NO}_3^-(aq)
  \]
- **Why it’s endothermic**:  
  The enthalpy of solution is +ΔH ≈ +26 kJ mol⁻¹. The process *absorbs* heat from the surrounding water.  
- **Practical effect**:  
  When you add NH₄NO₃ to a beaker of water, the solution feels cold. The temperature of the water drops by several degrees, exactly as predicted by the positive ΔH.

**Exothermic: Combustion of Light Bulb Incandescent Filament**

- **Reaction**:  
  \[
  \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 (s) + 1.5\,\text{O}_2(g) \;\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}\; \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(g) + \text{N}_2(g) + \text{heat}
  \]
- **Why it’s exothermic**:  
  The combustion of the carbon‑rich filament has ΔH ≈ –360 kJ mol⁻¹. It *releases* heat to the surroundings.  
- **Practical effect**:  
  The filament thrums warm; the bulb’s envelope and the surrounding air rise in temperature. The temperature rise can be visually observed by the glow of the filament.

---

### Bottom Line

- **Exothermic** reactions *release* heat; measured by a negative ΔH, a rise in temperature, and a positive heat flow in a calorimeter.  
- **Endothermic** reactions *absorb* heat; measured by a positive ΔH, a drop in temperature, and a negative heat flow in a calorimeter.  

These distinctions are core to physical chemistry, help engineers design reactors, and allow everyday phenomena (like baking soda & vinegar or ice melting) to be understood in a thermodynamic framework.
