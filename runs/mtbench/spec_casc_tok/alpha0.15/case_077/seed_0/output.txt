| Element | What it is | Why it matters for competition |

| **Legal foundation** | Statutes (e.g., Sherman Act, Clayton Act) and case law | Provides the rules producers must follow and the legal tools for the government to enforce them. |

*Impact on competition:*  Antitrust law reduces barriers to entry, limits the ability of dominant firms to impose fair‑price or “single‑price” policies, and encourages innovation through experimentation and risk‑taking.  When enforcement is weak, markets can become “so‑called” oligopolies where firms collude (explicitly or tacitly) and mimic each other’s prices, leading to higher prices and inferior products or services.  Strong enforcement restores the “price‑as‑a‑signal” function and preserves the “market‑pull” that drives firms to innovate.

## 1. United States antitrust framework

| Key statute | Main provisions | Typical enforcement tools |
|-------------|-----------------|---------------------------|
| **Sherman Act (1890)** | §1 prohibits agreements that restrain trade; §2 prohibits “monopolization” or attempts to monopolize. | Civil suits, criminal prosecutions (felony), injunctions. |
| **Clayton Act (1914)** | Section 2 bans price discrimination; Section 3 forbids exclusive dealing; Section 7 regulates mergers that might substantially lessen competition. | FTC or DOJ investigations, merger petitions, civil penalties. |
| **Federal Trade Commission Act (1914)** | Creates the FTC and gives it power to investigate and enforce “unfair competition.” | Investigations, cease‑and‑desist orders, civil litigation. |
| **Hart‑Scott‑Rodino Act (1976)** | Pre‑merger notification and review by FTC/DOJ. | Administrative orders, binaries, public hearing. |

**Key characteristics**

* **Consumer‑welfare focus** – The US courts traditionally apply the “rule‑of‑reason” test, weighing the pro‑competitive benefits against harms.  
* **Enforcement in both courts and agencies** – The DOJ and FTC can bring civil suits; the federal courts decide whether a violation occurred.  
* **Mergers focused** – Over the last decade, many high‑profile challenges have come from merger reviews (e.g., AT&T/WorldCom, Verizon/AT&T, Google/DeepMind).  
* **Strong private enforcement** – Private parties can sue for damages (negligent or collusive conduct).

## 2. China antitrust framework

| Law / Agency | Main provisions | Enforcement mechanism |
|--------------|-----------------|-----------------------|
| **Anti‑Monopoly Law (2008)** | 1) Prohibits monopolistic practices (e.g., abuse of dominance, collusion). 2) Requires “prohibited conduct” by dominant firms. 3) Merger review requirement. | **State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR)**  – administrative review, fines, restructuring; **People’s Courts** – criminal and civil litigation. |
| **Guidelines on Antitrust Administration (2020)** | Extension of law to digital economy, data monopolies, “platform” markets. | Administrative orders, “seven‑step” review for mergers. |
| **Special campaign “Internet+”** | Targeted crackdown on big‑tech data monopolies (Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, Xiaomi, Pinduoduo). | Public‑interest litigation, “shadow” investigations, regulatory “fact‑finding.” |

**Key characteristics**

* **State‑oriented enforcement** – The government acts as the sole regulator; court decisions are heavily guided by policy goals.  
* **Broad scope** – In 2018, the law was amended to cover “unstable” sectors; the 2020 guidelines address “digital‑platform” monopolies.  
* **Heavy penalties** – Class‑action fines can reach tens of billions of yuan (e.g., Alibaba’s 127.5 bn fine for data monopolistic behavior).  
* **Administrative review** – Mergers are screened by local and national regulators; the SAMR can impose “penalties with or without revocation.”

## 4. Case studies

### 4.1 United States

| Case | Year | Core issue | Outcome |
|------|------|------------|---------|
| **United States v. Microsoft Corp.** | 2001 | Microsoft’s licensing and integration practices in Windows. | Damaged monopoly behavior; Microsoft had to provide APIs to competitors. |

| **United States v. Google, Inc.** | 2016 | Alleged abuse of dominance in search and advertising. | FTC sued; Google agreed to a $2.5 bn settlement and data‑sharing commitments. |
| **Apple vs. Justice Department** | 2021 | Apple’s App Store closed‑door commissions and refusal to allow “self‑checking” apps. | Settlement provides Apple with a 15 % discount to 5 % to gender‑inged “big‑tech” for app fees. |

**Impact** – These cases demonstrate that the US litigates on the “consumer‑welfare” but also on the “restriction of competition” front.  Gains include standard setting for technology and a spill‑over that applies to outsourcing and software.  Compliance costs 💰 corporate investments, but the overall effect is a more level playing field.

## 6. Implications for global competition

* **Cross‑border M&A** – US firms must consider not only the Hart‑Scott‑Rodino review but also the Chinese SAMR merger review if the target or joint venture has a presence in China.  
* **Platform ecosystems** – In the US, the FTC has begun to focus on data monopoly and “platform bundling.”  In China, the guidelines intensify scrutiny on “platform enterprises” that gain “unfair advantage” through cross‑border data flows.  
* **Strategic alliances** – Chinese firms may prefer “liberalization” by other countries after being fined, altering the global network of suppliers.  
* **Innovation dynamics** – A rigorous US antitrust regime can spawn “creative rivalry” by encouraging smaller firms to innovate.  China’s strict enforcement may cool incremental innovation but encourage large‑scale “smart” reforms (e.g., cloud infrastructure competitiveness).

## 7. Take‑away for firms and policymakers

1. **Know the controlling law** – Before expanding domestically or overseas, companies should consult the anti‑monopoly law of the target jurisdiction.  
2. **Monitor data‑monopoly signals** – Both US and China are pre‑empting data‑centric monopolies.  Policies that restrict data gate‑keeping or enforce data portability are likely to be enforced.  
3. **Prepare for “consumer‑welfare” and “social‑welfare” arguments** – US cases will be strictly consumer‑centric; Chinese cases may frame abuses as bargaining the “common public interest.”  
4. **Leverage cross‑border cooperation** – Many firms will cooperate with local regulators to align product design with both US and Chinese antitrust expectations (e.g., separate data centers in China).  
5. **Reassess vertical integration** – As both regimes increasingly scrutinize “data‑driven” vertical integration, firms should perform systematic risk assessments of new bundling strategies.
