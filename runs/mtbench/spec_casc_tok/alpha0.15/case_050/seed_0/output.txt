### How These Methods Worked Together

1. **Questioning Opens the Door**  
   Socrates began any discussion with a broad, open question—“What does it mean to be just?”—the point being to get the interlocutor to define it in their own words. Most simply answered “justice is giving each what they deserve,” which awaited further scrutiny.

2. **Elenchus Exposes Gaps**  
   He then asked a series of follow‑up questions that revealed the gap: “But if you give each what they deserve, does that also mean that people get what they are best at?” The interlocutor’s answer either conflicted with itself or made the same problematic assumption surface again.

3. **Irony Lures the Claim Acknowledgment**  
   By pretending to be ignorant, Socrates let the other person sell him the premises of their argument. Once the premises were laid on the table, it was easier to show where they faltered.

4. **Maieutic “Birth” of Clarity**  
   By asking for a definition of “the good,” Socrates forced the interlocutor to articulate a concept that they had never previously considered. The lack of a clear definition made the experiences of the arguments more transparent.

5. **Aporetic Endings**  
   The process often ended with “I don’t know,” signaling either a new, more honest state of ignorance or the beginning of a deeper investigation. This was not a defeat but a strategic pause, pushing the conversation further.

6. **Shaping Public Ethics**  
   When Socrates questioned the athletes or the song‑teachers, he was not attacking the physical virtues they claimed to value; he was challenging the ethical framework supporting those claims. The result was a public environment where the people had to confront whether *virtue* had any real, calculable connection to success or honor.

### What “Prevailing Thoughts” Did He Target?

| Category | Prevailing Thought | Socratic Challenge |
|----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Political/ Social** | Civic virtue was a matter of public speaking, participation, and reputation. | By questioning the nature of *justice*, he forced Athenian politicians to think beyond mere representation. |
| **Ethical** | Virtue was a matter of knowledge or simply "doing the right thing." | Socrates argued that *virtue is knowledge* (Plato’s *Meno*), drastically reframing the debate. |
| **Metaphysical** | The world was a simple, physical reality; the soul was a concern for the afterlife. | In his dialogues, he suggested that the soul is a *rational* being capable of *knowledge*, far beyond the pure physicality of the body. |
| **Rhetorical** | Sophists could use clever arguments to “win” without caring about truth. | Socrates’ elenchus demonstrated that the Sophists’ arguments were self‑contradictory, exposing the futility of rhetorical skill alone. |
| **Cultural Norms** | You could learn *how* to live well by learning *when* to play, dance, or fight. | He suggested that you must *know* the *why* behind those actions—leading to a more profound reflective life. |

### Why It Made a Difference

Socrates’ approach was revolutionary because it made **ignorance** a respectable, even desirable, position. By openly admitting that he didn’t know, he defied the hubris of the sophists and elected to pursue *truth* by continually questioning. This insistence on cross‑examination and the turning of sweeping cultural assumptions into delicate puzzles became the basis for the entire Western philosophical tradition.

In short, Socrates challenged the prevailing thoughts of his time by:

1. **Turning ordinary claims into a series of pointed questions** that highlighted contradictions.  
2. **Using irony and the “pretend‑ignorance” tactic** to draw out the wrong assumptions of others.  
3. **Application of the maieutic method** to coax latent ideas into the open.  
4. **Ending with aporia** to force a step back from false certainty.  
5. **Addressing the everyday life of Athenians**—their politics, rhetoric, and self‑esteem—so that the examination was not just abstract but lived.  

These combined tactics made Socrates a relentless challenger of the unexamined life, forever altering how we assess knowledge, morality, and civic responsibility.
