### 1. Childhood: The Infinite Horizon  

**Neural underpinnings**  
- The prefrontal cortex, the brain’s time‑processing hub, is still maturing.  
- Children’s brains tick with a “present‑bias” and a lack of future‑planning circuitry.  

**What it feels like**  
- **Time is a toy**: “Yesterday, today, tomorrow” are just abstract markers.  
- **Mortality is a story**: Death is a distant, occasional plot point (“the ghost in the haunted house”).  

**Consequences**  
- An expansive sense of possibility—“I could be a superhero, a dragon, an astronaut.”  
- A comfort with the unknown, since the stakes are low.  

---

### 2. Adolescence: The Clock Starts to Ticking  

**Cognitive shift**  
- Executive functions (planning, impulse control) sharpen.  
- The brain’s “future‑processing” network begins to outpace the “past‑processing” one.  

**Psychological experience**  
- **Time becomes a resource**: “I have to finish my homework, but I also want to hang out.”  
- **Mortality is suddenly real**: The news of accidents, the “killing” of a fictional character, or the final school career exam all foreground the fragility of life.  

**Societal pressure**  
- Timelines (high school, college, first job) become visually charted.  
- Peer pressure creates a “deadline” mindset.  

**Impact**  
- A heightened sense of urgency.  
- The first “fear of missing out” (FOMO) that brands and social media feed.  

---

### 3. Early Adulthood: The “Split‑Second” Life  

**Neural adult pattern**  
- The prefrontal cortex fully matures; we can reliably forecast future outcomes.  

**Typical life markers**  
- **Career, relationships, reproducing**: ticking clocks force decisions.  
- **Mortality becomes a decision‑maker**: “Will I invest in life insurance? Will I buy a house?”  

**Psychological elasticity**  
- “Time is thin” – a celebrated phrase in productivity circles.  
- Yet, the ability to “back‑shift” future plans (get a promotion later) is in play.  

**Cultural touchstones**  
- Retirement age, “baby boom” ideal of spending 50 years in a job.  
- The “platinum” of life insurance and planning.  

---

### 4. Middle Age: The “Death of the Young”  

**Cognitive shift**  
- Time‑perspective research shows a shift toward “prospective” planning.  
- The “sunk‑cost” bias: older adults often feel they have “already” invested so much that they keep going.  

**Mortality salience**  
- **Terror Management Theory (TMT)**: When aware of mortality, people cling to cultural worldviews and personal achievements.  
- Health check‑ups become routine.  

**Psychological experience**  
- **Time is a racing car**: “I’m at 50, so I have to live it now.”  
- **Mortality is a familiar guest**: “I’ve seen my friend’s funeral, I’ve seen my own doctor’s visit.”  

**Consequences**  
- **Re‑evaluation**: Many reevaluate career, family, and personal goals.  
- **Identity shift**: The “mid‑life crisis” myth is a visible manifestation of mortality’s tug.  

---

### 5. Late Adulthood: The “Final Lap”  

**Neural changes**  
- Decreased processing speed but still intelligent; episodic memory hits decline.  
- The “time‑compression” illusion: Thinking about the past feels like a lifetime, time in the present feels like a carousel.  

**Psychological experience**  
- **Time slows down**: “I spend less time pending deadlines.”  
- **Mortality is an intimate conversation**: “I’ll see my own death.”  

**Living with mortality**  
- **Acceptance**: Many report an increased sense of peace (the “peace of mind” literature).  
- **Legacy frames**: Focus on storytelling, mentoring, or writing.  

**Cultural notices**  
- **Death in near proximity**: Death care arrangements, wills, or “life review” therapy.

## A Synthesis: What Life Stages Do to Us  

1. **From “I have forever” to “I have a deadline”**  
   - The reduction of the “future buffer” over time translates into a more pressure‑laden worldview.  

2. **From “I’m safe” to “I’m in the presence of the body”**  
   - Mortality moves from a distant concept to a “mortality salience” that shapes decisions.  

3. **From “I can be anything” to “I can leave behind something”**  
   - The desire to create a legacy increases, and how we conceptualize time shifts from a stock to a flow.  

4. **From “I have moments” to “I have moments I cherish”**  
   - The emotional weight of each interval intensifies.
