## 2. Broad Differential (Intense Abdominal Pain)

| Category | Typical Examples | Key Features |
|----------|------------------|--------------|
| **Acute abdominal catastrophes** | Appendicitis, perforated ulcer, aortic aneurysm, bowel obstruction, pancreatitis, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion, acute cholecystitis | Severe, often localized, may increase with movement or coughing, often accompanied by fever, vomiting, and systemic signs. |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Peptic ulcer disease, gastritis, IBS flare, biliary colic (gallstones), pancreatitis, gastroenteritis, inflammatory bowel disease flare | Pain may be crampy or colicky, sometimes relieved by bowel movement, associated with changes in stool or bowel habits. |
| **Gynecologic** | Ovarian cyst rupture, ectopic pregnancy, uterine fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease | Usually in women of reproductive age; pain may be associated with menstruation or vaginal bleeding. |
| **Metabolic / endocrine** | Hyperthyroidism, diabetic ketoacidosis, pancreatitis secondary to gallstones | Often accompanied by systemic signs (tachycardia, weight loss, rash). |
| **Other** | Urolithiasis, constipation, functional pain, stress‑related TMJ, referred pain from shoulder/arm | Look for related urinary or musculoskeletal symptoms. |

> **Red‑flag signs that warrant immediate medical attention:**  
> • Fever > 38.5 °C (101 °F)  
> • Persistent vomiting (especially blood or bile)  
> • Pain worsening or not improving after several hours  
> • Signs of dehydration (dark urine, dizziness)  
> • Severe abdominal distension or rigidity  
> • History of blood clotting disorders, recent surgery, or pregnancy

## 3. Traditional (All‑Press) Medical Options

| Symptom/Condition | Typical First‑Line Medicinal Therapy | Key Considerations |
|-------------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------|
| **Acute pain** | NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen 400–800 mg every 6–8 h); acetaminophen for mild pain | Avoid NSAIDs if ulcers or renal disease; monitor for GI upset. |
| **Acid‑related pain** | Proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole 20 mg daily) or H₂ blocker | Assess for gastritis or peptic ulcer; monitor for long‑term side‑effects. |
| **Tussive pain** | Antispasmodics (e.g., hyoscyamine, dicyclomine) | Caution in glaucoma, urinary retention. |
| **Infection** | Antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin‑clavulanate for presumed bacterial enteritis) | Use culture‑guided therapy if possible. |
| **Inflammatory bowel disease** | 5‑ASA (mesalamine), corticosteroids, immunomodulators | Requires specialist referral. |

> **Always** confirm the diagnosis before prescribing antibiotics or anti‑inflammatories.

## 6. When to Seek Immediate Medical Care

- **Sudden, severe, “worst ever” pain** especially after a fall or with any trauma.  
- **Bloody or coffee‑ground vomit** (possible ulcer perforation).  
- **Pain that spreads to the back or shoulder** (possible pancreatitis or gallbladder disease).  
- **Signs of dehydration, jaundice, dark urine, or confusion** (possible systemic illness).  
- **Any suspicion of pregnancy in women of childbearing age** (risk of ectopic pregnancy).  

In these situations, go to the nearest emergency department or call emergency services.

## 8. A Gentle “First‑Line” Approach (If Red‑Flag Symptoms are Absent)

1. **Take a 5‑minute rest** on a flat surface; don't lie on your stomach.  
2. **Apply gentle heat** (warm compress) to the abdomen.  
3. **Sip a small amount of warm water** or chamomile tea.  
4. **Take 400 mg ibuprofen** (if you’re not allergic, not on anticoagulants, and no ulcers).  
5. **If you feel nauseated, try a 200‑mg dose of ginger** (chewed or tea).  
6. **Monitor** for 30–60 min: if pain improves, stay hydrated and follow the diet plan.  
7. **If pain persists or worsens,** seek medical evaluation.
