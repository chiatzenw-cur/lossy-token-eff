The **base‑rate fallacy** (also called the *base‑rate neglect* or *base‑rate bias*) is a cognitive error that occurs when people give too much weight to specific information (the “case” or “signal”) and ignore the general, population‑level information (the “base rate”). In probability terms, they treat a rare event as if it were typical because they focus on a particular example that appears to fit.

**Illustrative example**

- **Base rate**: In a city, 5 % of the population is a professional musician.  
- **Specific evidence**: You meet a person who is a musician.  
- **Fallacy**: You “think” they’re *must* be a musician because you saw a musician, but you ignore the base rate that the chance of being a musician is only 5 %.  
- **Correct reasoning**: *The probability that a randomly chosen person is a musician is 5 %* – the base rate remains the relevant figure.

In political rhetoric, the base‑rate fallacy is often weaponized by **presenting a single anecdote or a small, unrepresentative sample** and then **claiming that it proves something about a larger population**. This misleads the audience because they ignore the broader statistical reality that should be considered.

### How to Spot the Base‑Rate Fallacy in Political Rhetoric

1. **Check the denominator** – Is the politician mentioning a tiny sample or a single event?  
2. **Look for omitted data** – What does the broader trend say?  
3. **Compare the numbers** – Are the specific figure(s) consistent with the overall statistics?  
4. **Assess the narrative** – Does the claim rely on anecdote or on a large, representative data set?

---

### Bottom Line

The base‑rate fallacy is a powerful rhetorical tool because it taps into the human tendency to see patterns in individual stories. By consciously looking for the *base rate* (the larger, general probability) and demanding that politicians supply that context, voters can guard against being misled by cherry‑picked anecdotes.
