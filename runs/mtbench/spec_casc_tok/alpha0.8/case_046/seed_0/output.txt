If the orbital speed of a satellite in a circular orbit is reduced, the satellite will no longer stay in a circular orbit.  
It will move inward into an elliptical orbit whose semi‑major axis is smaller than the original radius.  
Consequently the satellite’s minimum orbital radius (the perigee) will be smaller than the original radius, and its period of revolution will be shorter.

---

## 1. What happens when the speed is lowered?

### 1.1 Circular orbit condition

For a circular orbit of radius \(r\) about the Earth

\[
\frac{mv^{2}}{r}= \frac{GMm}{r^{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 
v_{\rm circ}= \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}\; .
\]

The gravitational force provides just enough centripetal acceleration to keep the satellite on that circle.

### 1.2 Reduction of speed

Suppose the satellite’s speed is suddenly decreased to \(v'<v_{\rm circ}\) while it is still at the same radius \(r\).  
The gravitational force still pulls the satellite inward with magnitude \(GMm/r^{2}\), but the required centripetal acceleration is now

\[
a_{\rm cent}= \frac{v'^2}{r} < \frac{GM}{r^{2}} .
\]

Hence the satellite experiences a net inward acceleration: it will no longer remain on the circular path.  
Its instantaneous velocity is now tangential, but the trajectory is no longer a circle; it is the beginning of an **elliptical orbit**.

---

## 2. Properties of the new orbit

### 2.1 Vis‑viva equation

For any Keplerian orbit

\[
v^{2}=GM\!\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right),
\]

where \(a\) is the semi‑major axis of the ellipse.

At the instant of the speed drop, the satellite is at radius \(r\) and has speed \(v'<v_{\rm circ}\).  
Insert \(v'\) into the vis‑viva equation:

\[
v'^2 = GM\!\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{1}{a} = \frac{2}{r} - \frac{v'^2}{GM}.
\]

Because \(v'^2 < GM/r\), the term \(\frac{v'^2}{GM}\) is smaller than \(\frac{1}{r}\), so

\[
\frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a < r .
\]

Thus the semi‑major axis of the new orbit is smaller than the original radius.  
The point of the speed drop is the **apogee** (farthest point from the Earth) of the new ellipse; the satellite will move inward to a perigee radius \(r_{\rm p} < r\).

### 2.2 Period of revolution

Kepler’s third law (derived from the vis‑viva equation) gives

\[
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{GM}} .
\]

Because \(a < r\), the period \(T\) is **smaller** than the original period \(T_{\rm circ}=2\pi\sqrt{r^{3}/GM}\).

---

## 3. Summary of the satellite’s motion

| Quantity | Before speed reduction | After speed reduction |
|----------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Orbit shape | Circular | Elliptical (apogee at the original radius) |
| Radius at that instant | \(r\) | \(r\) (apogee) |
| Semi‑major axis | \(r\) | \(a < r\) |
| Minimum radius (perigee) | – | \(r_{\rm p} < r\) |
| Period | \(T_{\rm circ}\) | \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^{3}/GM} < T_{\rm circ}\) |

The satellite will spiral inward toward the perigee while the orbital period decreases.  
If this reduction in speed is sustained (e.g., due to atmospheric drag or a continuous braking thruster), the satellite will keep losing orbital energy, its semi‑major axis and perigee will shrink, and eventually it will de‑orbit and re‑enter the atmosphere. If the speed reduction is a single impulse, the satellite will simply oscillate along its new elliptical orbit.

---

### Key physics principles used

1. **Conservation of angular momentum**: \(L = m r v\) decreases when \(v\) decreases, which forces the orbit to become elliptical (a circular orbit requires a specific \(L\)).  
2. **Vis‑viva equation** (derived from Newton’s law of gravitation and conservation of energy): relates velocity, radius, and semi‑major axis.  
3. **Kepler’s third law**: \(T\propto a^{3/2}\) links the semi‑major axis to the period.  

These principles jointly show that lowering the speed while keeping the satellite at the same radius forces the satellite into an elliptical orbit with a smaller semi‑major axis and a shorter period.
