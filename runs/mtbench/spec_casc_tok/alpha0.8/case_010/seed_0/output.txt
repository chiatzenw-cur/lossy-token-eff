In quantum mechanics the state of a system is described by a vector (or wavefunction) in a Hilbert space.  
If the system has a set of *basis states* \(\{|i\rangle\}\) (for example, the spin‑up \(|\uparrow\rangle\) and spin‑down \(|\downarrow\rangle\) states of an electron), the principle of superposition says that any linear combination of those basis states is also a physically admissible state:

\[
|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i\,|i\rangle ,
\qquad\text{with}\;\; \sum_i |c_i|^2 = 1 .
\]

The coefficients \(c_i\) are complex numbers; the probabilities of obtaining each basis outcome upon measurement are \(|c_i|^2\).  
If the system is in a pure superposition, the measurement will **collapse** the state to one of the basis states with the corresponding probability.

Typical examples:

| System | Basis states | Typical superposition |
|--------|--------------|-----------------------|
| Spin‑½ electron | \(|\uparrow\rangle, |\downarrow\rangle\) | \(|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\uparrow\rangle + |\downarrow\rangle\bigr)\) |
| Photon polarization | \(|H\rangle, |V\rangle\) | \(|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigl(|H\rangle + 2|V\rangle\bigr)\) |
| Two‑path interferometer | \(|\text{path A}\rangle, |\text{path B}\rangle\) | \(|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\text{A}\rangle + e^{i\phi}|\text{B}\rangle\bigr)\) |

The hallmark of superposition is *interference*: when two branches of a superposition recombine, the probability amplitudes can add or cancel, giving rise to interference fringes (e.g., the double‑slit experiment).

### Entanglement: a “superposition of correlations”

Entanglement is a property that arises when we look at **two or more systems** jointly.  
Suppose we have two qubits, \(A\) and \(B\). The joint state lives in the tensor product space \(\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B\).  
A general joint state can be written as

\[
|\Psi\rangle = \sum_{i,j} c_{ij}\,|i\rangle_A \otimes |j\rangle_B .
\]

If the coefficients factorize as \(c_{ij} = a_i\,b_j\), the joint state is a product state:  
\(|\Psi\rangle = \bigl(\sum_i a_i |i\rangle_A\bigr)\otimes\bigl(\sum_j b_j |j\rangle_B\bigr)\).  
In that case the two subsystems are independent.

**Entanglement** occurs when the joint state cannot be written as a product of individual states.  
A classic example is the Bell (EPR) state

\[
|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\bigr),
\]

which is a superposition of the *joint* basis states \(|00\rangle\) and \(|11\rangle\) but **not** a product of a state of \(A\) and a state of \(B\).

Key points linking superposition to entanglement:

1. **Superposition of joint states** – Entanglement is literally a superposition of product states that cannot be separated.  
2. **Correlations** – For an entangled state, the outcomes of measurements on the two subsystems are correlated in ways that cannot be explained by any classical (mixed) state.  
3. **Non‑locality** – When a measurement is performed on one subsystem, the joint state collapses, instantly determining the outcome of a measurement on the other subsystem, regardless of the distance between them. This is a direct consequence of the superposition principle applied to the combined system.  
4. **Entanglement as a resource** – Because the superposition is global, entangled states enable tasks like quantum teleportation, superdense coding, and violate Bell inequalities, which have no classical analogue.

| \(|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\uparrow\rangle + |\downarrow\rangle\bigr)\) | \(|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle + |\downarrow\downarrow\rangle\bigr)\) |
| Measurement of a single spin collapses to either \(|\uparrow\rangle\) or \(|\downarrow\rangle\). | Measurement of spin‑A collapses the joint state to either \(|\uparrow\rangle_A\lvert\uparrow\rangle_B\) or \(|\downarrow\rangle_A\lvert\downarrow\rangle_B\); the state of spin‑B is instantaneously determined. |

Thus, **superposition** is the foundation: it allows a quantum system to be in a mixture of basis states.  
**Entanglement** is the phenomenon that arises when two or more systems are put into a superposition of *combined* states that cannot be factorized—giving rise to correlations that defy classical explanation.
