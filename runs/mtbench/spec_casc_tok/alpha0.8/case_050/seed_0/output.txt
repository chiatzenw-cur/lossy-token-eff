At the heart of Socratic thought was a set of practical tools that turned ordinary conversations into philosophical investigations.  These tools were not so much “techniques” in the sense of a single, fixed script, but a pattern of interaction that encouraged interlocutors to expose the contradictions in their own beliefs.  Below is a guide to the most important elements of Socrates’ method and how they functioned to challenge the prevailing ideas of his time.

| # | Method | What It Looks Like | Why It Undermines Conventional Thinking |
|---|--------|--------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **1** | **Elenchus (Socratic Reversal)** | Socrates asks a question, receives an answer, then asks a *counter‑question* that reveals a logical inconsistency. | By turning a claim back on itself, Socrates forces people to confront the lack of firm foundations behind their opinions—an uncomfortable thing for a society that prized civic pride and rhetorical flourishes. |
| **2** | **Dialectic (Continuous Dialogue)** | Socrates engages in a back‑and‑forth conversation, not aiming for a single conclusion but for the refinement of the question itself. | The dialectic demonstrates that certainty is often illusory; it counters the prevailing notion that “the right answer is always obvious” and encourages critical thinking. |
| **3** | **Socratic Irony** | Socrates pretends to be ignorant or “somewhat uneducated,” inviting others to explain their ideas. | The pretense lowers the interlocutor’s guard and turns them into the “expert” of a subject. By doing so, Socrates exposes the thinness of their professed knowledge. |

## How The Methods Worked in Practice

1. **Elenchus in the Agora**  
   *Example:* In the *Apology*, Socrates asks a magistrate whether he (Socrates) is a harm or a help to the demes. Thinking the conversation should be straightforward, society expects a single answer. But Socrates keeps probing: “What would you say if you were a harm? What if you were a help?” The magistrate’s own answer—largely “a harm”—becomes a self‑fulfilling admission of a false belief about the role of the philosopher.

2. **Irony & the “Athenian Philosopher”**  
   In the *Meno*, Socrates feigns ignorance about virtue to lure Meno into explaining his own concept. By letting Meno claim he knows virtue, Socrates turns Meno’s claim into a demonstration of the impossible (the “theory of recollection”). This charming play on self‑perception shocked the audience into questioning the authority of the Sophists.

3. **Division and Aporetic Questions**  
   In the *Euthyphro*, Socrates parcels the concept of piety into “what is pious?” and “what is impious?” The question leads to the difficult “Is an act pious because the gods approve it, or do the gods approve it because it is pious?” The conversation stumbles on a foundational refusal to assume that divine approval automatically validates moral truth.

4. **Public Engagement**  
   Rather than writing treatises, Socrates spoke in public spaces. The theater of the agora made it impossible to ignore him; the louder his voice, the louder the audience’s realization that the *mata* (things) they had taken for granted were not undisputed.

5. **Paradox and the “All‑Kid”**  
   The *Republic*’s “All‑Kid” paradox—that a child’s mind is naturally perfect—demonstrates that the best we could do is to **carefully** preserve a child’s innate virtue. The Athenian society, which valued discipline and rhetoric, saw this as contrarian.

## Why Socrates’ “Method” Was a Challenge

| Prevailing Thought | Socratic Challenge | Result |
|--------------------|--------------------|--------|
| **Sophists’ Specialty** | Knowledge is a skill; cleverness is mastery. | Socrates shattered the idea that *skill* is equivalent to *truth*, showing that knowledge has an objective basis rather than merely rhetorical flourish. |
| **Unquestioned Authority** | Rulers, philosophers, and the gods are infallible. | Socrates’ persistent questioning of magistrates and the gods undermined the notion that authority could be taken as unquestioned. |
| **The Power of Rhetoric** | Persuasion is the ultimate goal. | Socrates’ pursuit of logical truth over rhetorical win actively de‑valorized the rhetorical games of the Athenian court. |
| **The Thirteenth Law of the City** | The city is a *syllabic* system; rational oversight is unnecessary. | Socrates highlighted the deficiencies in the law of the city’s “law of the city” itself, culminating in the conviction that the city’s values were not built on a logical explanatory framework. |

## Take‑away

Socrates’ method was not a rigid formula; it was an *attitude of inquiry*.  By:

1. **Asking the right questions** that inevitably led to contradictions.  
2. **Greatly exaggerating his own ignorance** to lower the defensive walls of his interlocutors.  
3. **Arbitrarily turning the conversation onward** to expose the ironies in ordinary certainties.  
4. **Performing in public spaces** so that the entire society could see and breathe in the uncertainty.  

He turned the prevailing orthodoxy into a living laboratory for *self‑questioning*.  His methods were the oldest form of “critical thinking” and the foundation of modern philosophy, democratic deliberation, and even scientific critique.  The result: Athens had to confront the possibility that what people thought they knew might be built on shaky foundations.
