Think of probability as a **menu** that tells you how likely each item (or outcome) is, just like a menu at a restaurant tells you how many different dishes you could order.

| Dish (Outcome) | Probability |

- **Daily decisions** (e.g., “Should I carry an umbrella?” = What’s the chance of rain?).
- **Games & sports** (e.g., “What’s the chance of getting a blackjack?").
- **Science & engineering** (e.g., “How likely is a particle to decay this way?”).
- **Finance & insurance** (e.g., “What’s the probability of a market crash?”).

## 🔢 Basic Concepts

| Concept | What it Means | Example |
|---------|---------------|---------|
| **Experiment** | A process you can repeat (e.g., rolling a die). | Rolling a die once. |
| **Sample Space (S)** | All possible outcomes of an experiment. | {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} for a fair die. |
| **Event** | A subset of the sample space. | Rolling an even number = {2, 4, 6}. |
| **Probability of an Event (P)** | A number between 0 and 1 that tells how often the event occurs. | P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2. |

## 📏 How to Compute Probability

### 1. **Classical Probability** (fair, equally likely outcomes)

**Formula**  
\[
P(A) = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of outcomes}}
\]

**Example**  
You have 3 red marbles and 7 blue marbles in a bag.  
- Total marbles = 3 + 7 = 10  
- Event: “draw a red marble”  
- Favorable = 3  

\[
P(\text{red}) = \frac{3}{10} = 0.30
\]

---

### 2. **Relative Frequency (Empirical)**

- Repeat the experiment many times.  
- Count how often event A occurs.  
- Divide by the total number of trials.

**Example**  
Flipping a coin 100 times gives 55 heads.  
\[
P(\text{heads}) \approx \frac{55}{100} = 0.55
\]

---

### 3. **Conditional Probability** (when one event impacts another)

**Formula**  
\[
P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}
\]

**Example**  
You have a bag with 5 black and 5 white balls.  
- Draw a ball, it’s black.  
- Put it back, draw again.  
- Probability the second ball is black given the first was black?  

Since you replaced the ball, the draws are independent.  
\[
P(\text{black on 2nd} | \text{black on 1st}) = P(\text{black on 2nd}) = \frac{5}{10} = 0.5
\]

If you **didn't** replace the ball:  
- First draw black → only 4 black left, 9 total.  
\[
P(\text{black on 2nd} | \text{black on 1st}) = \frac{4}{9} \approx 0.444
\]

## 🧠 Common Pitfalls

1. **Assuming independence when it’s not** – e.g., drawing without replacement.
2. **Misinterpreting “chance” vs. “probability”** – the word “chance” is vague.
3. **Confusing probability with “likelihood”** – likelihood is a statistical term, not the same as probability.
4. **Using “probability” as a deterministic prediction** – it’s a guide, not a guarantee.

## 📘 Quick “Starter” Problem

**Problem**  
You roll a **fair** 6‑sided die.  
What’s the probability that the number is **greater than 4**?

**Solution**  
- Sample space: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}  
- Favorable outcomes: {5, 6} (two numbers)  
- Probability: \(\displaystyle \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.33\)

**Interpretation**  
If you roll the die many times, about 33% of the rolls will land on 5 or 6.
