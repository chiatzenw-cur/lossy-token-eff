| Feature | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---------|------------|-------------|
| **Heat Flow** | Releases heat to the surroundings (ΔH < 0). | Absorbs heat from the surroundings (ΔH > 0). |
| **Energy of Products** | Lower than that of the reactants. | Higher than that of the reactants. |
| **Energy Profile** | “Downhill” in the reaction coordinate diagram. | “Uphill” in the reaction coordinate diagram. |
| **Typical Example** | Burning wood, combustion of methane, neutralization of acid with base. | Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water, photosynthesis, melting ice. |

### How We Distinguish Them  
The most straightforward criterion is the **enthalpy change, ΔH** (or the heat measured in a calorimeter).  

1. **Calorimetry** – Conduct a calorimetry experiment (e.g., bomb calorimeter) and record the temperature change of the surrounding medium.  
2. **Calculate ΔH** –  
   \[
   \Delta H = -C_{\text{cal}}\,\Delta T
   \]
   where \(C_{\text{cal}}\) is the calorimeter’s heat capacity and \(\Delta T\) is the temperature change.  
3. **Interpret the sign**  
   * If \(\Delta H\) is **negative** (temperature rises), the reaction is exothermic.  
   * If \(\Delta H\) is **positive** (temperature drops), the reaction is endothermic.

**Why the sign matters**  
- **Exothermic**: Energy is released because the system’s internal energy decreases. The products are in a lower‑energy state.  
- **Endothermic**: Energy is absorbed, raising the system’s internal energy. The products occupy a higher‑energy state.

---

## Real‑World Example: Dissolving Ammonium Nitrate in Water

| Step | What Happens | Why It Matters |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| 1. **Mixing** | Ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) is added to water. | The solute and solvent interact on a molecular level. |
| 2. **Heat Transfer** | The temperature of the solution drops by several degrees. | The measured temperature drop shows a **positive** ΔH (endothermic). |
| 3. **Energy Balance** | The system (solution) absorbs heat from the surroundings (the beaker, the air, the experimenter’s hand). | The exothermic reaction that occurs inside the solution (breaking of ionic bonds) is not enough to offset the energy required to separate the ions and hydrate them. |
| 4. **Practical Use** | The cooling effect is used in instant cold packs. | The endothermic reaction is harnessed to absorb heat and provide cooling. |

### What the Numbers Show
- **Calorimeter readout**: \(C_{\text{cal}} = 4.18\,\text{kJ K}^{-1}\) (typical a 1‑L solution).  
- **Temperature drop**: \(\Delta T = -5.0\,\text{K}\).  
- **ΔH**: \(\Delta H = -4.18 \times (-5.0) = +20.9\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}\).  
  The positive ΔH confirms the process is **endothermic**.

### Contrast with an Exothermic Example  
- **Burning methane (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O)**  
  - ΔH ≈ **–890 kJ mol⁻¹** (very negative).  
  - Temperature rises, heat is released, and the reaction is exothermic.

---

### Bottom Line
- **Exothermic**: \(\Delta H < 0\) → heat is released → products lower in energy.  
- **Endothermic**: \(\Delta H > 0\) → heat is absorbed → products higher in energy.  

The sign of ΔH, measured experimentally or calculated from enthalpy tables, is the definitive criterion for distinguishing between the two types of reactions.
