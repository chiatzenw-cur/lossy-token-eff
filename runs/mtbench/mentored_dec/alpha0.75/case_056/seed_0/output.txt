> “Probability is a way of telling you how likely something is to happen.”  

---

### 1. Think of **probability** as a “chance”

- **Chance** is just a fancy word for *probability*.
- We usually express it as a **fraction (½, ¼, 1/6, 0.7)**, a percentage (50 %, 20 %), or a number between 0 and 1 (0.00–1.00).

**Why it matters**: When deciding whether to carry an umbrella or split a pizza, you can use probability to make the smartest (and most fun) choice.

---

### 2. The Core Formula

> **P(Either)** = # of *favorable outcomes* ÷ # of *total outcomes*

#### A quick example

| Event | Favorable outcomes | Total outcomes |
|-------|--------------------|----------------|
| Draw an Ace from a standard deck | 4 (♠A, ♣A, ♥A, ♦A) | 52 |
| Throw a 6‑sided die | 1 (6) | 6 |

- **P(Ace) = 4 / 52 = 1/13 ≈ 0.077 (7.7 %)**  
- **P(6) = 1 / 6 ≈ 0.1667 (16.7 %)**

This simple division is the backbone of almost all probability calculations.

---

### 3. The Building Blocks

| **Symbol** | **Meaning** | **Example** |
|------------|-------------|--------------|
| **E** | An event (e.g., “draw a red card”) | E = red card |
| **P(E)** | Probability of event E | P(red card) = 1/2 |
| **Ω (Omega)** | All possible outcomes | All 52 cards in a deck |
| **A ∩ B** | Both A and B happen | A = “draw a heart”, B = “draw a queen” |
| **A ∪ B** | A or B happens (or both) | A = “draw a heart”, B = “draw a king” |
| **A^c** | Not A (the complement) | A = “draw a 6”, A^c = “not a 6” |

#### Types of events

| Type | What it means | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|
| **Independent** | One event’s result doesn’t affect the other | Tossing a coin *and* rolling a dice |
| **Dependent** | One event affects another | Drawing two cards without replacement |
| **Disjoint** | They can’t happen together | “It’s a Tuesday” and “It’s a Thursday” |

---

### 4. Everyday Situations

| Situation | What you’re trying to know | Example calculation |
|-----------|---------------------------|--------------------|
| **Choosing a snack** | Probability of picking the boundary‑less “breakfast” snack | 3 of 5 options → 3/5 = 60 % |
| **Ride‑sharing arrival** | Probability that the driver will arrive in 5 minutes | If 3 out of 10 similar drivers arrive ≤5 min → 3/10 = 30 % |
| **Weather** | Probability that it will rain | If 70 % chance in the forecast → 0.7 = 70 % |
| **Playing a card game** | Probability that your hand contains 3 of a kind | 3/52 = 5.8 % |

---

### 5. “What If” – Complicated Cases

#### A. Probability of *at least* one event (two dice, looking for a 6)

- The easier trick: **complement** the opposite (nothing happens).
  - Probability *no 6*: 5/6 × 5/6 = 25/36 ≈ 0.694
  - Probability *at least one 6*: 1 – 25/36 = 11/36 ≈ 0.306

#### B. Conditional Probability (given information)

- **P(A | B)** = Probability of A **given** that B has happened.
  - Example: “If you know you drew a red card, what’s the probability it’s a heart?”  
  - P(heart | red) = 13/26 ≈ 0.5 (since there are 13 hearts, 13 diamonds).

#### C. Expected Value (averaging long‑term outcomes)

- **EV = Σ (value of outcome × probability of that outcome)**  
  - Example: A slot machine that pays $10 on a 1/50 chance, $0 otherwise.  
  - EV = $10 × 1/50 + $0 × 49/50 = $0.20 per pull.

### 6. Quick Practice Problems

> **Problem 1**: A group of 12 friends has 9 females and 3 males.  
>  You pick 4 friends at random.  
>  **Question:** What is the probability that at least one of them is male?

**Solution**  
- Total ways to pick 4 from 12: **C(12,4)**  
- All‑female ways: C(9,4)  
- Probability of *at least one* male: 1 – [C(9,4) / C(12,4)]  
  = 1 – (126 ÷ 495)  
  = 1 – 0.2545  
  ≈ 0.7455 (**74.5 %**).

> **Problem 2**: If you roll a fair die twice and want a sum of 7, what is the probability?

- Possible pairs (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), plus (1,6) and (6,1).  
- 6 winning pairs.  
- Total pairs: 6 × 6 = 36.  
- Probability: 6/36 = **1/6 ≈ 16.7 %**.

Try these on your own; they’re a great way to get comfortable with the math!

## 🎉 Take Home

1. **Probability is a fraction**—favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.  
2. **Use complement events** (doing the opposite) when it’s easier to count.  
3. **Think in real‑world terms** (e.g., toss a coin, pick a card, deal with “today’s weather”).  
4. **Practice** – each new scenario is a tiny experiment.
