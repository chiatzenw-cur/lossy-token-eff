| Feature | **Exothermic** | **Endothermic** |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Heat flow** | **Released to the surroundings** (the reaction “feels hot”). | **Absorbed from the surroundings** (the reaction “feels cold”). |
| **Enthalpy change (ΔH)** | **Negative (ΔH < 0)** – products are lower in energy than the reactants. | **Positive (ΔH > 0)** – products are higher in energy than the reactants. |
| **Internal energy change (ΔU)** | **Negative (ΔU < 0)** in an adiabatic system; heat leaves the system. | **Positive (ΔU > 0)** in an adiabatic system; heat must be supplied. |
| **Effect on temperature** | Surrounding temperature rises if the reaction is isolated. | Surrounding temperature falls if the reaction is isolated. |
| **Typical examples** | Combustion of gasoline in a car engine, burning of coal, neutralization of acid and base (often a few degrees). | Dissolving ammonium chloride in water, photo‑oxidation of cloud‑water, the cooling effect of a dry‑ice/CO₂ system. |
| **Thermodynamic flag** | If the Gibbs free energy change ΔG < 0 *and* the heat released is positive, the process is spontaneous and exothermic. | If ΔG < 0 but the process requires external heat input, the reaction is “endothermic” despite being overall spontaneous. |

### 1. How to distinguish them

| Observable | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|------------|------------|-------------|
| **Measured ΔH sign** | **Negative** – determined by calorimetry. | **Positive** – heat is supplied. |
| **ΔT of a calorimeter** | **ΔT > 0** (if the experimental calorimeter is not insulated) – the waters inside the calorimeter warms up. | **ΔT < 0** – the solutions or surroundings cool down. |
| **Adiabatic calorimeter** | **System’s internal energy drops** (ΔU < 0) → the system gives off heat. | **System’s internal energy rises** (ΔU > 0) → the system must absorb heat. |
| **Adiabatic eye test** | The reaction mixture looks hot; the surroundings are warmer. | The reaction mixture feels cold; the surrounding air or a nearby thermometer registers a drop. |
| **Chemical intuition** | The difference of heat of formation shows a net negative ΔH (e.g., ΔHrxn← -200 kJ mol⁻¹). | Reaction terms add up to a positive ΔH (e.g., ΔHrxn← +50 kJ mol⁻¹). |

Any analytic method that tells us *how much* heat the reaction released or absorbed (for example, the calorimetry equation **Q = m CΔT**) is the practical tool to under‑go the sign.

---

### 2. The “criteria” we use

- **Enthalpy change**: ΔH = Hproducts – Hreactants.  The sign of ΔH is the deciding factor on how to categorize a reaction.
- **Temperature change within a sealed calorimeter**: ΔT > 0 signals exothermic; ΔT < 0 signals endothermic.
- **Observation of the surroundings**: Whether the reaction mixture warms or cools the environment.
- **Spectral or chemical evidence**: The release of photons or the absorption of “heat” is often implicitly correlated with the ΔH sign.

In practice, the **ΔH sign** is considered the most reliable and directly corresponds to the “heat” you *measure* or *feel*.

---

### 3. Real‑world example – the coolness of a dairy “ice bath” (endothermic)

**The reaction**:  
When an ice–water bath (sometimes called a “cooling bath”) is added to a hot extruding melt of gelatin, the “ice bath” must absorb heat to keep the gelatin from over‑cooking.

**What actually happens in the bath**:  

1. **Ice → Water** (fusion).  
   For water at 0 °C, the heat of fusion is 6.01 kJ mol⁻¹ (i.e., +6.01 kJ energy per mole of water).  
2. **Water (liquid) ↔ 0 °C (liquid)** (maintains the bath temperature).  
3. **Heat absorbed by the bath** (from the hot gelatin, the stove, or the oven).

**Connection to exothermic / endothermic**  
- The *melting of ice* is **endothermic with ΔH ≈ +6.01 kJ mol⁻¹** because the system absorbs energy from the ambient to get the solid to the liquid phase.  
- This makes the **bath feel cold**; the reaction mixture (gelatin) in the bath is **cooling** because the bath absorbs more heat than it has to give back.

**Why it’s a textbook example**  
- It is wholly implicit: the liquid temperature *lowers* because our system (the bath) *absorbs* heat.
- The calorimeter would have a ΔT < 0, and the ΔH (ice’s latent heat) clearly positive.
- You can also measure it directly: place a temperature probe in the bath, count the time until the ice is completely melted and the surrounding temperature drops to the expected ~5 °C for the gelatin.

### 4. Another quick example – an exothermic reaction (combustion of paper)

1. **Paper** – \( \text{C}_x\text{H}_y\text{O}_z \) – reacts with **O₂**.  
   \[
   \text{C}_x\text{H}_y\text{O}_z + O_2 \;\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}\; CO_2 + H_2O + \text{heat}
   \]
2. **Measured ΔH** ≈ -2000 kJ mol⁻¹ (negative).  
3. **Observation**: The surrounding temperature rises dramatically, the flame glows.  
4. **Calorimeter**: The temperature of the surrounding liquid will climb (ΔT > 0).

The exact form of the experiment – whether the reaction produces light, a flame or something else – is irrelevant to the core criterion: it is a *negative* ΔH and heat released to the surroundings.

---

### 5. Take‑away

- **Exothermic** ⇑: heat released → negative ΔH, great to keep your kitchen warm.  
- **Endothermic** ⇓: heat absorbed → positive ΔH, used to keep it cool in an ice bath, in a cooling water system, or even in a chemical refrigeration cycle.  

Knowing the *sign of ΔH* is the single, most reliable fingerprint of why a reaction feels hot or cold, and it is the key that differentiates between “exothermic” and “endothermic” in any chemical world.
