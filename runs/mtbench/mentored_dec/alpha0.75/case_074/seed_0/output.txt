Solid calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) is a basic carbonate.  
When it is mixed with aqueous hydrochloric acid (HCl) it reacts in two steps:

1. **Acid neutralization:**  
   H⁺ from the acid reacts with the carbonate ion (CO₃²⁻) giving a carbonate‑deficient intermediate.

2. **Carbonate decomposition:**  
   This intermediate rapidly decomposes to give water and carbon dioxide gas.

The net reaction can be written as a single acid‑base reaction:

\[
\boxed{\text{CaCO}_3(s) \;+\; 2\,\text{HCl}(aq)\;\longrightarrow\;\text{CaCl}_2(aq)\;+\;\text{CO}_2(g)\;+\;\text{H}_2\!O(l)}
\]

- **CaCO₃** – solid calcium carbonate  
- **HCl** – aqueous hydrochloric acid (free H⁺ ions)  
- **CaCl₂** – aqueous calcium chloride (soluble in water)  
- **CO₂** – carbon dioxide gas (released as bubbles)  
- **H₂O** – water formed in the solution

The equation is already balanced: one Ca, one C, three O (2 × O from HCl + 2 × O from CaCO₃) give 3 O on the right, 2 H+ from HCl + 2 H from water give 2 H on the right, etc.

**2. What type of reaction is this?**

- **Broad classification:** *Acid–base (neutralization) reaction* (and more specifically a *carbonate–acid* reaction).  
- The reaction is also a *decomposition* of the carbonate part (CO₃²⁻ → CO₂ + O²⁻) coupled with proton‑transfer.  
- For most chemists’ “common repertoire” it is a classic **double‑displacement/acid–base** reaction that produces gas and a soluble salt.

**3. Observations that the reaction is taking place**

| Observation | What it indicates |
|-------------|--------------------|
| **Effervescence (bubbling)** | CO₂ gas produced in the reaction mixture. |
| **Increase in volume of the gas** | CO₂ escaping into the air.|
| **Heat** | Exothermic neutralization (the solution often warms).|
| **Color change** | Usually no color change (unless cuprous or other colored salts are present), but clarity of the solution may improve as the solid dissolves.|
| **Dissolution of the solid** | The once‑solid calcium carbonate disappears, leaving a clear liquid.|
| **Rapid disappearance of the solid** | That the acid is reacting fast enough.|
| **Foam or froth sometimes** (especially when acid is concentrated) | Some CO₂ bubbles are trapped in the liquid forming a froth.|
| **Acidic smell (HCl vapors)** | A weak HCl odor may be present in the hoods if the acid is not very dilute.|
| **Temperature rise** | Typical exothermic nature of the reaction. |

**4. How to observe it in a lab**

1. **Set up a clean beaker or flask** with a small amount of CaCO₃.  
2. Add **few drops of dilute HCl** (or slowly pour it into a sealed container).  
3. **Watch the fizzing** – the carbonate dissolves while CO₂ bubbles appear.  
4. If the solution is kept in a heated or sealed container, you can “collect” the CO₂ by pointing a curved glass tube into a bag or an inverted glass bulb – you will see the CO₂ displacing the air; you’ll also see the resulting water (e.g., a clear liquid of CaCl₂).  

The process is quick and visibly dramatic: a bright, acidic smell, fizzing bubbles, a clear solution where the solid has disappeared. That’s the complete picture of a classic acid–carbonate reaction!
