**Definition** –  
The base‑rate fallacy occurs when a decision‑maker, statistician or pundit **ignores the general population frequency** (the “base rate”) of an event and focuses instead on a particular, usually vivid, case. The result is a distorted impression that the particular event is more typical or more relevant than it truly is.

In everyday politics, this can look like:

| What the argument tells you | What the argument ignores | Why it matters |
|-----------------------------|---------------------------|----------------|
| “The next big murder in a city is proof that crime is surging.” | The overall, *average* crime rate in the city is down and perhaps lower than the national average. | People gravitate toward the dramatic vision of a single incident, not the quiet statistical reality. |
| “Our border‑policy would save X people. The evidence is the show‑stopper case of a migrant.* | The overall, **base‑rate** of successful migrants vs. people who become authorities is extremely low. | We talk about dramatic “heroic” narratives while dealing with a fallacy that the story is far from representative. |

**Why it is a fallacy** – In probability theory, a total probability of the event must be weighed against its *prior* frequency. Skipping that prior tends to lead to decisions that have a higher risk of mis‑classification.

### How to Spot the Fallacy

| Question | How to Spot It | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| “Who is speaking? Do they mention a single anecdote or a bunch of data?” | A solitary voice often signals a base‑rate fallacy. | Singular data points belittle bigger numbers. |
| “What is the base rate of this phenomenon in the general population?” | Compute an average or look at the fact pattern. | Gives you a more accurate estimate. |
| “Given the base rate, does the anecdote actually create the effect it claims?” | A single event may not change the entire distribution. | Substantially different conclusions can be drawn. |
