If a satellite that is in a circular orbit around the Earth has its speed reduced,
the orbit will no longer stay circular.  The satellite will fall into a lower‑energy
elliptical orbit whose *semi‑major axis* is smaller than the original radius.  
Consequently the satellite’s radius at the lowest point (the perigee) becomes
smaller than it was before, and the period of revolution becomes shorter.

---

## Why does the orbit change?

### 1. The circular‑orbit condition  
For a satellite of mass \(m\) in a circular orbit of radius \(r\)

\[
\frac{GM_{\oplus} m}{r^{2}}=\frac{m v^{2}}{r}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;v_{\rm circ}= \sqrt{\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r}}
\]

where \(G\) is the gravitational constant and \(M_{\oplus}\) is the mass of the Earth.

### 2. What happens when \(v\) is decreased?

If the speed is instantaneously changed from \(v_{\rm circ}\) to a smaller value
\(v' < v_{\rm circ}\) while the satellite is still at the radius \(r\), the actual
centripetal acceleration required is

\[
a_{\rm req}= \frac{v'^2}{r} < \frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r^{2}}=a_{\rm grav}
\]

so the gravitational pull exceeds the required centripetal push – the
satellite starts to accelerate inward.  The orbit can no longer be circular
and the satellite will settle into an elliptical path.

---

## Energy viewpoint

The specific mechanical energy (energy per unit mass) of the satellite is

\[
\varepsilon=\frac{v^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r}
\]

For a circular orbit, \(\varepsilon_{\rm circ}= -\dfrac{GM_{\oplus}}{2r}\).

After the speed reduction:

\[
\varepsilon' = \frac{v'^2}{2}-\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r}
< \frac{v_{\rm circ}^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r}
= \varepsilon_{\rm circ}
\]

Thus the new specific energy \(\varepsilon'\) is **more negative**.  
For a Keplerian orbit the semi‑major axis \(a\) relates to the energy by

\[
\varepsilon = -\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2a}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = -\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2\varepsilon}
\]

A more negative \(\varepsilon'\) gives a smaller \(a\).  
The new orbit is therefore an ellipse whose semi‑major axis \(a'\) is
smaller than the original radius \(r\).

The perigee radius \(r_{\min}\) and apogee radius \(r_{\max}\) are

\[
r_{\min}=a'(1-e),\qquad r_{\max}=a'(1+e)
\]

with eccentricity \(e\) depending on the exact magnitude of the speed
reduction.  In any case \(r_{\min}<r\); the satellite actually goes closer to
Earth at the point where it had the original speed.

---

## Period of revolution

Kepler’s third law for a bound orbit is

\[
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{GM_{\oplus}}}
\]

Because \(a'\!<\!r\), the new period

\[
T' = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a'^{3}}{GM_{\oplus}}}
\]

is **shorter** than the original period \(T_{\rm circ}=2\pi\sqrt{r^{3}/GM_{\oplus}}\).

---

## Summary

| After speed reduction | Orbital radius (at perigee) | Orbital radius (overall) | Period |
|-----------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------|--------|
| Circular assumption invalid | Smaller than before | Reduced semi‑major axis (ellipse) | Shorter |

So, when the satellite’s speed decreases, the satellite drops to a lower, smaller‑radius orbit, and it completes a revolution in a shorter time.
