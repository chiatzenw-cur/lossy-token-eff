## How These Methods Interact With the “Prevailing Thought”

1. **The Sophist Temptation**  
   In early fifth‑century Athens the Sophists were seen as cunning teachers of rhetoric who could make any point sound convincing.  Socrates showed that *style* alone can’t guarantee truth.  He challenged them in dialogues such as **Euthyphro** (honour) and **Protagoras** (virtue), revealing that the Sophists abandoned intuitive ethical reasoning in favor of persuasive speech.

2. **Political and Demotic Doctrine**  
   Democracy presented itself as “the greatest”.  Socrates, by probing the definition of “good” and “justice,” found that many citizens mistakenly accepted “the will of the majority” as virtue.  His paradoxical *peripatetic* refusal to define “everyone’s rights” exposed the precariousness of political legitimacy.

3. **Cultural Conventions**  
   The Athenians revered their gods and historiography.  Socrates distrusted the uncritical acceptance of myths, insisting that we should examine any claim by how it relates to *phronesis* (practical wisdom).  He engaged poets, demagogues, and judges by refusing to accept the obvious, insisting that doctrine “endure only if its content is clarified”.

4. **Education System**  
   The conventional schooling promoted memorization of the Sophists’ treatises.  Socrates’s method of questioning rewrote the basis of pedagogy: students should interrogate content, not simply accept it.  It still underlies modern critical and liberal education.

## A Brief Narrative Flow (How Socrates Countered Life in Athens)

1. **He starts in the Agora**—the public square of Athens—a space where Athenian opinion is made.  
2. **Encounter a Sophist**—they claim virtue can be taught purely by appealing to “good taste.”  
3. **Socratic method**: "What is virtue?" *A different answer.* The Sophist's answer collapses.  
4. **Move to the legal forums**—courtrooms were the battlegrounds of Athenian democracy.  
5. **Socratic *epoche***: In *Apology* he turns the legal indictment into an intellectual inquiry, showing that the charges are based on misunderstandings.  
6. **Public debates**—Socrates regularly engaged with Athenian leaders in person, forcing them to defend policy choices in analytical, logical terms.  
7. **Civic Reform**—in the letter to the Greek states, Socrates argues that accountability requires citizens to question enslaver‑heads or leaders.

## Legacy: Why These Methods Matter Today

- **Dialectic as the skeleton of Western critical thinking**: modern professors still use Socratic questioning in seminars.  
- **Infinity of Historical Integrity**: Socrates introduced a procedural method to confront “strong consensus” (like the book “The History of the Tyrants” or the way atheists de‑violate the state).  
- **Ethical Self‑Analysis**: His insistence on “know thyself” remains central to moral philosophy, psychotherapy, and even contemporary public policy debates.

### Bottom Line

Socrates did not swim against a wall; he *consciously interrogated* the architects of Athenian thought.  His key methods—the **elenchus (cross‑examination)**, **Socratic irony**, **Aporia**, **dialectic**, **epoche**, **narrative questioning**, and **critical self‑analysis**—provide a systematic, iterative process to make prevailing ideas visible, to reveal their defects, and to invite emerging communities into a construction that is genuinely “under debate” rather than “already decided.”  These were the practical tools he used to “challenge the prevailing thoughts of his time.”
