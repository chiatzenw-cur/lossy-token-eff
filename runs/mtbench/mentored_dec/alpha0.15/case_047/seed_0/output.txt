### Criteria We Use to Decide Whether a Reaction Is Exothermic or Endothermic

1. **Measurement of Heat Flow**  
   - **Calorimetry**: In a bomb or coffee‑cup calorimeter, the temperature change of the water (or bomb) tells us whether heat was supplied or taken away.  
   - **Sign convention**: By convention, a **positive** \(q_{\text{rxn}}\) (heat evolved by the reaction) means the reaction is exothermic; a **negative** \(q_{\text{rxn}}\) (heat absorbed) means endothermic.  

2. **Enthalpy Change (\(ΔH\))**  
   - \(ΔH = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}\).  
   - If \(ΔH\) is negative, the products have lower enthalpy than reactants → exothermic.  
   - If \(ΔH\) is positive, the products have higher enthalpy → endothermic.

3. **Temperature Observation**  
   - In a *separated* system, a rise in temperature of the *surroundings* (or of the reaction mixture itself) indicates exothermic; a drop indicates endothermic.

4. **Thermodynamic Windows**  
   - For a *constant‑pressure* environment (typical lab conditions), the enthalpy change equals the heat exchanged: \(ΔH = q_p\).  
   - For a *constant‑volume* setting (bomb calorimeter), the internal energy change equals the heat: \(ΔU = q_v\).

5. **Energy Profile Diagrams**  
   - Exothermic paths have a lower potential energy for products than reactants.  
   - Endothermic paths have a higher potential energy for products.

## Real‑World Example

### The Combustion of Methane (Exothermic)

**Reaction:**  
\[
\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2\,O_2(g) \;\longrightarrow\; CO_2(g) + 2\,H_2O(g)} \quad \Delta H = -890\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
\]

**Why it’s exothermic?**  
- **Enthalpy change**: The product side (CO₂ + H₂O) is 890 kJ per mole of CH₄ less energetic than the reactants.  
- **Heat release**: When a methane flame is lit, the surroundings glow hot. The fire feels warm because the reaction is pushing heat outward.  
- **Calorimetry**: If you put this reaction in a calorimeter, the thermometer would record a rise in temperature of the water, confirming \(q_{\text{rxn}}>0\).

### The Ammonium Nitrate Dissolution in Water (Endothermic)

**Reaction:**  
\[
\mathrm{NH_4NO_3(s) \;\longrightarrow\; NH_4^+(aq) + NO_3^-(aq)} \quad \Delta H = +25.7\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
\]

**Why it’s endothermic?**  
- **Enthalpy change**: The dissolved ions are higher in energy than the solid crystal.  
- **Heat absorption**: When you dissolve NH₄NO₃ in water, the solution feels cold to the touch.  
- **Calorimetry**: An ice‑cold probe inserted into the dissolving solution would record a temperature drop; \(q_{\text{rxn}}<0\).

**Bottom line:**  
*Exothermic reactions give *away* energy, leaving heat in the surroundings and lowering internal energy of the products; endothermic reactions *consume* energy, absorbing it from surroundings and raising the internal energy of the products.*
