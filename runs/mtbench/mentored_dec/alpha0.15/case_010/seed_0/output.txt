**Basic statement:**  
A quantum system is described by a state vector \(|\psi\rangle\) living in a complex Hilbert space \(\mathcal H\).  
If \(|\phi\rangle\) and \(|\chi\rangle\) are two *possible* states (for example, eigenstates of some observable), then any linear combination

\[
|\psi\rangle = \alpha\,|\phi\rangle + \beta\,|\chi\rangle , \qquad 
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C,\; |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
\]

is also a legitimate state of the system.  
This linearity is the heart of the *superposition principle*.

**Physical meaning**  
- The state is *not* a random mixture of \(|\phi\rangle\) and \(|\chi\rangle\); it is a coherent sum.  
- When a measurement of an observable is made, the probability of seeing outcome \(i\) is \(|\langle i|\psi\rangle|^2\).  
- After a measurement with outcome \(i\), the state collapses to \(|i\rangle\) (in the idealized textbook situation).

**Common example**

An electron’s spin along the \(z\)-axis has eigenstates \(|\!\uparrow_z\rangle\) (spin‑up) and \(|\!\downarrow_z\rangle\) (spin‑down).  
A superposition could be

\[
|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\!\uparrow_z\rangle + |\!\downarrow_z\rangle\bigr),
\]

which has no definite value of \(S_z\) until measured.

### 2.  Quantum entanglement

**Definition**  
Consider two (or more) subsystems with Hilbert spaces \(\mathcal H_A\) and \(\mathcal H_B\).  
The composite system lives in the tensor product \(\mathcal H_A\otimes\mathcal H_B\).  
A bipartite state \(|\Psi\rangle\) is **entangled** if it cannot be written as a single product

\[
|\Psi\rangle \neq |\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B .
\]

Instead, it must involve a *superposition* of product states:

\[
|\Psi\rangle = \sum_{i,j} c_{ij}\, |i\rangle_A \otimes |j\rangle_B .
\]

If at least two terms \((i,j)\) with non‑zero coefficients are necessary, the state is entangled.

**Canonical example – a Bell state**

\[
|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl(|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B + |1\rangle_A|1\rangle_B\Bigr).
\]

Each qubit individually is maximally uncertain (its reduced density matrix is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}\)), yet the pair is perfectly correlated: measuring \(Z\) on one automatically tells you the result on the other.

### 3.  How superposition underpins entanglement

| Feature | Superposition (single system) | Entanglement (multiple systems) |
|---------|--------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Mathematical form** | Linear combination in the system’s Hilbert space | Linear combination *in the tensor‑product space* |
| **Coherence** | Single system’s phases interfere | Coherence between different *joint* basis states |
| **Measurement outcome** | Probabilistic collapse to one of the eigenstates | Joint measurement outcomes exhibit correlations that violate classical bounds (Bell inequalities) |
| **Necessity for correlation** | Alone no. | **Yes** – entangled correlations arise precisely because the composite state is a superposition of *product* basis states that cannot be factorized |

In other words, an **entangled state is a special kind of superposition**: the superposition is taken not over states of a single subsystem but over tensor‑product basis states of *all* subsystems.

---

### 4.  Entanglement vs. Superposition: the subtle differences

| Superposition | Entanglement |
|---------------|--------------|
| May involve a single subsystem. | Always involves at least two subsystems. |
| Can be described by a single vector \(|\psi\rangle\). | Requires a vector in the joint space \(\mathcal H_A\otimes\mathcal H_B\). |
| Expressible as a sum of single‑particle basis states. | Expressible as a sum of *product* basis states, but **cannot be factorized** into a single product state. |
| Basis for interference patterns (e.g., double‑slit). | Basis for non‑local correlations that cannot be mimicked by classical randomness. |

Thus, while every entangled state contains superposition, not every superposition is entangled.

### 5.  Physical intuition and implications

1. **Creation of entanglement:**  
   By allowing two systems to interact (e.g., two spins coupled by a Hamiltonian or two photons produced in a parametric down‑conversion process), the interaction term in the Schrödinger equation rapidly drives the joint state into a superposition that is not factorisable.  

2. **Non‑local correlations:**  
   Because the joint state contains coherent weight on multiple product states, measurement on one subsystem instantaneously changes the *state description* of the other, but it does not transmit signal faster than light (no signalling theorem). This is the quantum “spooky‐action” that Bell’s theorem and experiments expose.

3. **Monogamy of entanglement:**  
   A system maximally entangled with one partner cannot be entangled with another—this is a deep consequence of the linear superposition structure.

4. **Applications:**  
   - **Quantum teleportation:** Uses an entangled pair and a Bell‑state measurement to transfer an unknown state using only local operations and classical communication.  
   - **Superdense coding:** Sends two classical bits by transmitting only one qubit that is part of an entangled pair.  
   - **Quantum error correction, quantum algorithms (e.g., Shor’s, Grover’s).**

### 6.  Bottom line

- **Superposition**  
  – A quantum system can exist in a weighted sum of basis states.  
  – This is a purely quantum consequence of linearity and gives rise to interference.

- **Entanglement**  
  – A property of composite systems where the joint state is a non‑factorizable superposition across subsystems.  
  – Entanglement is *built* from superposition, but it introduces correlations between subsystems that have no classical analogue.

Understanding these two ideas and their interplay is essential for grasping quantum information science, quantum communication, and the foundational questions about the nature of reality that quantum mechanics raises.
