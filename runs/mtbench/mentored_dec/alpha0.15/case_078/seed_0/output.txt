The **base‑rate fallacy** (also called the *base‑rate neglect*) is a cognitive bias in which people or decision‑makers ignore or underestimate the *base rate*—the underlying frequency or prevalence of an event or characteristic in a population—when evaluating evidence or making a judgment. Instead, they focus on the *specific* or *case‑specific* information presented.

**In other words:**  
When you’re told a story (“This group of individuals works in a kitchen; one of them stole the lunch money.”) people often over‑estimate the probability that “any kitchen worker” is a thief, because the story is vivid and specific, even though the *base rate* (the actual percentage of kitchen workers who steal) might be very low.

### Why It Matters

- **Misjudged risk**: Underestimating rare risks or over‑estimating common ones.
- **Poor policy decisions**: Policies based on anecdotal evidence can ignore valuable population data.
- **Propaganda**: Politicians can exploit the bias to shape public beliefs, especially in emotionally charged contexts.

## Five Ways Politicians Use the Base‑Rate Fallacy in Campaigns

| # | Tactic | How it exploits the fallacy | Example in a political context |
|---|--------|----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **1** | **Highlighting “rare” success stories** | Focusing on a single or small number of successes that appear extraordinary while ignoring the low prevalence of such successes among all candidates or policies. | “Our candidate won an election against a 40‑point margin in a historically opposition‑leaning district, just like [Example X]. So we’ll win your district too!” Even if the base rate of such upsets is 1% across all districts. |
| **2** | **Appealing to “personal experience” anecdotes** | Promoting anecdotal evidence of a positive or negative outcome, prompting voters to infer that the outcome is common. | “I grew up in a rural community where the new farm‑tax tariff made my family thrive.” Voters may ignore that the majority of the population lived *not* in that rural setting. |
| **3** | **Utilizing “extreme” but “unique” cases** | Discussing a severe case (“Teen left home to join a war‑zone”) to generalize about driving safety or immigration, ignoring that the base rate for that behavior is minuscule. | “When a 17‑year‑old in our state chose to live overseas, the result was devastating. ### How Each Example Plays on the Fallacy

1. **Rare success stories**: People remember one impressive win and assume it reflects the norm. Politicians cherry‑pick a favorable case and ignore how often politicians actually lose.
2. **Personal experience anecdotes**: Voters often over‑generalize from a single “I” story. Politicians supply a compelling narrative, letting the “base rate” of that narrative’s commonality get lost.
3. **Extreme cases**: The vividness of a dramatic example biases judgment, masking that such examples are statistically insignificant.
4. **Reframing rare events**: A tiny number of alarm bells can be spun into a large risk; the size of the base population is hidden.
5. **Specific vs. generic stats**: Small sample statistics are presented as definitive, hiding that larger, more representative data contradicts the claim.

## Practical Takeaway

- **Check the base rate**: Ask, “What is the overall prevalence of this phenomenon?” before believing a single anecdote or case.
- **Seek broad data**: Look for larger, representative studies rather than isolated statistics.
- **Recognize the emotional trigger**: Vivid, specific stories often override statistical reality—this is the sweet spot for political persuasion.

By understanding the base‑rate fallacy, you can spot when a campaign is leveraging a psychological shortcut and make a more informed evaluation of the claims being presented.
