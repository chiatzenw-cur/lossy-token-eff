> When the speed of a satellite that is already in a circular orbit is lowered, the satellite can no longer stay on the circular orbit.  
> The gravitational attraction becomes larger than the centripetal force required at the instant the speed has been reduced, so the satellite is pulled inward. The orbit becomes an ellipse whose perigee (the point of the orbit where the speed reduction occurred) is the same distance from the Earth as the former circular orbit but the apogee is now at a smaller radius.  
> Because the satellite loses kinetic energy, the semi‑major axis of the new ellipse is smaller than the old circular orbit radius:  
>   
> \[
> E_{\text{new}}=\frac{v_{\text{new}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r_{0}}
> \;<\;
> E_{\text{old}}=\frac{v_{\text{circ}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r_{0}}
> =-\frac{\mu}{2r_{0}}
> \]
> and, for an ellipse, \(E=-\mu/(2a)\). Hence \(a_{\text{new}}\!<\!r_{0}\).   
> From Kepler’s third law  
> \[
> T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{\mu}}
> \]
> the period associated with the new orbit is therefore **smaller** than the original period.  
> Consequently, the orbital radius decreases (the satellite spirals inward) and its period of revolution shortens.
