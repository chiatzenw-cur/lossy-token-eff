## 2.  The Classic Causal Chain

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal/Monetary policy** | **Aggregate Demand (AD)** | **Inflation** | **Output & Unemployment** |
| **Fiscal stimulus → higher AD** | **Higher AD → ↑ real GDP (↑ wages / hiring)** | **↑ wages & spending → higher CPI** | **↓ unemployment (Okun’s law)** |

The “classic” chain follows **ex‑ante** assumption that the economy operates below or near capacity:

1. **Fiscal stimulus** (or monetary easing) creates demand for goods and services.
2. Firms respond by hiring, raising wages, and expanding production.
3. Rising wages and higher sales feed a higher price level (inflation), unless the economy is at full capacity.
4. The workforce shrinks, low unemployment follows.

However, the real world has multiple mechanisms at work:

| Mechanism | How it alters the link |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Supply side constraints** (tight labor markets, bottlenecks, raw‑material scarcities) | Even a modest AD boost can push inflation higher before output lags. |
| **Expectations** | If businesses expect “inflationary” pricing, they pre‑emptively raise prices. |
| **Monetary credibility** | If the central bank’s commitment to a target is weak, inflation expectations swell, and the trade‑off costs rise. |
| **Currency effect** | A lower domestic interest rate weakens the exchange rate, boosting import‑price inflation. |

## 3.  Theoretical Backbones

| Theory | Key Insight | Uses |
|--------|-------------|------|
| **IS–LM** (20th‑century macro) | Fiscal‑policy shifts IS; monetary‑policy shifts LM. | Explains how fiscal or monetary tightening is the trade‑off between growth and inflation. |
| **AD‑AS Model** | The average‑price‑level intersection of AD and AS determines output and inflation. | Shows why the same AD shift can lead to a *both* rise in output & price (if AS is rigid) or *only* inflation (if AD hits the economy’s capacity). |
| **Phillips Curve (inverted)** | Short‑run trade‑off: ↓ unemployment → ↑ inflation. | Basis for many policy prescriptions and forecasts. |
| **Okun’s Law** | ΔUnemployment ≈ -0.5 % × ΔGDP (or the output gap). | Gives a numerically handy rule-of-thumb for how much employment swings when GDP moves. |
| **Expectations‑augmented Phillips** | Real inflation = sup‑price changes + expectations. | Emphasises the role of forward‑looking expectations. |
| **Real‑Business‑Cycle (RBC)** | Output and unemployment are driven by technology and real shocks, not monetary. | Highlights why policy that only changes nominal variables (inflation) may not move output permanently. |

## 4.  Fiscal Policy: What It Does, Where It’s Effective

| Policy instrument |  How it changes aggregate demand |  Typical Stimulus Channel |  Impact on GDP |  Impact on Inflation |  Impact on Unemployment |  Caveats |
|-------------------|----------------------------------|---------------------------|----------------|-----------------------|--------------------------|----------|
| **Tax cuts** (income, corporate) | ↑ Disposable income → ↑ consumption | Consumption spending; business tax cuts → ↑ investment | ↑ | ↑ (if AD large) | ↓ | May be diluted by “tax‑killing” if savings rise; low marginal propensity to consume => smaller multiplier |
| **Transfer payments** (Welfare, SNAP) | ↑ spending of low‑income households | Direct consumption, less offset by savings | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ | Can create inflation if the economy is near capacity |
| **Public investment** (infrastructure, R&D) | Raises potential output (shift AS right) & immediate AD | Direct demand + supply‑side Augmentation | ↑ (short‑run + long‑run) | ↑ | ↓ | Requires that projects are well‑timed, avoid crowding out private app |
| **Debt‑financing** | ↑ Government spending bankroll; can crowd out private credit if banks’ reserves are tight | ↑ Construction & salaries | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ | Fiscal multiplier can shrink once debt‑service costs rise; risk of future inflation if debt is monetised by the bank |

### 4.1  Fiscal Multipliers

* **Multiplayer > 1** (under a slack economy) → one‑unit increase in AD increases GDP by >1 unit.
* **Multiplayer < 1** (augmented/return‑fiscal, high forages) → a *crowding‑out* effect or under‑spending.

**Examples**

* The US 2008 stimulus had an estimated multiplier of 0.7–1.1 in the short run; a 1‑st‑class stimulus would have >1.5 in the 2020s with high slack.
* The early 2000s Chinese “2002 stimulus” produced 0.5–1 multipliers owing to significant unutilised capacity and large input multiplier.

## 5.  Monetary Policy: “Driving the Wheel”

| Tool | How it moves AD | Impact on GDP | Impact on Inflation | Impact on Unemployment | Multiple or single‑target approach |
|------|-----------------|--------------|---------------------|------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Interest rate** (policy rate / Fed funds) | Lower rates → cheaper borrowing for firms & consumers → increase spending/ investment | ↑ (convex effect) | ↑ (inflationary pressure) | ↓ | Wider net hope |
| **Quantitative easing (QE)** | Increases money supply, lowers longer‑term rates (tells the bond market) | ↑ (financial‑market‑channel effect) | ↑ (potential) | ↓ | Indirect channel; often required for large stimulus |
| **Forward guidance** | Sets expectations about future rates → pre‑emptive costs in markets → shift AD forward | ↑ | ↑ (via expectation) | ↓ | Highly dependent on credibility |
| **Discount/credit‑facilities** | Lowers banks’ cost to lend → more credit pools | ↑ | ↓ or ↑ (depends on utilisation) | ↓ | Penetrates after decades of regulator tightening |

### 5.1  Canal Mechanisms

1. **Expectations** – Discount rate signalling *future* inflation expectations influences investment decisions.
2. **Portfolio‑balance** – Lower rates reduce the return on bonds, pushing investors into riskier assets, boosting household wealth and consumption.
3. **Price‑level** – Lower rate lowers the “cost” of borrowing -> households are more willing to pay higher prices.
4. **Wage‑price spiral** – Central bank action can influence the wage set in a labor‑market rift, causing a price rise.

**Policy Food‑For‑thought**

* *Target‑rate rule” (e.g., Taylor‑rule) → systematic policy that balances inflation & output gaps.
* *Rate‑pavement” – A gradual shift of policy rates.
* *Zero‑lower bound/negative‑interest‑rate scenarios* – When rates are already near 0, fiscal remains the most potent tool.

## 6.  The Dual‑Mandates in Play

| Target | Goal | Sign of Policy | Polycial effect |
|--------|------|----------------|----------------|
| **Inflation** | 2–3 % | Low → lower rates; High → higher rates | Acceptable short‑run loss in growth | 
| **Employment** | “Full‑employment” (natural unemployment) | Low → lower rates | May cause inflation if too far from potential |

The migration of these two extremes reveals how policy makers must balance **“output gap”** and *expected inflation*.

### 6.1  The Phillips Curve Revisited

| 1998 True Phillips Curve | 2020 *Modern Adaptation* | 2028–2030 Outlook |
|-------------------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Inflation unanchored by expectations (often <0.5 % shift) | Expectations become the major factor; Bayesian forecasts | Expectation‑driven, better smoothing; potential *breakdown* if markets overshoot (i.e., high inflation expectation spurs inflation even when GDP is low) |
| **Key lesson** | Use a *flexible* inflation target, not just a *fixed* inflation number. | Use *forward guidance* and *currency‑based* expectations to maintain credibility. |

## 7.  Concrete Examples (Case Studies)

| Period | Policy Move | Macro Outcome | Key Learnings |
|--------|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| **1997‑2000 US** | Aggressive fiscal & monetary easing (Federal Reserve 3.5 % ↔ 5 % OC). | GDP growth ~3 %, inflation ~2.5 % (peak), Unemployment ~4 %. | The expansionary clamp‑down proved a *balanced* approach. |
| **1970s “stagflation”** | Persistent cash‑rights & loose monetary policy. | Inflation peaked at ~13 % with unemployment ~6–8 %. | Supply shocks (oil) amplified demand‑side inflation; low‑rate policy exacerbated. |
| **2008‑2009 crisis** | Massive fiscal stimulus ($1.9 trill+ USA) + QE (3‑stage). | GDP fell 2–3 % (short‑run), unemployment surged 10 %; inflation suppressed below 2 % in US. | *Debt‑financing* and *market‑channel* QE restored the AD curve but kept inflation below target. |
| **2020‑21 COVID** | Fiscal “rescue programs,” global QE. | GDP fell 3–4 %, unemployment peaked 20 % (US). Inflation → 2–3 % 2022, then above 8 % 2023 volatility. | Builds intuition that **policy excess** can be undone by *unstuck expectations* (e.g., supply‑chain disruptions). |
| **2024‑2025** | Fiscal austerity bail‑in in **europe** (EU + UK). | Inflation cumulative reduces from 5–7 % to 2–3 %; GDP contracts by 1–2 % | Rate increases with recession: “Inflation‑control” tends to hurt growth, but may not *kill* it if the economy is over‑adjusted. |

## 8.  The Big Picture – Fiscal vs. Monetary

| Issue | Fiscal | Monetary |
|-------|--------|----------|
| **Magnitude** | Very large (in percent of GDP) | Relatively small (ajust 0.25–0.5 pp) unless QE scopes huge |
| **Speed** | Lag (months to year) | Fast (days to weeks) |
| **Long‑run capacity** | Expands potential output via infrastructure | Expands potential output via “cheap financing” (ideally). |
| **Impacts on “real” vs. “nominal”** | Directly shifts wages, investment, and relative supply | Mainly via expectations; effect on real variables is a *reaction*/3‑month lag |
| **Coordination** | Best when used jointly (tax cuts + deficit‑spending) | Monetary policy must be considered in the context of the fiscal stance (e.g., high debt may impede rate‑puzzles) |

Rule of thumb: **In a slack economy, fiscal policy is your “credence” multiplier, whereas monetary policy is a bolt‑on “price” toggle**. In a sluggish but not over‑inflated economy, fiscal stimulates marginal *output*, while monetary tries to lock *inflation* in safe territory.

## 10.  Final Take‑Away

| Insight | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **GDP ↔ Inflation ↔ Unemployment** | In a cyclical downturn, a fiscal‑ or monetary‑stimulus raises GDP; unless the economy is overshooting capacity, unemployment falls but inflation may rise later. |
| **Policy must balance** | In a recession with low inflation, the priority is *output*.  In a boom with high inflation, the priority is *price stability*. |
| **Credibility matters** | Inflation expectations can be a *best‑guess alone* plus a *policy cue*: the more credible the policy, the less the price‑inflation side‑effect. |
| **Multipliers** | The size of the fiscal or monetary multiplier depends on the amount of slack, the structure of the economy, and the efficacy of channels. |
| **Combo‑policy** | When fiscal and monetary policy coordinate (e.g., fiscal stimulus + “loose” rate), the net effect is a stronger GDP boost, but it tends to *force* inflation unless an *adjective* infrastructure lift is included. |
| **Expectations shuffle** | Wrongly sized or mis‑timed policy can create a lingering *expectation spill‑over* that fosters persistent inflation (or under‑employment).
