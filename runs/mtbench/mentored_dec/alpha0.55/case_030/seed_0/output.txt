### Applying the Principles: A Mini‑Case Study

Suppose the argument reads:

> “Installing smart tablets in every classroom will reduce the dropout rate in rural schools. Current research shows students who have internet access score 10 % higher on exams.”

|-----------|--------------------------|-------|
| 1. Clarity | Clear claim: “tablet installation reduces dropout.” But the premise (“students with internet score higher”) is vague; not all students with internet access have tablets. | Need to refine: “students who use tablets in labs” |
| 2. Logic | The argument jumps from internet scores to dropout rates. Missing link: *why exam scores determine dropout*. | Add a mediation premise: “Higher scores improve confidence, leading to lower dropout.” |
| 3. Evidence | The study is cited but no authors or year shown. Also no details on methodology. | Provide citation, ensure dataset matches rural context. |
| 4. Counter‑Arguments | No discussion of cost‑access, teacher training, or technology fatigue. | Add a brief counter‑argument section. |
| 5. Relevance | Relevant to rural dropout policy. Purpose clear. Ethics: potential bias toward tech. | Acknowledge that tech may widen digital divides. |

> **Verdict**: The argument is *not fully rigorous*; it needs clearer premises, stronger evidence, and a discussion of counter‑arguments.

### How to Use These Principles in Your Writing

1. **Identify the Claim & Premises** – Write out the argument as a sentence diagram or outline.
2. **Check for Logical Flow** – Use arrows or a flowchart to show how each premise leads to the conclusion.
3. **Audit the Evidence** – List source names, publication dates, and sample sizes; question whether the data truly support the claim.
4. **Probe Counter‑Arguments** – List at least one plausible objection; then show how your argument addresses or refutes it.
5. **Reflect on Purpose & Ethics** – Re‑read the thesis in light of the task prompt and ethical norms.  Be honest about limitations or potential biases.

When you systematically evaluate arguments against these five principles, you can generate essays that are **thoughtful, credible, and persuasive**—exactly what analytical writing demands.
