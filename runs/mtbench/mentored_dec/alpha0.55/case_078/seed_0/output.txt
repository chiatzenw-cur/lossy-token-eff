**Definition**  
The base‑rate fallacy (also called base‑rate neglect or base‑rate bias) occurs when we ignore or give very little weight to the *a priori* (prior) probability of an event when making a judgment, and instead focus on additional or sensational information.  
In other words, we over‑weight the most striking data point and under‑weight the statistical “background” that tells us how common the event really is.

**Illustrated with a simple example**

| Situation | Prior probability (base‑rate) | Additional information | Neglecting base‑rate → Result |
|-----------|------------------------------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| A telephone call asks if you know *John Doe* (a male, from a neighborhood with 10% of the population that could be John). | 10% | A rare marker: “John Doe has a tattoo of a dragon.” | Because the tattoo is uncommon you might think “Yes, that’s our John Doe.” |
| The actual chance that a random call refers to *John* is the base‑rate 10%, but the observation that the caller mentions a dragon‑tattoo **does not dominate** the decision. The base‑rate should stay central in the assessment. |

**Why it matters**  
- Our intuition favors “story” or “signal” over the blunt fact that the event occurs roughly *X %* of the time.
- In many policy discussions the random “base‑rate” of an event is low, but the story or evidence can create a sense that the event is much more common and threatening.

## 2. Politicians’ “Base‑Rate‑Free” Playbook  
Below are **five concrete, historically‑documented cases** in which politicians consciously ignore the base‑rate, focusing instead on a handful of potent anecdotes or statistics that get stuck in the public mind. These are not merely accidental; they are often engineered into campaign messages, speeches, and media visits.

| # | Issue | How the Base‑Rate is Ignored | Consequence in Campaign |
|---|-------|-----------------------------|------------------------|
| **1** | **Gun Violence & Mass Shootings** | • Politicians highlight a single mass shooting (e.g., the Sandy Hook, Parkland) that occurs *once every year or so.* <br>• They present the headline “**X** people killed in a gun‑related incident.” <br>• They ignore the **overall statistics** that gun homicide rates are around 6 people per 100 k in the U.S. (≈ 4% of all violent crime), or that the *chance* of an individual American being killed by a gun in their lifetime is < 1 in 4,000. | The public feels that guns are **saturated with danger**, which pushes support for stricter gun controls, even when the everyday risk is low. |
| **2** | **Immigration & Crime** | • Smoothed down *overall immigrant crime statistics* (e.g., less than 1% of violent crime is committed by a foreign‑born person). <br>• Politicians present a single “golden‑rule” interview: “**A migrant once robbed a bank on the way to a shelter**.” <br>• This anecdote is framed as *representative* and is repeated until the “rookie immigrant” image becomes a default. | The base‑rate saying *“immigrants are less likely to commit crime”* is erased, leading to calls for restrictive border policies and “anti‑immigrant” laws. |
| **3** | **Vaccines & Adverse Events** | • A handful of cases of autism or developmental delay are publicised in a “**vaccines caused autism**” story. <br>• The massive base‑rate data (e.g., the baseline autism prevalence is ~ 1‑2 % of children, while the risk is not elevated after vaccines) is either not mentioned or glossed over. | A false “risk” narrative swells public fear, allowing politicians to push for “more safety”—i.e., argument for mandatory vaccination mandates, even when data show no causal link. |
| **4** | **War & Terrorist Threats** | • After a terrorist incident (e.g., 9 / 11, 2015 Paris attacks), politicians may label the region as “**toxic**” and risk a “war” in every *major* city. <br>• They never refer to the *overall statistical probability* that a random citizen in Europe will be attacked by a terrorist—usually < 1 per 8 million. | The *marginal base‑rate* of terror is hidden; the policy to increase security spending, airport pre‑checks, or restrict NGO funding is justified. |
| **5** | **Education & School‑Closed Runs** | • A teacher’s “safety report” lists a “5‑year kindergarten child got hurt after an incident." <br>• Politicians post this on social media, framing it as “**our children are unsafe**.” <br>• The base‑rate indicates that only about 1 in 15,000 students details a serious injury in a school. | This narrative inflates the perceived danger, leading to campaigns for stricter safety regulations, mandated external inspectors, and larger funding allocations. |

## 3. A Quick “How‑to” on Recognizing Base‑Rate‑Free Propaganda

1. **Look for the *most dramatic* statistic** – it often has a single source (a single incident, a dramatic headline, or an “explosive” new report).  
2. **Ask for the *overall* context** – recall the usual “baseline” or “population rate” behind the statistic.  
3. **Check the *source* of the dramatic statistic** – are they a peer‑reviewed paper, a single community bulletin, a press release, or an anecdote?  
4. **Re‑calculate the probability** – plug the fraction of total events into the base‑rate, re‑apply Bayes’ theorem if needed.  
5. **Seek out the *large‑scale* studies** – e.g., the CDC or WHO report that characterizes the *long‑run* risk of a public health issue.

## 4. Bottom line

- **Base‑rate fallacy** is the mental trap of chasing the story and ignoring the underlying frequency.  
- **Politicians use it in campaigns** to dramatise risk, pitch policies for “safety” or “security,” and shift public opinion toward thinly‑argued regulatory mandates.  
- **Awareness** of both the fallacy and the specific tactics gives voters a sharper tool to analyze political messaging.  

By always asking, *“How common is this event in the overall population?”*, we can better see through the rhetoric and keep the base‑rate front‑and‑center in our judgments.
