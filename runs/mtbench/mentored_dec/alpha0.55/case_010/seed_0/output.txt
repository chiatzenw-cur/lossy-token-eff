In quantum mechanics a system’s state is described by a vector \(|\psi\rangle\) in a complex Hilbert space.  
Because this space is *linear*, any linear combination of allowed states is also a valid state.  
For a single qubit (the simplest quantum system) a convenient basis is

\[
|0\rangle \quad\text{and}\quad |1\rangle
\]

and an arbitrary state is

\[
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle,
\qquad |\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1 .
\]

The coefficients \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are *amplitudes*.  
The “superposition” terminology comes from the fact that the system is **simultaneously** in all the basis states that appear in the sum, weighted by their amplitudes.  
When you *measure* the qubit the wavefunction “collapses” to one of the basis states, with probabilities \(|\alpha|^{2}\) and \(|\beta|^{2}\).

---

**Entanglement as a special form of superposition**

Entanglement isn’t a separate principle; it’s a natural consequence of the same linearity that gives superposition in single‑particle systems.  
When two or more quantum systems are considered together, the combined Hilbert space is the *tensor product* of the individual spaces:

\[
\mathcal{H}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes \mathcal{H}_{B}.
\]

A *product* state looks like

\[
|\psi\rangle_{\text{prod}} = |\phi\rangle_{A}\otimes |\chi\rangle_{B}.
\]

But the total space contains many *non‑factorizable* states that cannot be written as a simple product.  These are the *entangled* states.  They are, by definition, superpositions of product states:

\[
|\Psi\rangle_{\text{entangled}}
 = \sum_{i,j} c_{ij}\,|i\rangle_{A}\otimes|j\rangle_{B}
\quad\text{with no factorisation}\;c_{ij}=a_i\,b_j.
\]

### Classic example

The singlet (EPR) pair of two spin‑½ particles:

\[
|\Psi^{-}\rangle
 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl(|\uparrow\rangle_{A}\,|\downarrow\rangle_{B}
 - |\downarrow\rangle_{A}\,|\uparrow\rangle_{B}\Bigr).
\]

It is a *superposition* of the two product states  
\(|\uparrow\rangle_{A}\,|\downarrow\rangle_{B}\) and  
\(|\downarrow\rangle_{A}\,|\uparrow\rangle_{B}\).  
Because the two terms are added with a relative minus sign, the state cannot be separated into a single state for \(A\) and another for \(B\).  That is the essence of entanglement.

### What entanglement gives us

1. **Correlations that cannot be explained classically**  
   Measuring the spin of particle \(A\) instantly determines the spin of particle \(B\) (the opposite, in this case), even if the two are light‑years apart.
2. **Quantum interference between the joint terms**  
   If you later perform a joint measurement that mixes the two product states, the relative phase of the superposition determines the outcome probability.  
   This is the basis for quantum information protocols such as teleportation and quantum key distribution.

| Concept | One‑particle state | Two‑particle (non‑entangled) state | Two‑particle (entangled) state |
|---------|--------------------|-----------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| *Superposition* | Linear combo of basis states | Same, but for each particle independently | **The definition**: a superposition of *pair* states |
| *Correlations* | None (only one particle) | Only classical (independent statistics) | Quantum correlations = signature of entanglement |

### Bottom line

*Superposition* is the rule that any linear combination of allowed states is again a valid state.  
*Entanglement* is the phenomenon that arises when two or more systems are put together in a *coherent* superposition of product states.  It is a deeper, multi‑particle manifestation of the same underlying linearity that underlies all quantum superpositions.  Without the superposition principle, the extraordinary correlations of entangled systems would simply not exist.
