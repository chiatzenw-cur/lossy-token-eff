1. **Solubility** – Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) is a largely insoluble solid.  
2. **Acid attack** – Hydrochloric acid (HCl, aqueous) supplies protons (H⁺).  
3. **Dissolution + decomposition** – The H⁺ ions attack the carbonate, dissolving the solid and concurrently breaking the carbonate ion into carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water (H₂O).  
4. **Product formation** – The resulting calcium ions (Ca²⁺) are captured by the chloride ions (Cl⁻) to give soluble calcium chloride (CaCl₂) in aqueous solution.

---

### Balanced Chemical Equation

\[
\boxed{\text{CaCO}_{3(s)} \;+\; 2\,\text{HCl}_{(aq)} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{CaCl}_{2(aq)} \;+\; \text{CO}_{2(g)} \;+\; \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)}}
\]

- **“s”** – solid,  
- **“(aq)”** – dissolved in water,  
- **“(g)”** – gas.

---

### Type of Reaction

- **Acid‑base (neutralisation) reaction**: HCl (acid) neutralises the carbonate (base) to produce salt (CaCl₂) and water.  
- **Decomposition**: The carbonate ion is decomposed into CO₂ and OH⁻ (which combines with H⁺ to form water).  
- **Double‑displacement** (metathesis) step: Ca²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ ↔ CaCl₂.

---

### Observations that Indicate the Reaction is Occurring

| Observation | Why it Occurs |
|-------------|----------------|
| **Effervescence (bubbling)** | CO₂ gas is liberated from the solution; bubbles form and rise. |
| **Fizzing** | The steady popping of bubbles is typical of a gas‑evolving reaction. |
| **Colorless, clear solution** | No precipitate forms (CaCl₂ is soluble), so the liquid remains clear. |
| **Heat release** | The reaction is mildly exothermic; the surrounding surface and hands can feel slightly warm. |
| **Change of volume** | Some volume of liquid is lost as CO₂ gas escapes, often measurable in a controlled setup. |

### Practical Demonstration (for a laboratory or educational setting)

1. **Set‑up**: Place a small amount of solid CaCO₃ in a beaker, cover with a piece of pH‑sliding glass or a syringe with a gas‑collecting inverted funnel.  
2. **Add acid**: Slowly pour 2 × the molar amount of HCl (10 % solution) over the CaCO₃.  
3. **Observe**: Immediately see vigorous fizzing, rise of bubbles, and the liquid turning clear as CaCl₂ dissolves.  
4. **Optional**: Collect the released CO₂ in a water‑displaced gas‑collection apparatus to confirm CO₂ by its faint acidity and smell.  

This “fizzing” reaction is the textbook illustration of an acid reacting with a carbonate to form a salt, water, and carbon dioxide.
