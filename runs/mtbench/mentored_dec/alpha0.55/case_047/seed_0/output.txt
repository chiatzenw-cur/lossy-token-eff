### How we *distinguish* the two

1. **Calorimetry or thermometry**  
   - Place the reaction in a calorimeter; if the temperature rises, the reaction is exothermic (and ΔH < 0).  
   - If the temperature drops, the reaction is endothermic (ΔH > 0).

2. **ΔH or ΔU calculations**  
   - Use stoichiometry and bond energies:  
     \[
     \Delta H = \sum \text{(bonds broken)} - \sum \text{(bonds formed)}
     \]
     A negative ΔH means the energy of bonds formed is greater than that of bonds broken – exothermic.

3. **Observational criteria**  
   - Warmth felt from the reaction vessel or dissipation of heat into the surroundings → exothermic.  
   - Cold spots or requirement of cooling to keep the reaction at a constant temperature → endothermic.

4. **Energy balance**  
   - For a closed system:  
     \[
     Q = \Delta H \quad(\text{at constant pressure})
     \]
     If \(Q>0\) (heat absorbed by the system from the surroundings), the reaction is **endothermic**.  
     If \(Q<0\) (heat released), the reaction is **exothermic**.

*(Note: In a constant‑volume calorimeter, the energy change is ΔU, but the sign logic is identical.)*

## Real‑world example: The burning of a candle vs. photosynthesis

| Process | Reaction | ΔH | Energy flow | Sensory cue |
|---------|----------|---|-------------|-------------|
| **Candle burn** | \(\text{C}_\text{(s)} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat}\) | \(-\)17 kJ mol⁻¹ | Heat *leaves* the flame → the room feels warm | Warm glow, heatwave |
| **Photosynthesis** | \(\text{photosynthetic pigments} \xrightarrow{light}\ \text{glucose} + \text{O}_2\) | \(+472\) kJ mol⁻¹ (for every 6 CO₂→1 glucose) | Light energy *absorbed* → plant feels cool | Green leaves appear cool, dew‑drops form |

*Why the difference?*  
- In the candle, the carbon is oxidized to CO₂ and H₂O; the new bonds released contain more energy than what was required to break the bonds of the reactants. The excess energy shows up as heat.  
- In photosynthesis, the reactants (CO₂, H₂O, and pigment molecules) are pushed to a higher‑energy state (glucose). The process requires the energy supplied by photons, so the plant “takes in” heat from the environment and ends up cooler, which is why freshly bloomed flowers feel cool to touch.

### Key Take‑aways

1. **Heat direction matters** – into the system → endothermic; out of the system → exothermic.  
2. **ΔH sign** is the sign convention that formalises the direction of heat flow.  
3. **Thermometric observation** gives an immediate, intuitive sense of which type of reaction is happening.  
4. **Real‑world analogues** often involve everyday sensations: a warm stove vs. a cold, freshly‑bleached shirt.

Feel free to try a simple classroom experiment: heat a known quantity of copper sulfate on a metal strip and observe the temperature rise—an exothermic demonstration of lattice energy changes. Or, dissolve ammonium nitrate in water and note the drop in temperature—an endothermic case of ice cooling your hands.
