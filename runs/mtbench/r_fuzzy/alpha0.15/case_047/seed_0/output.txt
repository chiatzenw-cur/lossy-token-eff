In chemistry the **enthalpy change (ΔH)** of a reaction tells us whether the system gives the surrounding medium heat or takes it in.  
* **Exothermic** → ΔH < – 0 (the system releases heat).  
* **In endothermic** → ΔH > + 0 (the system absorbs heat).

---

### 2. The criteria I used to distinguish them

| Criterion | Exothermic reaction | Endothermic reaction |
|-----------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| **Sign of ΔH** | Negative (ΔH < 0) | Positive (ΔH > 0) |
| **Thermal flow** | Heat flows **out** of the reaction mixture | Heat flows **into** the reaction mixture |
| **Temperature of surroundings** | Usually rises (if the heat is not removed) | Usually falls (if the heat is not supplied) |
| **Product stability** | Products are at lower enthalpy than reactants; a homologous “bond‐forming” step happens | Products are at higher enthalpy; a “bond‑breaking” or rearrangement step dominates |
| **Equilibrium consideration** | ΔG° = ΔH° – TΔG° = negative (often), so the reaction is thermodynamically favorable | ΔG° positive unless compensated by a gain in entropy (ΔS) or an applied external energy source (e.g., light) |
| **Typical experimental indicator** | A hot flame or a noticeably warm beaker | A cold patch or an obvious dilution of heat sink (e.g., an ice cube melting faster) |

The **most direct** approach is to look at the measured heat flow: if calorimetry tells us that the surroundings lose 30 kJ per mole of reaction, the system is exothermic. If it loses 15 kJ, the system is endothermic.

### 3. A real‑world pair of examples

| Reaction | Type | ΔH (approx. per mole of reacts) | Physical observation | What is happening? |
|---------|------|----------------------------------|----------------------|--------------------|
| **Combustion of methane**: \(\displaystyle \ce{CH4(g) + 4 O2(g) -> CO2(g) + H2O(l)}\) | Exothermic | ΔHₙ ≈ –802 kJ mol⁻¹ | The flame is bright and the reaction chamber rises in temperature; a glass of water on the rack turns hot. | High atom‑bond re‑formation in CO2, H2O; freeing large amounts of stored energy. |
| **Dissolution of ammonium nitrate**: \(\displaystyle \text{NH4NO3(s)} \rightleftharpoons \text{NH4}⁺(aq)+\text{NO3}⁻(aq)\) | Endothermic | ΔH ≈ +26 kJ mol⁻¹ | The beaker becomes noticeably cool to the touch; an ice cube placed nearby melts faster. | The crystal lattice breaks; water molecules must take up salts’ hydration energy, consuming heat from the surroundings. |
| **Photosynthesis**: \(\displaystyle 6 \text{CO}_2(g)+12 \text{H}_2O(l)+\text{light} \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6(s)+6 \text{O}_2(g)\) | Endothermic (when light is included) | ΔH ≈ +2800 kJ mol⁻¹ (per mole of glucose) | Plants absorb sunlight; the process is slow in darkness and stops.

### 4. Putting it together

1. **Measure** or **look up** the reaction enthalpy, ΔH.  
2. **Check** the sign: negative → exothermic, positive → endothermic.  
3. **Observe** the surroundings: rising temperature → exothermic; falling temperature → endothermic.  
4. Any extra context (e.g., a *aura of heat* in a day‑time heater or a filter cooling separately, an activation barrier that must be overcome) confirms the direction.

In everyday life, **combustion** (exothermic) powers engines, heaters, and fireworks, while **dissolving cold packs** or **electrolytic processes** harness endothermic reactions for cooling and industrial energy input. Understanding whether a reaction provides or consumes heat is essential for designing safety protocols, energy balances, and even predicting how organisms harness or dissipate heat in living systems.
