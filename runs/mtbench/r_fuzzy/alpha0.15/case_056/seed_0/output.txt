### 1. What Is Probability?

**Think of it as a “likelihood machine”**.  
- If you shout “It’s raining,” probability is the chance you’ll be right, *based on evidence*.  
- In math, the “machinery” is the set of **possible outcomes** and the **ways** they can appear.

> **Short definition:**  
> The probability that an event \(E\) happens given a set of possible outcomes \(S\) is  
> \[
> P(E)=\frac{\text{Number of ways } E\text{ can happen}}{\#\text{ of all possibilities}}.
> \]

---

### 2. The Basic Building Blocks

| Term | Everyday picture | Mathematical form |
|------|------------------|-------------------|
| **Sample space (S)** | Every possible outcome of the “experiment.” | \(S\) |
| **Events** | Subset of S where the event we care is true. | \(S_E \subseteq S\) |
| **Probability of an event** | “How often do we see this event?” | \(P(E)=\frac{|S_E|}{|S|}\) |
| **Complement** | The “not‑E” outcomes. | \(\overline{E} = S \setminus S_E\) |

**Example:**  
Throw a fair six‑card die (card numbers 1‑6).  
- Sample space: \(S = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\)  
- Event “Rolling an even number” \(E = \{1,3,5\}\) (oops! actually even are 2,4,6 – good catch!)  

Let’s correct:  
\(E = \{2,4,6\}\) → 5 possible outcomes total, 3 even numbers →  
\(P(\text{even}) = \frac{3}{6}\)=0.5.

### 3. Classical vs. Scientific Probability

| Classical | Scientific/Frequentist |
|-----------|------------------------|
| “Equal chances” for standard toys: coin, dice, cards. | Uses long‑run frequency or statistical inference. |
| Example: Rope with 1 red and 1 blue bead → \(P(\text{positively colored})=1/2\). | Used by epidemiologists estimating disease prevalence. |

When you have numbers you've *counted*, classical probability is the right tool.

### 4. Step‑by‑Step Example: “Choose a Card from a Deck”

**Classic deck: 52 cards, 4 suits, 13 ranks.**  
1. **Goal:** Find \(P(\text{draw a Queen})\).  
2. **Count queens:** There are 4 queens.  
3. **Apply formula:**  
   \[
   P(\text{Queen}) = \frac{\text{# of queens}}{\text{total cards}} = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}\approx 0.077.
   \]
4. **Interpretation:** You’d expect a queen in ≈7.7 % of draws.

**Adding a Twist: “What’s the probability of drawing a Queen *and* OTHERS?”**  
- Use **multiplication rule** (discussed below).

### 5. The Three Basic Rules

#### (i) Addition Rule (Union of Disjoint Events)
If two events \(A\) and \(B\) can never happen together (they’re disjoint),  
\[
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B).
\]
*Example:*  
Probability of rolling **1 or 5** on a die:  

\(P(1)=1/6,\;P(5)=1/6\) →  
\[
P(1\ \text{or}\ 5)=\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{1}{3}.
\]

#### (ii) Multiplication Rule
For two events that may both occur (independent trials), the probability that *both* happen is \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B)\).

*Example:*  
Throw two coins.  
The chance of *both* being heads: \(P(H\cap H)=\frac12 \times \frac12 = \frac14\).

*Dependently* (e.g., drawing two cards *without* replacement), we adjust:
\[
P(1\text{st queen}\ \text{and}\ 2\text{st king}) = \frac{4}{52}\times \frac{4}{51}.
\]

#### (iii) Conditional Probability  
The likelihood of \(B\) *given* that \(A\) has already happened:
\[
P(B|A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}.
\]
*Example (Cards):*  
Pick 2 cards. Probability both are hearts, conditional on the first card being a heart:  

\(P(\text{second heart}\mid\text{first heart}) = \frac{\text{remaining hearts}}{\text{remaining cards}}=\frac{12}{51}\).

### 6. Quick Example: “Weather Forecast”

Suppose a weather website says there’s a 30 % chance of rain today, and a “streak” is that if it rains on Monday you’d rather it rains on Tuesday too.

- **Probability of rain on Tuesday**: Might be \(30\%\) *independent* of Monday.  
- **Conditional probability**:  
  \(P(\text{rain Tuesday} | \text{rain Monday}) = 0.4\).  
  The forecast *updates* when Monday’s rain is confirmed.

> **Tip:** Recognize when events “remember” each other (non‑independence) and use conditional probability.

### 7. What Happens in the Real World?

| Field | Why Probability is essential | Quick‑Check |
|-------|-----------------------------|--------------|
| **Medicine** | Testing for diseases (false positives/negatives). | If a test is 95 % accurate, what’s the patient’s real likelihood? |

### 10. Take‑away

- **Probability is a count** for equally likely outcomes.  
- **Sample space**: list all possibilities.  
- **Event size / contributions**: how many favorable outcomes.  
- **Rules**: *add* for exclusive events, *multiply* for combined, *condition* for “given.”  

Remember: **In probability, numbers are models of our uncertainty.** Once you know how to set up them, you can look at any situation – a coin toss, a health test, a board game—the same rules apply.
