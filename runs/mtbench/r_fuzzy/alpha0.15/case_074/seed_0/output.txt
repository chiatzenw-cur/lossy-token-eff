When a dry piece of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) is poured into an aqueous solution of hydrochloric acid (HCl), the solid dissolves and a vigorous fizzing occurs. The acid “scrubs” the carbonate, producing water, a soluble salt (calcium chloride), and carbon dioxide gas that is released as bubbles. 

**Balanced equation**

\[
\text{CaCO}_3(s) + 2\,\text{HCl}(aq) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{CaCl}_2(aq) + \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)
\]

- **CaCO₃** (solid) + **HCl** (aq) → **CaCl₂** (aqueous) + **CO₂** (gas) + **H₂O** (solvent).  
- No atoms are lost; all are accounted for on both sides.

**Type of reaction**

- Acid–base (neutralization) reaction.  
- It is also a classic “double‑displacement” or “metathesis” reaction, sometimes called a *combination* reaction because the carbonate combines with the bicarbonate of HCl and releases CO₂ and water.

**Observations that indicate the reaction is occurring**

| Observation | What it tells you |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Fizzing / bubbling of gas** | Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is being produced. |
| **Rapid dissolution of solid** | Calcium carbonate is reacting with chloride. |
| **Clear or slightly colored water** after the solid disappears | Calcium chloride remains in solution. |
| **Slight rise in temperature** (often warm) | Exothermic nature of the neutralization. |
| **Decrease in pH** | The solution becomes less acidic after HCl is fully neutralized. |

These signs collectively confirm that CaCO₃ is reacting with HCl to give CaCl₂ and CO₂, completing the conversion to the aqueous salt and gas.
