The **base‑rate fallacy** is a cognitive shortcut (or *bias*) that occurs when people give disproportionate weight to specific information (e.g., anecdotes, vivid details, or “special cases”) and ignore the broader, statistical context (the *base rate*).  
- **Base Rate (or Ground Rate)** – the general or overall prevalence of a characteristic in a population.  
- **Specific Information** – a particular or exceptional case that feels more relevant or memorable.

When politicians – or anyone trying to persuade – highlight a single positive result or an isolated success story but *omit* the much‑background statistics that would dampen the effect, they commit the base‑rate fallacy. For example, a candidate might say “*five small towns in our state saw a 15 % drop in unemployment after we passed this bill*” and thereby imply that everyone will see the same benefit, when nationwide the average drop is more like 1 %.  The audience is misled into thinking that a big part of the population will experience the same outcome.

## Why These Examples Break Down the Fallacy

1. **Anonymity of a minority** – Each case is the *exception* that inversely frames the *norm*.  
2. **Selective Use** – Politicians cherry‑pick data that bolsters their agenda, leaving out the opposing or neutral statistics.  
3. **Emotional Appeal** – A sentence about a single success story travels far faster emotionally than a paragraph of raw statistical tables.  
4. **Absence of Counter‑Evidence** – The audience rarely receives a statistical counter‑argument formatted in a similarly memorable way (though it could be).  
5. **Public Trust** – Even if the story is true, attuned to the base rate, the effect is cognitively stronger than longs-form context that would moderate the claim.  

---

### Bottom Line  
When a politician recounts a *single positive story* and is asked to justify it against the *blunt whole‑population data*, they are inviting a base‑rate fallacy. Being aware of this cognitive bias is essential for anyone consuming or critiquing politicians’ messaging.  

---

**Take‑away:** Look for where a gender, economic class, or geographic group is presented as *representative* while ignoring that the actual population distribution (the base rate) says otherwise. That’s where the **base‑rate fallacy plays its role**.
