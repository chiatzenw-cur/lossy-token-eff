The song‑book principle of modern genetics is that the *information* stored in an organism’s genome flows in an essentially one‑way direction:

```
DNA  →  RNA  →  Protein
```

In other words, DNA contains the coded instructions that are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA), and these instructions are read by the ribosome to assemble a polypeptide chain.

### What "Central Dogma" Means

While most of the genome’s data is expressed via the fol‑lo‑wing route, the *central dogma* does **not** forbid exceptions. The real power of the concept lies in its clarity: it defines the *default flow* of information. Rather than a strict one‑way street, it is a *rule of thumb*—a starting point, not a universal law:

* **Reverse transcription** – Retroviruses (e.g., HIV) use reverse transcriptase (an RNA‑templated DNA polymerase) to produce a DNA copy of their RNA genome.  
  *Exception:* the dogma still holds because the *DNA* produced is still an intermediate that eventually leads to RNA transcripts (but the flow is reversed on purpose).  

* **Non‑coding RNAs** – Many functional RNAs (e.g., rRNA, tRNA, miRNA) do not contribute to proteins but are generated directly from DNA and perform crucial roles.  
* **RNA genomes** – Bacteriophages with RNA genomes (e.g., *MS2*) replicate by *RNA‑dependent RNA polymerase*, bypassing a DNA intermediate.  
* **Protein‐templated nucleic crystal** – The Prion model shows that a misfolded protein can template other proteins, causing disease.  

The central dogma is *always* true, but it is *not* a law that unambiguously forbids all backward copies. It simply states that **sequence information is usually transmitted from DNA to RNA to protein**, starting in the living cell’s genetic material and ending in the proteins that make the cell work.

---

### Who Coined the Term

The phrase **“central dogma”** was coined by Carl François Crick – the physicist‑turned‑biologist dedicated to the structure of DNA and to the genetic code – in his 1958 paper *“On protein synthesis: genetic control of the genetic code”* published in *Nature* (Vol. 201, p. 141). He first used it in the 1950s as an informal rhetorical device aimed at summarizing the “flow of information” that defined the field. It later gained widespread use in educational textbooks, especially after Crick’s book *Molecular Biology of the Gene* (first edition, 1970). He was a Fields Medal‑winning two‑time Nobel laureate (1952) and a key figure in Cambridge’s scientific community.

* **Central Dogma**: Genetic information flows from DNA → RNA → Protein.

* **Exceptions**: Reverse transcription (retroviruses), RNA genomes, non‑coding RNAs, prion phenomena.  
* **Named by**: Francis Crick (1950s/58), not a formal law, but a guiding principle that organizes biology.
