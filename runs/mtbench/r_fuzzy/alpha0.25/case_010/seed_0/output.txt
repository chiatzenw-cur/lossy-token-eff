## 1. Quantum superposition

| **Definition** | In quantum computing, a physical system (electron, photon, atom, etc.) can be described by a **state vector** \(|\psi\rangle\) in a linear (Hilbert) space.  
A core postulate of quantum mechanics is that if \(|\psi_1\rangle\) and \(|\psi_2\rangle\) can occur, then *any* linear combination  

\[
|\psi\rangle = \alpha| \psi_1\rangle + \beta| \psi_2\rangle , \qquad |\alpha| + |\beta| = 1 \ (\text{for classical probabilities})  
\]

is also a legitimate state. |
| **Intuitive picture** | Think of a spinning coin that, while being flipped, is “heads and in‑between tails.” That “and‑yet‑neither” sign is a *superposition* of the classical states. |
| **Mathematical representation** | A qubit is described via a two‑dimensional Hilbert space with computational basis \(\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}\). A general qubit is
\[
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + x|1\rangle
\]
with \(\alpha, x \in \mathbb{C}\). The measurement probabilities are \(|\alpha|^{2}\le 1\) and \(|x|^{2}\le 1\). |
| **When and why it matters** | Superposition allows a system to “try” a whole computational basis simultaneously—fundamental to quantum algorithms and interference. It also underlies the *wave‑particle duality* nature of particles and quantum uncertainty. |

### What happens upon measurement?
When measuring a system in a quantum state, its state **collapses** onto one of the eigenstates of the relevant operator, with probabilities given by the squared amplitudes. After this collapse, information about the other components of the superposition has been lost, but the act of measurement does not disturb the *other* subsystems that existed until the measurement was performed.

## 2. Quantum entanglement

| **Definition** | Entanglement occurs when a composite joint system’s state cannot be written with a *product‑state* representation per joint subsystem. It is *a superposition* of product states, not just the product of individual superpositions. |
|---|---|
| **Example** | Two qubits in the Bell state
\[
|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\,|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B + |1\rangle_B|1\rangle_B \bigr)
\]
cannot be expressed as  
\(\bigl(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\bigl)_A \otimes(\gamma|0\rangle + \delta|1\rangle\bigl)_B\).
All allowable factorizations have to be *bunched* together, i.e. the state is a simple sum of two separable terms. |
| **Key property** | If the joint state of two subsystem states is pure, intermediate density matrices on each subsystem are mixed. The shared correlation collapses when a measurement occurs on one; entanglement is thus “distributed” across *both* subsystems in a *single* universal superposition pattern. |
| **Non‑classical correlations** | The joint probabilities of measurement outcomes of entangled states violate *Bellman inequalities* in comparison to any classical model that would allow each subsystem to be *independent* of the other. |

---

## 3. Why do we think “superposition” and “entanglement” are intertwined?

| **Feature** | **How superposition gives rise to entanglement** |
|---|---|
| **Joint Hilbert space** | Build on a product space \(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\). The linearity \(c_1,z_1 \in \mathcal{A}\) and \(\mathcal{B}\) yields building blocks. |
| **Not a simple product** | Many entangled states are related to a *unitary* basis change of the factorized product distribution but cannot be re‑ordered as the separate parameters of individual systems.|
| **Superposition + correlation** | When you superpose cross‑terms, an act that influences the outcomes of one subsystem by a side‑effect reaching the other (e.g. measuring A collapses the global \(|\Phi^+\rangle\) and instantaneously changes the state of B). |
| **Resource for quantum tasks** | Entanglement’s effectiveness as a resource (teleportation, superdense coding) relies on entangled states that *encode* a superposition of classical key bits across more than one Hilbert space. |

## 4. Concrete examples

| **Quantum system** | **Wave‑function** | **Interpretation** |
|---|---|---|
| **Single qubit** | \( |\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle) \) | Quantum gate performing mixed operations. |
| **Two qubits (Bell)** | \(|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0\rangle_A|1\rangle_B + |1\rangle_B|0\rangle\_B\bigr) \) | An outcome shift on one instantly changes the other’s probability distribution, no classical analogue. |
| **Three qubits (GHZ)** | \(|\mathrm GHZ\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|000\rangle_A|000\rangle_B|000\rangle_C| + |111\rangle_A|111\rangle_B|111\rangle_C) \) | A large‑scale superposition of product states that is not purely entangled by pairwise correlations. |

## 5. How superposition underpins entanglement

1. **Mathematical necessity**: Every entangled state and its dynamics are built from tensor‑valued superposition operations. A product of two superpositions is a *double* superposition.  

2. **Spatial separation**: The state of a composite world is a superposition of the entire product basis. The *fact* that one state acts distant from another caused a *non‑local* interference effect, which in principle can be seen geometrically, given a unitary network that interlocks when one module’s measurement outcome is considered.

3. **Symmetry and group actions**: In Bell states, each subsystem pairs state vector combinations leading to a *coupled* superposition principle. The entangled state is not a “hidden product” of simple superpositions – the superposition is the *entanglement*.

4. **Quantization**: Entanglement supplies *entropy* that can be quantified as the *von Neumann entropy* of the reduced side. This entropy is non‑zero exactly because the joint state is *not* a simple sum of factorised individual states.

---

## 6. Key takeaway

- **Superposition** is a *single* system’s core theory: the ability to maintain both (or many) states simultaneously.  
- **Entanglement** is a *collective* property of multipartite systems where every subsystem’s state is a *superposition* of product states, but the global configuration cannot simultaneously be separated into independent pieces.  

At the heart of the matter, **entanglement is a particular kind of superposition of *separable* sub‑states**. By superposing the unit cells of two or more separated subsystems, entanglement arises; its non‑local aspects are a direct consequence of the superposition principle operating in a composite setting.

So next time you think of a coin *both* flipped and not flipped, imagine it now being a **branching dance** across several farthest‑apart coins, shimmering connecting the heads of one to the heads of another. That's what many‑body superposition is: a real-world manifestation of unthinkable states.
