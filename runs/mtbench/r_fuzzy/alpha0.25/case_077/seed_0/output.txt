Antitrust law is the branch of commercial law that seeks to prevent or punish conduct that restrains trade, reduces competition, or harms consumers. The core principle is that *competition* is not only a tool but a *policy objective* for efficient markets: it leads to lower prices, higher quality and innovation, and a more responsive economy.   

Below is a comparative survey of the **United States (US)** and the **People’s Republic of China** (PRC) antitrust regimes, their historical evolution, and illustrative case studies. We highlight both the *policy doctrines* and the *enforcement of real‑world cases*.

### 1. Antitrust Fundamentals

| Concept | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Consumer Welfare Standard (U.S.)** | The primary focus is on reducing consumer price, quantity, or quality impacts. The Supreme Court decisions **United States v. United States Gaming Corp.** (1980) and **United States v. Auer** (2001) set the doctrine. |
| **Market‑Efficiency Standard (China)** | Similar to U.S. but shifts emphasis on *efficacy*, *reallocation* of resources, and *innovation*. The PRC’s *MCE Law* (1998) introduces **“cumulative effect”** and *“market share”* norms. |
| **Types of Anti‑Competitive Behavior** | 1. *Monopolization* (dominance through exclusionary practices). 2. *Price‑fixing, bid‑rigging*, or *collusion* among competitors. 3. *Merger abuse* that could produce *significant adverse competition* (SCAA). |
| **Enforcement Methods** | 1. **Private**: claimants may litigate against violators. 2. **Government**: federal courts or competition authorities (e.g., FTC, US Department of Justice (DOJ) in the U.S.; China’s State Intellectual Property Office (SIPO) or with *National Market Supervision Administration* (NMSA) in China). |
