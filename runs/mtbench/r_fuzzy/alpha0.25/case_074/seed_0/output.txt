You are reacting a solid salt (calcium carbonate) with an aqueous acid.  
Two of the hydrogen ions (`H⁺`) of two molecules of hydrochloric acid (HCl) attack the carbonate ion (`CO⁻₂`) to form carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water.  
You end up with the salt calcium chloride in solution – the only other products are the bright‑fizzing liquid water and the invisible gas CO₂.

**2. Balancing the two‑step equation**

| Species         | 1 | 2 |
|-----------------|---|---|
| **Reactants (1)**  | CaCO₃ (s) | 2 H⁺ (aq) |
| **Reactants (2)**  |   | 2 Cl⁻ (aq) |
| **Products (1)**   | Ca²⁺ (in CaCl₂) | CO₂ (g) |
| **Products (2)**   | H₂O (l) |  |

Putting it together:

\[
\boxed{\text{CaCO}_{3}(s) + 2\,\text{HCl}(aq) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{CaCl}_{2}(aq) + \text{CO}_{2}(g) + \text{H₂O}(l)}
\]

**Stoichiometry** – One mole of CaCO₃ reacts with two moles of HCl so that the chlorine (CaCl₂) and the hydrogen balance in the products of charge and mass.

---

**3. Nature of the reaction**

| 类型 | 解释 & 典型术语 |
|------|----------|
| **酸–碱（中和）反应** | H⁺ is neutralised by the acidic carbonate, forming water. |
| **双位移（置换）反应** | Reaction of a soluble intermediate (H⁺) with the insoluble carbonate, yielding a soluble salt and a gas. |
| **脱羧反应** | The carbonate is *de‑carbonated* – CO₂ is liberated. |

(‘碳酸盐脱羧’ is commonly called the acid–carbonate reaction in chemistry texts.)

---

**4. Observations to confirm the reaction is “on”**

| Observation | What it tells you |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Immediate gas bubbles** | CO₂ is evolving at constant speed, a hallmark of acid‑carbonate chemistry. |
| **Fizzing / “Fizzling”** | Indicates vigorous chemical motion. |
| “**Heat felt at the base**” | Exothermic; heat is accounted for by the temperature rise or a slightly warmer surface. |
| **Solidification of CaCO₃ disappears** | The original carbonate dissolves completely. |
| **Clear, slightly cloudy or colorless liquid** | The product CaCl₂ is in solution; it may look slightly turbidish because of Ca(OH)₂ or other trace impurities. |
| **Pressure buildup** (if sealed) | A rise in internal pressure is a common way to reach vapour‑phase CO₂ in a closed system. |
| **pH changes at 8–10** | The solution turns progressively yellowish‑brown (when using phenolphthio‑in dye) because chloride gas is released and the reaction is slower. |

**Use of a simple sensor** – A simple gas‑collection collar or a scale measuring the mass of the solid and solution change can reveal the evolution of CO₂.

You can also photograph the bubbling **over the course of a minute** (the reaction is typically quick (<3 min). You see a “ring” of CO₂ forming in a chamber, and a faint white effervescence of water droplets.

**5. Verify the balanced equation****

- Ca: 1 → 1    
- C: 1 → 1    
- O: 3 «‑→‑» (2 in CO₂ + 1 in H₂O)    
- H: 2 (from two HCl) + 0? (One equivalence—*2* in H₂O)  
- Cl: 2 → 2  

Everything balances at the atomic level.
