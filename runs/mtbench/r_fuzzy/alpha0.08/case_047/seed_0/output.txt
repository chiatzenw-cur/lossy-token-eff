The terms come from the Greek words “exo” meaning *outside*, “endo” meaning *inside*, and “therme” meaning *heat*. They describe how heat flows during a chemical reaction (or, more generally, a physical process).  

| Feature | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---------|------------|-------------|
| **Enthalpy change (ΔH)** | Negative (ΔH > 0 is heating of the surroundings, ΔH < 0 is release of heat). | Positive (ΔH > 0). |
| **Heat flow** | Heat *flows out* of the system into the surroundings. | Heat *flows in* from the surroundings into the system. |
| **Temperature change** | System temperature usually rises (unless heat is removed). | System temperature usually falls. |
| **Surroundings feel** | Warm or hot. | Cool. |
| **Example** | Burning a candle, combustion of gasoline. | Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water (ice pack). |

## 1. The thermodynamic criterion

| Quantity | How it’s used |
|----------|---------------|
| **ΔH (enthalpy change)** | The most common, easiest to tabulate. If ΔH < 0, the reaction is exothermic; if ΔH > 0, it is endothermic. |
| **ΔU (change in internal energy)** | For reactions where pressure is constant, ΔH ≈ ΔU + PΔV. In most chemical reactions ΔV is small, so ΔH ≈ ΔU. |
| **Heat measured in a calorimeter** | An exothermic reaction increases the calorimeter’s well temperature, while an endothermic reaction lowers it. |

Thus the **criterion** I used is the *sign of the enthalpy change (ΔH)*. A negative ΔH means the system has given up energy as heat; a positive ΔH means the system has taken up energy from the surroundings.

---

## 2. Why heat moves the way it does

### Exothermic (ΔH < 0)

- In the reaction’s *formation* of products, the bonds formed are *lower in energy* than the bonds broken.  
- The excess energy is released as thermal energy that can be sensed as a temperature rise.  
- Because energy flows from high to low, the system loses the excess; this is what we call “exothermic.”

### Endothermic (ΔH > 0)

- Here, the products require *more* energy than the reactants had.  
- The system must absorb energy from surroundings (usually as heat).  
- The surroundings lose that energy, cooling down.

---

## 3. A concrete, everyday example

### *Using an ice pack (endothermic)*

- **Process**: Dissolve ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) in water.  
- **ΔH**: +25 kJ mol⁻¹ (typical value).  
- **Result**: The solution absorbs heat from the skin and the air, resulting in a cooling sensation. The ice pack feels cold because the reaction has taken thermal energy from the environment.

### *Igniting a candle (exothermic)*

- **Process**: Oxidation of paraffin wax (C₁₅H₃₀) with oxygen to produce CO₂ and H₂O.  
- **ΔH**: ~‑280 kJ mol⁻¹ per molecule of wax.  
- **Result**: The reaction releases a large amount of heat, lighting the wick and raising the temperature of the surrounding air. You can feel the heat radiating from the flame.

## 4. Quick “checklist” in practice

| What you measure | What it tells you |
|------------------|-------------------|
| Temperature rise in a sealed container (no heat loss) | Exothermic (ΔH < 0) |
| Temperature drop in a sealed container (no heat input) | Endothermic (ΔH > 0) |
| Calorimeter reading differences after reaction | Use ΔH to confirm sign |
| Observed sensation (warm vs cool) | Good qualitative clue |

---

## 5. Take‑away

- **Exothermic**: Heat leaves the system. ΔH is negative. Surfaces feel warm.  
- **Endothermic**: Heat enters the system. ΔH is positive. Surfaces feel cool.  

In everyday life we encounter both: a burnt toast (exothermic) and a “cold pack” for injuries (endothermic). Understanding the sign of ΔH is the key criterion that lets us classify any reaction as one or the other.
