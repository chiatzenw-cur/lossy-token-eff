## 2.  How the Three Indicators Move Together

### 2.1  GDP ↔ Unemployment – Okun’s Law

* **Law of Okun** – a 1‑percentage‑point drop in unemployment relative to its natural rate typically translates into a 2–3 % increase in real GDP. It is a **statistical regularity** (not a causal principle) that holds best in the *short run* when real wages and productivity are relatively inelastic.

* **Practical take‑away** – when the economy’s output rises, workers are hired; unemployment falls; the circular‑flow of income amplifies.

* **Limitations** – structural changes (automation, demographic shifts) weaken the correlation; the law tends to be flatter in advanced economies that are already near potential output.

### 2.2  GDP ↔ Inflation – The Phillips Curve

Empirical and theoretical analyses tell us that:

| Stage | Relationship | Intuition |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| **Short run** (before expectations adjust) | **Inverse**: ↑ output → ↑ inflation | When businesses squeeze the economy, they bid up wages and prices; before expectations catch up, the curve slopes down. |
| **Long run** (after ingrained expectations) | **Vertical**: Output ≈ potential; inflation only changes when policy causes expectations to diverge | The economy returns to its potential GDP; only the price level diverges. |
| **A‑shocks (aggregate–supply, technology, labor‑market) ** | ↑ potential output → ↓ inflation (if demand is unchanged) | More supply can ease price pressures. |

* **Practical lag** – changes in policy‑induced demand take ~6–12 months to feed fully into inflation. People form expectations; firms adjust wages; inflation expectations nestle.

* **Crisis case** – during the 1970s stagflation, a supply‑side shock (oil price spike) eliminated the short‑run trade‑off: output went down while inflation stayed high.

## 3.  Fiscal Policy – what it does & the trade‑offs

### 3.1  Expansionary Fiscal Policy

| Tool | Mechanism | Typical Impact | Caveats |
|------|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Increased Spending** (infrastructure, health, education) | Raises government demand → pushes the *AD* curve rightward. | ↑ GDP, ↓ unemployment, ↑ inflation (sensitively). |
| **Tax Cuts/Transfers** on households | Increases disposable income → higher consumption → AD ↑. | Same as above; may narrow deficits, generate crowd‑out if financed by debt. |
| **Subsidies/Targeted Grants** | Incentivize specific sectors or low‑income households | Can boost economic activity and productive capacity; fiscal multiplier can exceed 1. |

- **Multiplier size** is context‑dependent: 1.0–1.5 in a low‑interest‑rate economy, 0.5–1.0 when monetary policy is tight.

- **Financing matters**: Debt‑financed expansion is more inflationary than tax‑funded expansion, particularly when the debt servicing burden becomes sizable. But the Fed can “harden” the room for debt‑financing when monetary policy is ultra‑acquiescent.

### 3.2  Contractionary Fiscal Policy

| Tool | Mechanism | Typical Impact | Caveats |
|------|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Reduced Spending** (cuts public‑investment) | AD falls → GDP falls → Unemployment rises. | Inflation falls, but at the cost of potential output. |
| **Higher Taxes** | Crowds out private consumption; lower disposable income. | Higher revenue, potentially counteracting inflation risk. |

- **Fiscal “tightening”** must be timely formatted to avoid triggering recessions early if the economy is fragile.

## 4.  Monetary Policy – the Levers of the Centre

### 4.1  Conventional Policy: The Policy (Nominal) Interest

| Tool | Mechanism | Typical Impact | Caveats |
|------|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Open‑Market Operations (OMO)** – buying/selling Treasury bills | Alters reserves → Loanable‑funds rates. | Lower policy rates → cheaper borrowing → higher AD. |
| **Reserve‑Requirement Ratio** | Decrease → banks hold fewer reserves → more lending. | Amplify AD further. |
| **Discount Rate** | Lower → easier bank access to central bank funds. | Modest effect; often less used than OMOs. |

- **Why it matters** – Lower rates lower the cost of capital; mortgages, business loans; change asset‑price expectations.  
- **Transmission delay** – 3–15 months before the interest‑rate change fully affects GDP and inflation.  
- **Risk of “liquidity trap”** – when nominal rates are at zero (or negative) and can't push AD further, central banks turn to *quantitative easing* (QE).

### 4.2  Unconventional Policy: QE, Forward Guidance, Negative Rates

| Tool | Mechanism | Typical Impact | Caveats |
|------|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **QE** – buying long‑term bonds & other assets | Expands the balance sheet; reduces long‑term rates; raises asset prices | Increases household net worth → spending; also yields “portfolio rebalancing” effect. |
| **Negative‑Rate Policy** – interest on reserves below 0 | Discourages risk‑tolerant investors; pushes them to take more credit risk or accept low rates | Uncertainty about long‑term effect; may destabilise financial markets. |
| **Forward Guidance** – committing future stance | Food for expectations; can reduce later uncertainty | Requires credible promise; mis‑timing can damage credibility. |

## 7.  Rules of Thumb for Policy Makers

| Principle | What to Monitor | When to Act |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Inflation‑Expectation Stability** | CPI, PCE, inflation forecasts (e.g., 5‑year average) | Stimulate only if inflation expectations remain below target. |
| **Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)** | Unemployment deviation from trend | If unemployment falls below NAIRU → risk of overheating. |
| **Fiscal Sustainability** | Debt-to-GDP, primary deficit | Avoid chronic deficits that threaten fiscal credibility. |
| **Balance‑Sheet Milk‑Ops** | Central‑bank balance‑sheet / reserves | Keep reserve rate environment aligned with target. |
| **Output Gap** | Real GDP – Potential GDP | If output gap > 0 → expansion; < 1 → contraction. |

## 8.  Caveats and Modern Complications

| Issue | Small‑size | Why it matters |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| **Supply‑Side shocks** | Oil, weather, pandemics | They shift the SS curve, undermining the simple GDP–inflation trade‑off. |
| **Financial‑market constraints** | Credit crunch, rated‑bank issues | Even low rates may not translate into spending. |
| **Expectations‑rich world** | Forward guidance, “price‑level targets” | Policies now rely on expectations more strongly than before. |
| **Globalisation** | Worker migration, outsourcing | Domestic employment may be decoupled from GDP growth. |
| **Evolving fiscal institutions** | Sovereign‑debt ceilings, tax base erosion | Makes fiscal policy more political. |

## 9.  Bottom‑Line: The Policy Trade‑off

For a given country, economies roughly satisfy the “inverse” Keynesian short‑run trinity:

1. **Lower unemployment** → **Higher GDP** → **Higher inflation**  
2. **Lower inflation** → **Higher potential output (**thriving**)** → **Lower unemployment**  

Policy makers **must** balance the *temporal* nature of these relationships: an aggressive stimulus reduces unemployment fast but can lead to a price spike after a lag, and a contractionary stance can reassure inflation but may stifle growth and push unemployment up.

The *new* mantra is **“the inflation outlook, not lifting rates to the top of the range”**. Central banks use **forward‑guidance**, **target‑inflation** frameworks, and **flexible‑but‑anchored guidance** to keep expectations calm while remaining ready to quell overheating.

**In a nutshell:**  
- GDP, inflation, and unemployment are loosely (but importantly) linked.  
- Fiscal discipline or stimulus moves the economy *west* or *east* on the AD curve.  
- Monetary policy nudges the *interest* level, which influences investment, consumption, and expectations.  
- The trick is to time and calibrate those nudges so that the economy stays on the **potential‑output** trajectory without letting price pressures spiral or work arcing.  

That’s the synthesis policymakers use every day, and it’s why macro‑economists spend centuries debating the *optimal* path through the adjustment.
