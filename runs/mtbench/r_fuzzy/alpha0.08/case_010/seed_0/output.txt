In quantum mechanics every system is described by a **state vector** (or *wavefunction*)  
\(|\Psi\rangle\) that lives in a complex Hilbert space.  
The *principle of superposition* states that

> **If \(|\phi_1\rangle\) and \(|\phi_2\rangle\) are allowed states of the system, then any linear combination
>  \[
>   |\Psi\rangle = a\,|\phi_1\rangle + b\,|\phi_2\rangle ,
>  \]
>  with complex coefficients \(a,b\) (subject to \(\lvert a\rvert^2+\lvert b\rvert^2=1\) for a single‑particle state), is also an allowed state.**

The linearity of the Schrödinger equation guarantees that this property has no cost: once a state is prepared in one basis, it steadily evolves into another basis, preserving the superposition coefficients.

### 1.1  Concrete Examples

| System | Basis states | Superposition |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| Spin‑½ particle (qubit) | \(|\uparrow_z\rangle,\,|\downarrow_z\rangle\) | \(|\Psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\uparrow_z\rangle+|\downarrow_z\rangle\bigr)\) (a “plus‑way” qubit). |
| Photon polarization | \(|H\rangle,\,|V\rangle\) | \(|\Psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|H\rangle+|V\rangle\bigr)\) = 45° linear polarization. |
| Schrödinger‑cat description | \(|\text{alive}\rangle,\,|\text{dead}\rangle\) | \(|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\text{alive}\rangle+|\text{dead}\rangle\bigr)\) (hypothetical superposition). |

The **probability** that a measurement of some observable \(A\) finds result \(a_i\) is \(|\langle a_i | \Psi\rangle|^2\). After the measurement the state *collapses* to the eigenstate associated with the outcome (the famous “measurement problem”).

## 2.  What is Quantum Entanglement?

Entanglement is a property that only a **composite** system may possess.  Consider two subsystems \(A\) and \(B\) with state spaces \(\mathcal{H}_A\) and \(\mathcal{H}_B\).  The full state lives in the tensor product \(\mathcal{H}_A\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\):

\[
|\Psi\rangle_{AB}\in\mathcal{H}_A\otimes\mathcal{H}_B.
\]

### 2.1  Separable vs Entangled States

- **Separable (product) states**:  
  \[
  |\Psi\rangle_{AB}=|\phi\rangle_A\otimes|\chi\rangle_B .
  \]
  These are *not* entangled; each subsystem has its own well‑defined state, independent of the other.

- **Entangled states**:  
  \[
  |\Psi\rangle_{AB}=a\;|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B + b\;|1\rangle_A|1\rangle_B,
   \quad a\neq 0,\,b\neq 0,
  \]
  cannot be factorized into a product of one‑subsystem states.  
  The most elementary example is a **Bell state**:
  \[
  |\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0\rangle_A+|1\rangle_A\bigr)
                 \otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0\rangle_B+|1\rangle_B\bigr)
                 =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle+|11\rangle\bigr).
  \]

In a Bell state, measuring qubit \(A\) immediately determines the state of qubit \(B\), regardless of the distance between them—hence the “spooky action at a distance” slogan.

### 2.2  Entanglement as a *Specific* Superposition

An entangled state is **itself** a simple superposition, but the superposition is over *joint* product states.  In other words:

\[
|\Psi\rangle_{AB}= \sum_k c_k\,|k\rangle_A\otimes|k\rangle_B
\]

The coefficients \(c_k\) encode *correlations* that cannot be described by assigning local states to A or B alone.  Thus, entanglement **builds on** the superposition principle; without a superposition, you cannot have entanglement.

---

## 3.  How Superposition Gives Rise to Entanglement

1. **Construct the joint Hilbert space**:  
   For two qubits, the basis is \(\{|00\rangle, |01\rangle, |10\rangle, |11\rangle\}\).

2. **Prepare a joint state that is a superposition of two (or more) of these basis vectors**:  
   Example: \(|\Psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)\).

3. **Check separability**:  
   Attempt to write \(|\Psi\rangle\) as \(\alpha|a\rangle_A\otimes|b\rangle_B\).  If impossible, the state is entangled.

4. **Observe measurement statistics**:  
   Even though each qubit individually shows no definite result until measured, the outcomes are perfectly correlated (or anti‑correlated) because of the joint superposition.

## 4.  Practical Illustrations

| Phenomenon | Superposition Step | Entanglement Feature |
|------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Compton scattering of photons | Superposition of momentum states | No entanglement (single photon) |
| Spin‑orbit coupling in electrons | Superposition in spatial and spin space | Usually not entangled unless multiple particles |
| SPDC (Spontaneous Parametric Down‑Conversion) | Superposition of two‑photon states | Produces entangled photon pairs in polarization |
| Quantum teleportation | Uses a Bell‑state superposition | Transfers an unknown qubit via entanglement |
| Bell test (CHSH inequality) | Prepared Bell states | Demonstrates non‑local correlations that violate classical bounds |

## 5.  Key Relationship Points

1. **Superposition is a general rule; entanglement is a particular kind of superposition.**  
   - All pure states are superpositions.  
   - Only those superpositions that cannot be factorized into separate subsystem states are considered entangled.

2. **Entanglement requires multiple subsystems.**  
   - A single particle cannot act entangled with itself.  
   - The *dimension* of the joint Hilbertspace must be at least the product of the dimensions of the subsystems.

3. **Measurement on one part of an entangled state instantaneously (non‑locally) fixes the state of the other part** – this is a consequence of the correlations encoded in the superposition coefficients.

4. **Decoherence erases superposition and therefore destroys entanglement.**  
   - Interactions with an environment typically project the system into a classical mixture, removing the off‑line terms in the density matrix that encode superpositions.

5. **Entanglement measures quantify the “amount” of superposition across subsystems.**  
   - Example: Von Neumann entropy of the reduced density matrix for a pure bipartite state  \(\rho_A = \operatorname{Tr}_B |\Psi\rangle\langleΨ|\).

---

## 6.  Bottom Line

- **Superposition** is the foundational feature of quantum mechanics: a system’s probability amplitudes can add linearly, giving rise to interference, tunneling, and the very possibility of coherent quantum computation.

- **Entanglement** is a manifestation of superposition at the *multi‑particle* level.  It represents correlations that cannot be rewritten as independent (separable) states of the subsystems.  Without superposition, entanglement could not exist; conversely, every entangled state is built from a superposition of product basis states.

In practice, experiments that prepare and measure entangled states (Bell tests, quantum teleportation, quantum cryptography) use the very linearity that leads to superposition—but exploit the fact that these superpositions live in a bipartite (or multipartite) space, allowing *non‑classical* correlations that are at the heart of quantum technology.
