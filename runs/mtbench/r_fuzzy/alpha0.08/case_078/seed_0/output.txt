The base‑rate fallacy (also called the *representativeness bias* or *availability heuristic*) is a logical error that occurs when people **overlook a relevant “base rate” (prior probability)** and focus instead on more vivid or specific information. In other words, we make an inference about a group or event without taking into account the underlying frequency of that particular outcome in the general population.

The classic format is:

```
Given:
– Event A’s base rate in the population: 1%
– New evidence suggests A is likely (e.g., “A often co‑occurs with B”, but B’s own base rate is 90%).

If we ignore the base rate and only consider the new evidence, we over‑estimate how likely A is.
```

### Why Politicians Love It

- **Simplicity in messaging**: “We tackled X; here’s what that looks like.”  
- **Emotional appeal**: Specific anecdotes or illustrative statistics resonate more than abstract probabilities.  
- **Control of narrative**: By dissociating from the broader context, they can shift perception to a more favorable light.

## 2. Five Concrete Ways Politicians Deploy the Base‑Rate Fallacy in Campaigns

| # | Scenario & Example | How the Base Rate Is Ignored | Why It Persuades |
|---|--------------------|------------------------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Crime Drop Claim** | A candidate says, “Our city’s violent crime fell 30% last year. That’s proof our tough‑on‑crime policy works.” The *base rate*—that *national* violent‑crime rate is still 400 per 100,000 residents—is omitted. | Voters extrapolate the local success to the whole country, feeling safe against crime. |
| 2 | **Unemployment “Reset”** | “We’ve cut unemployment by 0.3 percentage point to 4.2%.” The *base rate* of 4.2% still means RANKED among the worst states, but the headline quietly hides the comparison. | Sounds like good news; the finer context (e.g., the national average is 7.1%) is ignored. |
| 3 | **Grass‑and‑Plants in Polling** | “In the last poll, 55% of voters in County X trust us to fix the potholes.” Reporters project that 55% across the entire state means the candidate will outperform rivals. | 4 | **“Tighter Belt” on Taxes** | “Tax revenue grew by 3% during our first year.” Politicians often ignore that the *base rate* for overall state revenue growth that year was 5% due to national trends; the 3% rise is merely a lagging indicator. | The audience thinks they’re adding money to the coffers, when in reality the economy was already booming. |

## 3. How to Spot These Uses

| Indicator | What to Look For |
|-----------|------------------|
| **Localizing a number** | “In District A, we did X.” If the district isn’t the same size or mixture as the electorate, that statistic may not scale. |
| **Positive framing with no comparative data** | “We lowered the rate by Y%." Look for what the *previous* rate was, and how that compares to a broader baseline. |
| **Over‑promised outcome** | “This will stop Z in our area” when the *underlying* risk of zero for Z is already low in that region. |
| **Missing counter‑variate** | Ignored variables that influence the statistic. For instance, crime dropped while the population base changed. |
| **Temporal mismatch** | Using a recent statistical headline when the national trend is not aligned (e.g., last month’s unemployment peak vs. national four‑month average). |

---

### Final Note

The base‑rate fallacy isn’t just a statistical trick; it’s a persuasive tool. Politicians use it by spotlighting a specific, usually “good” statistic while *withholding* the broader context that would neutralize or even reverse the message. By spotting the absence of the base rate—and asking “What percent of the *whole* electorate is this really describing?”—voters can guard themselves against misleading narratives.
