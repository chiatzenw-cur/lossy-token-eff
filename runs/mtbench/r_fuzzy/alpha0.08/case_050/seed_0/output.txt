Socrates did not outline a single “system” of philosophy the way his student Plato later wrote.  
Instead his strategy for questioning the prevailing views of Athens was a set of practical, conversational techniques that made his interlocutors confront the gaps and contradictions in their own thinking. Below are the main methods that Socrates used, and how each of them served to upend ordinary assumptions.

| # | Method | How Socratic Method Works | What He Achieved (Challenge to Athenian Thought) |
|---|--------|--------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| **1** | **Socratic Elenchus (Cross‑Examination)** | Socrates asks a series of increasingly specific questions that force his partner to “restate” their claim, often revealing logical inconsistencies. | Athenian elites, nobles, and sophists were left unable to justify their own positions. By exposing the lack of solid foundations, Socrates raised doubt about the default certainty that ruled public discourse. |
| **2** | **Socratic Aporia (State of Puzzlement)** | When a questioner is led into contradiction—or when the evidence is insufficient—the result of the dialogue is a state of aporia, a mindful acknowledgment of ignorance. | A theocratic democracy prized civic engagement and honest self‑assessment. Aporia made every citizen see humility as a starting point, undermining the hubris that was common among politicians, judges, and athletes. |
| **3** | **Socratic Irony (“I’m Knowing Nothing”)** | Socrates pretends ignorance or feigned humility to encourage others to talk freely. His “irony speaks as if he is the one who does not know the truth.” | By convincingly claiming ignorance or announcing “I only know that I do not know,” Socrates challenged the self‑confidence of sophist teachers and the self‑assured methods of Athenian law and public life. |
| **4** | **Dialectic “Seeking the Universal”** | Rather than prescribing specific doctrines, Socrates sought the common concept that would encompass all examples (student, writer, soldier, king). | He pushed participants into a search for the generic essence that could sustain the polis’s institutions *and* the moral conduct that supported them—a way of thinking that was absent in the prevailing “techne” (skill of the clever swear‑thieves). |
| **5** | **Personal Ethical Examination** | Socrates famously said, “The unexamined life is not worth living.” He demanded that the *individual* equal the *public* in honesty. | Athenian arrogance, the “rich but cowardly, poor but brave,” was confronted by a universal call to virtue that extended beyond elite classes.  This ethical critique led to a “new democracy” where personal introspection mattered as much as rhetoric. |

### Why These Methods “Challenged” the Consensus

1. **Unreliability of Authority**<sup>1</sup>  
   The sophists and intellectuals of Athens claimed expertise, and politicians / jurists claimed pragmatic wisdom. Socrates’ **elenchus** turned the talking into a provocation that showed the fallibility of educated authority. Head‑standers could not defend their knowledge—so the entire “expert” class was questioned.

2. **The Power of a Reflective Mind**<sup>2</sup>  
   Atheist ethos—”ignore truth and seek power”—was neutralized by **aporia**. Socrates demonstrated that the first step to wisdom is recognizing one’s ignorance. This undermined the “learnable” stigma of political life and opened a new arena for civic virtue.

3. **Democratic Participation Reached a Higher Level**<sup>3</sup>  
   Athenian democracy was highly public, but largely dominated by speech and shouts. The Socratic **irony** made every citizen an *auditor* of their own knowledge, thereby pushing democracy beyond mere will‑expression to a consistent deliberation on the good.

4. **Ateus of the Ultimate Criterion**<sup>4</sup>  
   By challenging the *specific* (rules, laws, status) and focusing on *universal* notions, Socrates negated that Athenian society’s reliance on pragmatism. He made it clear that “good government” should be defended by a *definition* that applies to all policymakers, not just the influential.

5. **Individual Virtue as Public Good**<sup>5</sup>  
   The political culture embraced a *collective onslaught* (war in wars, plots and tragedies). Socrates’ insistence that each person examine their own conduct converted the citizen (and the state) into a moral laboratory.

### The Big Picture

- **“If I had a single problem, it would be that the citizens are taught…to think they are the generality of things” (Socrates, *Apology*, 4).**  
  By systematically deconstructed the standard thinking at the base of rhetoric, law, and education, Socrates didn’t claim to give *answers*, but he made it clear that knowledge required **persistent questioning**.

- **Challenge is an ongoing, collective process** that Socratic method drove: the critical eye shows that a rational dialogue can only exist when everyone is unconscious of their ignorance, and at the same time expects the *same* pound of doubt from the public.

This relentless method of **self‑scrutiny and communal interrogation** systematically dismantled the assumptions that underpinned Athenian philosophy, law, and politics, leaving room for a new age of philosophical inquiry that would be carried forth by Plato, Xenophon, and Aristotle.
