In quantum mechanics a system is described by a *state vector*  
\[
|\psi\rangle x \in \mathcal{H},
\]
where \(\mathcal{H}\) is a Hilbert space.  
For a single observable \(A\) (usually the Hamiltonian \(\hat H\) or a spin component), the *eigenstates* \(\{|a_k\rangle\}\) satisfy

\[
A |a_k\rangle = a_k |a_k\rangle .
\]

The **superposition principle** says that **any** linear combination of eigenstates is also a legitimate state:

\[
|\psi\rangle = \sum_{k} c_k |a_k\rangle,\;\;\; c_k \in \mathbb{C},\; \sum_k |c_k|^2 = 1.
\]

Key points about superposition

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Superposition** | The wave–function can be a sum of basis states. |
| **Interference** | Sub‑components \(c_k\) can interfere constructively or destructively en route to observable outcomes. |
| **Measurement collapse** | A measurement of \(A\) gives at random one eigenvalue \(a_k\) with probability \(|c_k|^2\). The post‑measurement state is forced to the eigenstate \(|a_k\rangle\). |
| **Simulation** | In numerical quantum work, we keep arrays of complex coefficients to track the superposition basis. |
| **No local “choice”** | The coefficients are intrinsic to the system, not a matter of ignorance before measurement. |

The famous scenario that illustrates the idea, “ superposition of mutually exclusive possibilities,” is the **Schrödinger’s cat** story: the cat’s state is  
\[
|\text{lives/​dead}\rangle = \alpha |\text{alive}\rangle + \beta |\text{dead}\rangle,
\]
with both parts present until a measurement (e.g. an observer opening the box) breaks the superposition.

### Quantum Entanglement

⟶ **Composite Systems**  
When two or more subsystems share a joint Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B\), a *separable* state is a simple product
\[
|\psi\rangle_{\text{sep}} = |\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B,
\]
meaning each subsystem has a definite, independent state.

⟶ **Entangled States**  
An entangled state embodies *non‑factorizable* correlations: it cannot be written as a single product. The simplest form is a Bell state:
\[
|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\bigl(|\uparrow\rangle_A |\uparrow\rangle_B +
     |\downarrow\rangle_A |\downarrow\rangle_B\bigr).
\]

**Why it is a superposition?**  
- The *ternary* term \(|\uparrow\rangle_A |\uparrow\rangle_B\) is one distinct product state.  
- The *other* term \(|\downarrow\rangle_A |\downarrow\rangle_B\) is another.  
- The state is a coherent **superposition** of these two product states, *with the same weight and a definite relative phase*. The latter yields spooky correlations when the subsystems are measured separately.

| **Entanglement** | A specific superposition of *object* states that cannot be factored apart | \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle\bigr)\). |
| **Probability-Amplitude** | The same coefficients that each sufficed for a single‐particle system | in entanglement the effective scales are \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) but the joint space is sparse. |

**Key Insight**  
Entanglement *isn't* a separate, “new” phenomenon that sits outside superposition. It emerges when we impose the superposition principle on a *joint* Hilbert space, and it is a special class of valid linear combinations that encode *perfect correlations* between subsystems. Formally, the Schmidt decomposition tells us any transaction in \(\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B\) can be written as

\[
|\psi\rangle
= \sum_{j} \lambda_j\,|\alpha_j\rangle_A \otimes |\beta_j\rangle_B,
\]

where the \(\lambda_j\) are non‑negative real numbers and \(\{|\alpha_j\rangle\},\{|\beta_j\rangle\}\) are orthonormal bases.  
If there’s more than one non‑zero \(\lambda_j\), then **the state is entangled**—and you just have a superposition (with more than one term) analogous to the single‑particle context but *in the joint space*.
