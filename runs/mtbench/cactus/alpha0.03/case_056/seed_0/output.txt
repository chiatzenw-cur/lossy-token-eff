### 1. What is Probability?  
> *Probability* is a **measure of how likely something is to happen**.  
> In math terms it is a number between 0 (can’t happen) and 1 (certain).  
>  
>  _Example_:  
>  - Toss a fair coin – probability of heads = **½** (0.5).  
>  - Pick a marble from a bag with 2 red and 5 blue – probability of blue = **5/7** ≈ 0.714.

### 2. How We Express It  
| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **S** | Sample space – all possible outcomes (e.g., all sides of the coin) |
| **A** | Event – the outcome(s) we care about (e.g., heads) |
| **P(A)** | Probability of event **A** |

We compute **P(A)** – % of ways event occurs over all possible outcomes.

### 3. The Data you Need  
- **Count the possibilities**: How many outcomes are in \(S\)?  
- **Count the successes**: How many are in \(A\)?  
- **Divide**:  
  \[
  P(A) = \frac{\text{# successes}}{\text{# total possibilities}}
  \]

### 4. Step‑by‑Step Example: “Roll a Die and Get a Hard Number”  
1. **Sample space** \(S\) of a six‑sided die: \( \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\) → 6 outcomes.  
2. **Hard number** (≥4): \( A = \{4,5,6\}\) → 3 successes.  
3. **Probability**: \( P(A) = 3/6 = ½ = 0.5\).  
So you have a **50 %** chance you’ll get a thick, ≥4 die on a single roll.

### 5. Compound Odds – A Simple Process (Event `A + B`)  
If you group several events, and you want the probability that *any* of them occurs, add their individual probabilities only keep the sums short‑hand:
- For **a single roll of a die**:
  - **A** → roll a 6, need 1 outcome out of 6 ➜ \(\frac{1}{6}\)
  - **B** → roll 3, need 1 outcome out of 6 ➜ \(\frac{1}{6}\)

P(A or B) = \( \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} \) = \( \frac{2}{6} \) which simplifies to \(\frac{1}{3}\).

> **Important**: If jointly possible outcomes overlap, we subtract the overlap to avoid double‑counting.  
> (Use the “Inclusion–Exclusion Principle”). For textbook problems, we usually double‑check it.

### 6. Events That Depend on Each Other  
When you’re drawing two items **without replacement** (you cannot re‑insert the first item):

- The first draw law: *First event happens with probability \(P_1\).*
- The second draw: *Now the probability of the second event is changed* because the sample space changes.

**Example** – Two marbles, 1 red, 1 blue:
- Draw 1 blue? ➜ 1/2.  
- Then draw another blue? ➜ (already got the blue, remove it) → 0/1 = 0.  
- You cannot draw a blue twice; events are *dependent*.

If you treat adding the first occurrences then the second will change the “game”.
