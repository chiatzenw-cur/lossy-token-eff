In quantum mechanics the state of a system is described by a **wavefunction** (or a vector) that lives in a space called *Hilbert space*.  
The wavefunction contains the full information about every possible outcome of an experiment.  
A particle can
*be* a **superposition** of several “classical” states, which means that the wavefunction is a *linear combination of the eigenstates* of each observable.

For a simple two‑state system (say, a spin‑½ particle or a qubit) one can write

\[
|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle , \qquad (|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1)
\]

with \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) the two possible states (e.g. “spin up” and “spin down”).  
If we now *measure* the **spin‑z** operator, the outcome is “up” with probability \(|\alpha|^2\) and “down” with probability \(|\beta|^2\).

*Key ideas*

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|----------------|
| **Quantization** | Observables are represented by operators that have discrete eigenvalues. | Physical measurables are discrete. |
| **Relationalism** | A quantum state changes the instant of a measurement. | The wavefunction encodes possibilities, not certainties. |
| **Singular MP‑update** | The act of measurement forces the state to “close” to one of the eigenstates. | Quantum theory is probabilistic. |

**Entanglement**

The superposition principle extends to *states of a system –‐ and that system can involve many particles*.  
When the joint state of a composite system cannot be written as a simple product of individual inner states, the subsystems are *entangled*.

Mathematically:

\[
|\Psi \rangle \times \text{(combined system)} \quad\not\ar\quad |\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B
\]

So, in the case of a state

\[
|\Psi \rangle_{AB} = \alpha |00\rangle + \beta |11\rangle , \qquad \text{so } |00\rangle\!\equiv\!\!|0\rangle_A\otimes|0\rangle_B
\]

the pairs \(A\) and \(B\) are *entangled* when \( |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2 = 1\) and \(\alpha,\beta\) are not all zero.  
If you look at one particle alone, its behavior is completely random (the *reduced state* is a mixture), while in the whole, the two pair are correlated in a way that no classical correlation can explain.

*Why entanglement relies on superposition*

1. **Non‑factorizability**. An entangled state is simply a non‑product superposition.  
   If the two superpositions had been 100 % canceled or simply matched to a product, the rest would be non‑ionizable.  
2. **Correlations**. In entangled states the outcome of one measurement applies to **both** particles because any single measurement collapses the joint state at once.
