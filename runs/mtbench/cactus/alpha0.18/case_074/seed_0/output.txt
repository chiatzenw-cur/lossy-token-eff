When a piece of solid calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) is added to a dilute aqueous solution of hydrochloric acid (HCl), the acid attacks the carbonate and liberates carbon dioxide gas.  CO₂ bubbles into the solution, and the calcium ion reacts with the chloride ions to give aqueous calcium chloride (CaCl₂).  The reaction also produces a small amount of water, so the mixture is typically a mixture of the aqueous salt and the surrounding hydration and the newly formed CO₂ gas.

---

### Balanced chemical equation

\[
\boxed{\text{CaCO}_3(s) \;+\; 2\,\text{HCl}(aq) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{CaCl}_2(aq) \;+\; \text{CO}_2(g) \;+\; \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)}
\]

* The carbonate ion receives two protons from the acid and is converted into CO₂ and H₂O.  
* All protonated species are found on the right‑hand side – the clear “hot” products (CaCl₂ in solution and water) are typical for an acid‑base reaction.

### Type of reaction

| Step | Reaction type | Class (in one word) |
|------|---------------|---------------------|
| Acid + carbonate react | **Acid–base neutralization** (common acid-base reaction) | **Acid–carbonate** |
| CO₂ (gas) evolution | **Gas evolution** | **Acid–base** (chemical displacement) |
| Because the products are *H₂O* & *CaCl₂* (both soluble in water), the reaction is *exothermic* and completed by a *one‑step* reaction.  

The overall reaction is a **double‑displacement (or substitution) reaction** in which the carbonate ion (CO₃²⁻) is displaced by chloride ions (Cl⁻).  It’s also classified as a **neutralization** that produces a volatile gas (CO₂).
