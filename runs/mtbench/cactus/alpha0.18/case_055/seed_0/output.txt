Think of a language model as a “smart calculator” that learns how words and sentences talk to each other.  
When you give it a handful of sentences, a human would tell you, “You probably want the next word or the next sentence in the conversation.”  A language model learned from thousands (or billions) of public examples that the word “cat” *jumps* after “The” or “She wants” *to* “treat”, etc.

## How does a language model learn: labeled or unlabeled data?

### 1. Unlabeled “raw” text (self‑supervised learning)

Most modern large language models (like GPT‑3’s family, Claude, Llama, and even smaller ones in GPT‑a‑X) are *audited by reading im­me­rant amounts of sentences*.  
They do not need anyone—humor, correct, or explicit sign‑posts.  
The model learns to solve the problem **“next‑word prediction”**:  
- Take the first part of a sentence, guess the next word.  
- In the training data, the system returns *verdict true/false* automatically.  

Because it only needs an unsupervised distribution of words (the data is unlabeled), it is “self‑supervised.” It can be trained from publicly available books, news articles, wikipedia, and even fan‑fiction, without domain experts tagging each sentence.

### 2. Labeled data only when training comes to a specialised purpose

If you want the model to perform a *task* (like “answer a legal question”, “convert text to Yiddish”, or “detect a fraud claim”), you can give it some labeled examples.  
- **Few‑shot or fine‑tuning** give the model a few labeled examples so it knows whether its text is acceptable or not.  
- The model soon becomes a *specialised classifier* or a *creative generator* that obeys the instructions.

But for jointly training across *any*** shape this model is essentially unsupervised.
