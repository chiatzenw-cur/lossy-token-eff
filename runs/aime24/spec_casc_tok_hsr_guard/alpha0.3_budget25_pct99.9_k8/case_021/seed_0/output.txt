The two digits have to satisfy  

\[
a+c=\sqrt n .
\]

Because \(a+c\) is an integer, \(\sqrt n\) must be an integer; hence  
\(n=k^{2}\) for some integer \(k\ge 2\).  
With \(n=k^{2}\) we have  

\[
k^{2}=a b+c,\qquad a+c=k .
\]

Eliminating \(c\) gives  

\[
k^{2}=a b + k-a\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a(b-1)=k(k-1).
\]

If we set  

\[
d=b-1\;(d\ge 1),
\]

then

\[
a=\frac{k(k-1)}{d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad
c=k-a .
\]

For all \(k\) with \(2\le k\le d\) (and only those) we have \(a\le d\) and
\(c\ge 0\).  
Thus a integer \(k\) gives a \(b\)-beautiful \(n\) **iff**

\[
d\mid k(k-1).
\]

### Counting the admissible \(k\)

Factor \(d\) into prime powers

\[
d=\prod_{i=1}^{r} p_i^{\,e_i}\quad (r=\text{number of distinct primes}).
\]

Because consecutive integers are coprime, for each prime power
\(p_i^{e_i}\) the factor \(p_i^{e_i}\) must divide either \(k\) or \(k-1\).
Hence

\[
k\equiv 0\pmod{p_i^{e_i}}\quad\text{or}\quad k\equiv 1\pmod{p_i^{e_i}},
\qquad i=1,\dots ,r .
\]

Thus for each prime factor we have two choices (0 or 1), giving  
\(2^{r}\) residue classes modulo \(d\) that satisfy the divisibility.
All of them are distinct residues in \(\{0,1,\dots ,d-1\}\).

Among these residues, the class \(k\equiv 0\pmod d\) produces the
integer \(k=d\) (which lies in the interval \([2,d]\)), while the class
\(k\equiv 1\pmod d\) gives \(k=1\) which is **not** counted.
Every other of the \(2^{r}\) residues corresponds to a unique integer
\(k\) in \([2,d-1]\).

Therefore the number of admissible \(k\) – i.e. the number of
\(b\)-beautiful numbers – is

\[
f(d)=2^{r}-1 .
\]

### When does \(f(d)>10\)?

We need \(2^{r}-1>10\Rightarrow 2^{r}>11\).
The smallest integer \(r\) satisfying this is \(r=4\) because
\(2^{3}=8<11\) and \(2^{4}=16>11\).

Hence \(d\) must be divisible by at least four distinct primes.

d_{\min}=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7=210 .
