The number \(n\) has exactly two base‑\(b\) digits.  
Its digit sum is \(a+c\).  
When \(n\) is required to be *\(b\)‑beautiful* we have

\[
\sqrt{n}= a+c \; .
\]

Denote  

\[
s=\sqrt{n}\;(>0).
\]

Then

\[
n = s^2 = a\,b+c .
\tag{1}
\]

Because \(c=s-a\), (1) gives

\[
s^{2}=a\,b+s-a \Longrightarrow s^{2}-s = a\,(b-1).
\tag{2}
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  Possible values of \(s\)

Let \(m=b-1\).  
Since \(n\) has two base–\(b\) digits, \(b\le n \le b^2-1\) and
\(n=s^2\le b^2-1\); hence  

\[
2\le s \le m \qquad (m=b-1\ge2).
\]

Equation (2) says that  
\(m\mid s(s-1)\).  
Hence \(m\) divides the product of two consecutive integers.  
For each prime power \(p^k\mid m\) the exponent \(p^k\) must divide
\(s\) or \(s-1\); the two possibilities are independent for distinct
prime powers.  
Consequently the number of solutions modulo \(m\) is

\[
\#\{s\pmod m :  m\mid s(s-1)\}=2^{\omega(m)},
\]

where \(\omega(m)\) is the number of distinct prime factors of \(m\).

From \(2\le s\le m\) we have exactly one integer in each residue class,
except the residue \(1\) (that gives \(s=1\) and would make \(a=0\)).
Thus

\[
\#\{\,s\in[2,m] : m\mid s(s-1)\,\}= 2^{\omega(m)}-1 .
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Counting \(b\)-beautiful integers

For each admissible \(s\) we find

\[
a=\frac{s(s-1)}{m}\quad\text{and}\quad
c=s-a .
\]

Because \(1\le a\le m-1,\;0\le c<m\), the pair \((a,c)\) is a valid
two‑digit representation of \(n=s^2\).  
Hence

\[
\boxed{\#\{\text{\(b\)-beautiful numbers}\}=2^{\omega(m)}-1},
\qquad m=b-1.
\]

### 3.  When is the number \(>10\)?

We need

\[
2^{\omega(b-1)}-1>10 \;\Longrightarrow\;
2^{\omega(b-1)}\ge 12.
\]

The smallest integer \(m\) satisfying this is obtained when
\(\omega(m)=4\), because \(2^{3}=8<12\) and \(2^{4}=16>12\).

\[
m=2\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot 7=210.
