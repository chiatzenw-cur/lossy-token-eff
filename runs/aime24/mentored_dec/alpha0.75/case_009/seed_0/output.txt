For a subtraction game in which a player may remove either \(1\) or \(4\) tokens,
let  

* \(L(n)\) = “the position with \(n\) tokens is losing for the player to move”  
* \(W(n)\) = “the position with \(n\) tokens is winning for the player to move”

The rules are

* \(L(0)\)   – the player to move has no move, so loses.  
* \(W(n)\)   if there exists a move to a losing position: \(n-1\) or \(n-4\).  
* \(L(n)\)   if all moves go to winning positions: \(n-1\) and \(n-4\) are both winning.

Positions that are losing for the player to move are:

\[
0,\; 2,\; 5,\; 7,\; 10,\; 12,\; 15,\; 17,\; 20,\; 22,\dots
\]

Observe their pattern: they are exactly the numbers

\[
n \equiv 0\pmod 5 \quad\text{or}\quad n \equiv 2\pmod 5 .
\]

Indeed, if \(n\equiv 0\) or \(2\pmod 5\) then  
\(n-1\) and \(n-4\) are both \(1\) or \(3\pmod 5\), i.e. winning positions,
hence \(n\) is losing.  
Conversely, if \(n\equiv 1,3,4\pmod 5\) one of \(n-1\) or \(n-4\) is losing,

Thus for the initial position \(n\) (Alice to move) the position is **losing** for Alice **iff** \(n\equiv0\) or \(2\pmod 5\).
