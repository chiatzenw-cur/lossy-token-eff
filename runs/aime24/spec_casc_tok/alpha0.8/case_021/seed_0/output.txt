\[

\[

Rearranging,

\[

\tag{1}

### 1.  Counting the solutions for a fixed \(b\)

Denote \(d=b-1\).  
Equation (1) becomes

\[
a=\frac{k(k-1)}{d}\qquad (1)
\]

with the constraints

\[
1\le a\le d,\qquad 0\le c=k-a\le d .
\]

Because \(k\) is a square root of \(n\), \(k\ge 2\).  
From (1) we see that the condition \(a\in\mathbb Z\) is equivalent to

\[
d\mid k(k-1).
\tag{2}
\]

The two consecutive integers \(k\) and \(k-1\) are coprime; therefore
every prime divisor of \(d\) must divide either \(k\) or \(k-1\).
Consequently for each prime \(p\mid d\) we have two possibilities:

\[
k\equiv 0\pmod p \quad\text{or}\quad k\equiv 1\pmod p .
\]

Using the Chinese remainder theorem, for each prime factor of \(d\) we
choose one of the two possibilities independently.  
Thus the number of solutions for \(k\) modulo \(d\) is

\[
2^{\,\omega(d)},
\]

where \(\omega(d)\) is the number of distinct prime factors of \(d\).
Among these residues the congruence \(k\equiv 1\pmod d\) corresponds to
\(k=1\), which is not admissible, so we subtract one.

Hence for a fixed base \(b\)

\[
\boxed{\text{#\(b\)-eautiful numbers}=2^{\,\omega(b-1)}-1}.
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Requiring more than ten \(b\)-eautiful numbers

We need

\[
2^{\,\omega(b-1)}-1>10 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2^{\,\omega(b-1)}>11 .
\]

The smallest integer power of \(2\) exceeding \(11\) is \(2^{4}=16\).
Thus we must have

\[
\omega(b-1)\ge 4 .
\]

So \(b-1\) must contain at least four distinct prime factors.  
The smallest integer with four distinct prime factors is

\[
2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7 = 210 .
\]

Therefore the smallest possible base is

\[
b = 210+1 = 211 .
\]
