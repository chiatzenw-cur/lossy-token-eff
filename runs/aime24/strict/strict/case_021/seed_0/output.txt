\[
\{0,1,15,21,36,70,85,91\}\quad\text{and each plus }105
\]

hence the residues

\[
0,1,15,21,36,70,85,91,105,106,120,126,141,175,190,196 .
\]

The admissible \(s\) lie between \(s_{0}=15\) and \(210\); they are

\[
\boxed{15,21,36,70,85,91,105,106,120,126,141,175,190,196,210}
\]

(15 values in total).

For each such \(s\) we compute

\[
a=\frac{s(s-1)}{210},\qquad
c=s-a,
\]

which are all integers with \(1\le a\le 209\) and \(0\le c\le 209\).
Thus each of those \(s\) yields a distinct \(211\)-beautiful integer
\(n=s^{2}\).

Therefore \(b=211\) produces \(15>10\) beautiful integers.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 5.  No smaller base works

If a base \(b<211\) were to work, its \(d=b-1\) would have fewer than
four distinct prime factors.  
Then \(2^{\,\omega(d)}\le 8\), so at most eight admissible \(s\) could
exist – a contradiction to the requirement of more than ten
beautiful numbers.
