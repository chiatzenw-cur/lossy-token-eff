### 1.  Consequences of (1)

Let \(d=b-1\).  Equation (1) can be rewritten as  

\[
a\,d=k(k-1).
\tag{2}

Since \(1\le a\le d\), the right–hand side is always a multiple of \(d\).  
Setting  

\[
a=\frac{k(k-1)}{d}
\]

and requiring \(1\le a\le d\) gives two conditions

1. \(k(k-1)\) is divisible by \(d\);
2. \(k(k-1)\le d^{2}\).

The second inequality forces \(2\le k\le d\).  
Thus a positive integer \(k\) gives a **\(b\)-eautiful integer** iff  

\[
\boxed{\,d\mid k(k-1)\ \text{with}\ 2\le k\le d\,}            \tag{3}

### 2.  Counting solutions of (3)

Let \(d\) be factored into distinct primes  

\[
d=\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_i^{\,e_i},\qquad s=\omega(d).
\]

Because \(k\) and \(k-1\) are consecutive, any prime power \(p_i^{e_i}\) dividing
\(d\) must divide either \(k\) or \(k-1\) but not both.  
Hence for each prime \(p_i\) we may choose

\[
k\equiv 0 \pmod {p_i^{\,e_i}}\quad \text{or}\quad 
k\equiv 1 \pmod {p_i^{\,e_i}}.
\]

As the sums of these congruence conditions for distinct primes are
pairwise coprime, the Chinese Remainder Theorem gives a unique solution
\(k\) modulo \(d\) for each choice, and that solution satisfies
\(2\le k\le d\) except when every choice is “\(k\equiv 1\mod2^e\)’’,
which leads to \(k=1\). That choice is excluded because \(k\ge2\).

Consequently there is exactly one admissible \(k\) for each non‑empty
subset of the \(s\) distinct primes, i.e. the number of solutions is

\[
\displaystyle N(d)=3=2^s-1.
\]
