The question seeks to identify the legal challenges women faced in Malaysia related to the **Law Reform (Marriage and Divorce) Act 1976 (LRA)** and its **subsequent impact on family law**, particularly concerning **customary practices** and **Shari’a law**. To answer accurately, we need to reconcile the key legal developments and ongoing challenges introduced by this reform.

---

### 1. **Context of the LRA and Shari’a Law System**

The **LRA of 1976** was designed to standardize **marriage and divorce laws for non-Muslims**. It:
- Made **registration of marriages mandatory**.
- Introduced a **minimum age of 18** for marriage, required **court permission for polygamy**, and **abolished polygamy as void** if not under Shari’a law.
- Established **conciliation procedures** as a prerequisite for divorce.

However, this act **did not apply to Muslims**, who continued to be subject to **Shari’a law**, which is regulated at the **state level** across 13 states. These **state-specific versions of Shari’a law** have led to **non-uniform interpretations** and applications, which have significant implications for women's rights.

---

### 2. **Key Legal Challenges for Women**

Following the LRA and its implementation, several core issues emerged, particularly affecting **Muslim women**, who remain subject to **state-administered Shari’a law**:

#### a. **Non-Uniformity of Shari’a Law**
- Different **state enactments** of Shari’a law (e.g., in Kelantan, Terengganu, etc.) resulted in **varying interpretations** of personal status laws.
- This **non-uniform application** has left women without consistent **legal protections** and **recourse**, especially in cases of **polygamy** and **matrimonial property rights**.

#### b. **Limitations in Women’s Legal Recourse**
- **Divorce rights**: Women could only pursue divorce through **conciliation procedures** or by **proving grounds of injustice** (e.g., abuse, abandonment), which are not always straightforward.
- **Polygamy control**: Although the LRA penalized polygamy under civil law, **Shari’a courts** still allowed polygamous unions **if the husband obtained court permission**. However, the process often failed to ensure **adequate financial support** or **consent from wives**, leading to **exploitative conditions**.
- **Property rights (harta sepencarian)**: Despite reforms, **women's rights to jointly acquired property** were not always recognized, especially in **state-issued Shari’a laws**, which often prioritize male dominance.

#### c. **Dual Legal System and Rural Disparities**
- The **dual court system**—**civil for non-Muslims** and **Shari’a for Muslims**—has created **barriers**, especially for **Muslim women**. 
- **Legal literacy** in rural areas remains **low**, and **cultural norms** sometimes hinder women's willingness to seek legal redress.
- **Inconsistent interpretations** among **Shari’a judges** and **limited access to legal aid** further marginalize women’s ability to enforce their rights.
