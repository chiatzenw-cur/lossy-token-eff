### Analyzing the Complexities and Tensions of Sociocultural Perspectives

To determine the best reflection of the complexities and potential tensions when applying **literacy as social practice**, **multiliteracies**, and **critical literacy** to literacy education and practice, we must understand the distinct emphases and limitations of each framework, particularly in relation to **identity** and **power relations**.

### Key Emphases and Limitations of Each Framework

1. **Literacy as Social Practice**  
   - This perspective, rooted in the works of Street and Barton & Hamilton, emphasizes the **situational and social nature of literacy**.
   - It defines literacy as **practices** that occur within cultural and institutional contexts, involving **values, attitudes, and power dynamics**.
   - It aligns well with identity and power structures, showing how literacy *shapes and is shaped* by individuals’ cultural identities and the broader social power hierarchies that influence interaction and access.

2. **Multiliteracies**  
   - The New London Group and Cope & Kalantzis argue for a **broad understanding of literacy** that includes **multiple semiotic systems** (e.g., visual, audio, spatial).
   - This framework introduces **technological literacy** and digital modes of communication as key components of literacy in the modern world.
   - It deviates from the **primacy of the written word**, and suggests that literacy should be understood and practiced in **multiple, context-specific ways**.
   - However, the **focus on media and modes may sometimes bypass deeper critical examinations of power structures that shape access to literacy resources.**

3. **Critical Literacy**  
   - Rooted in Freire’s work, this framework stresses that **literacy is not neutral**—it is **inextricably tied to power and social inequality**.
   - It emphasizes the **agency and transformation** of individuals who engage with texts and the **critical reasoning required to understand and challenge societal norms and structures**.
   - This approach serves as a **counterforce to dominant narratives in education**, promoting a **reflexive and social justice-oriented literacy**, where **identities are understood and contested** within the contexts of power and privilege.
