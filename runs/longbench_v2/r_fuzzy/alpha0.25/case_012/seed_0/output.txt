Q:\! \text{Given the two experimental findings, what would most likely be the result if the environmental media contains a conductivity ratio of }0.15 \text{ (roughly }0.1?),.\]

\] \[
\text{Marín et al. (2014) show that, when the surroundings film was essentially air (very
low conductivity), the freezing front progressed from the substrate to the
apex upwards, leaving the solid–air interface always perpendicular to the
isotherm.  The conical tip that forms was universalised and the tip angle
remained around }139^{\circ}\text{, independent of ambient temperature.}

\] \[
\text{Lyu et al. (2023) considered a warmer, more conductive environment
(e.g. a silicone oil bath).  The higher conductivity of the ambient medium
allows appreciable heat of release from the ice to escape not only on the
solid side and through the air, but also through the surrounding fluid.  As
the environmental heat transfer increases, some of the inner liquid is forced
unfrozen in the interior but the outer surface freezes rapidly.  The result is
an ice shell that envelops a liquid core, and the freezing front no longer
exhibits the simple, quasi‑static, self‑similar geometric behaviour
observed for air.  The final shape, therefore a nonspherical, non‑conical
drop, is a consequence of the liquid “encapsulation”.}

\] \[
\text{A factor of }10\text{ in air‑to‑ice conductivity (from }0.01\text{ for air to }
\sim0.15\text{) makes the heat transfer through the surrounding medium
negligible compared to air while still being far larger than that
considered by Marín et al.  Thus the drop will behave as the “mixed
regime’’ in the Lyu et al. study and will develop a shell of ice that
traps a small portion of liquid inside.  The tip will be altered and
the tip angle will no longer be the universal about \(139^{\circ}\).}

\] \[
\boxed{\text{C}}
