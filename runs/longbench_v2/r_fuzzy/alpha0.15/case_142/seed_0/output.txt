\Lambda_{\text{wdt}}(F_3)=\sigma(\alpha_{\text{wdt}}\Delta(F_3)),\qquad 
\Delta(F_3)\in\mathbb{R}.
\]

The sigmoid \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})\) maps real numbers into \((0,1)\).  
Multiplying \(\Delta(F_3)\) by the scalar \(\alpha_{\text{wdt}}\) changes how sharply
the sigmoid transitions from near‑0 to that near‑1:

- For a small \(\alpha_{\text{wdt}}\) the transition is gradual; faces with
\(\Delta(F_3)\) slightly above or below zero still give probabilities around
0.5, producing a softer, more continuous decision and weaker gradients.  
- For a large \(\alpha_{\text{wdt}}\) the sigmoid becomes steep: any \(\Delta(F_3)\)
just slightly positive gives a probability close to 1, while any negative
value gives a probability close to 0.  In this regime the probability behaves
 almost like a hard step function, and small changes in \(\Delta(F_3)\) cause
signy jumps in the probability and thus large gradients.

In DMesh the authors set \(\alpha_{\text{wdt}} = 1000\) to obtain a nearly
deterministic inclusion test but still keep the function differentiable,
allowing gradients to flow through the face‑existence probability during
optimization.

Hence the primary role of \(\alpha_{\text{wdt}}\) is **to modulate the slope of
the logistic curve (the sigmoid) and thus the sensitivity of the face
existence probability to changes in \(\Delta(F_3)\)**.  This influences the
magnitude of gradients and the stability/accuracy of the optimization
process, rather than directly controlling mesh complexity or computational
cost.
