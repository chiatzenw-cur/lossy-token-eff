Key points:

- Both revolve around contract formation and whether parties intended to be bound (the element of intention). There is confusion about joke, seriousness, etc. In Lucy v. Zehmer, court held that contract was enforceable, the parties intended to be bound, even if Zehmer claimed it was a joke. The trial judge found that claims that it was a joke were insufficient and that there's no fraud. In Texaco v. The key issue: was there a binding contract? The court upheld it. The question: "What do these two cases have in common?" Options:

A. The truthfulness of the parties' intent in entering into the contract is of paramount importance. This seems plausible: both cases are about the parties’ intentions and truthfulness of those intentions that made a contract valid. Both revolve around habit, jest, etc.

B. The defaulting party's reasons for refusing to honor the agreement are all that the other party's offer was too low. This is nonsense. Not correct.

C. The basic terms of the main agreement are defined. In Lucy v. Zehmer, the contract contains terms: 50,000 purchase price, etc. In Texaco v. D. The court upheld all of the plaintiff's claims. In Lucy v. Zehmer, the court reversed a dismissal and remanded. That effectively upheld the plaintiff's claims. But in Texaco v. Pennzoil, the trial court awarded damages to plaintiff; the appellate court affirmed judgment but with punitive damages reduction. The question likely wants to focus on the common basis: the question of intent is essential in both cases, the importance of truthfulness of intentions to bind. So Option A is most apt.

Let's check: Both cases revolve around the potential contract being considered a joke or not, partly around the truthfulness of parties' intentions. In Lucy v. Zehmer, "the evidence shows that Lucy did not so understand it but considered it to be a serious business transaction and the contract to be binding." And then the court made a judgment based on the lack of evidence of intent not to be bound. In Texaco v. Pennzoil, they evaluated whether the intention was to be bound; the court considered that the parties were not (or were) bound. The outcome is that the case recognized that expression of intention was key.

Hence Option A: "Truthfulness of the parties' intent" is common.
