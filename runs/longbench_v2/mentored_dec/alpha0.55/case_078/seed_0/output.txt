**Why?**  

1.  **Circumstances of Tacit Approval** – The court’s “acquiescence doctrine” comes into play only when Congress has *ongoing, unchallenged practice* of allowing a certain type of executive action.  
    *  Congress has had a *clear, consistent history* of allowing or otherwise remaining silent on a specific executive practice.  
    *  The executive branch has issued *repeated, similar orders* in the same area.  
    *  No congressional action has been taken to restrict or curb those orders, and no explicit or implicit congressional objection has been recorded.  

2.  **Key Factors** – The doctrine is not a blanket rule but depends on a handful of key factors:  
    * **Precedent of legislative delegation** – The same policy area must have been delegated or left to the executive in the past, creating a solid institutional precedent.  
    * **Duration and continuity** – The practice has been ongoing for a significant period with little interference, indicating acceptance.  
    * **Absence of opposition** – No congressional bill, resolution, or public statement has opposed the practice.  
    * **Context of statutory authority** – The executive order aligns with either a statutory delegation or, in some instances, relies on “inherent” powers.  
    * **Broad acceptance of the executive’s role** – The executive’s authority is recognized within the public and the courts as part of the “shared” division of power.  

3.  **Evolution of the Separation‑of‑Powers Doctrine** – Under these circumstances the doctrine moves from a textbook “strict separation” toward a more *shared‑power* model.  
    *  The positive interpretation of congressional inaction as implied consent underscores that the branches can “tacitly” agree to share powers.  
    *  The courts tend to reinforce this understanding by accepting that a long‑standing executive practice, when accompanied by congressional silence, can be treated as a policy choice that the legislature has deemingly endorsed.  
    *  This gradual evolution, rooted in the acquiescence doctrine, enhances the practical reality that power is often jointly exercised rather than rigidly divided.
