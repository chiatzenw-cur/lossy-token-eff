To solve the problem, we need to find the **least integer** $ b \geq 2 $ such that there are **more than ten** integers $ n $ that are **$ b $-beautiful**.

---

### Understanding $ b $-beautiful Numbers

A positive integer $ n $ is called **$ b $-beautiful** if:

- It has **exactly two digits** when written in base $ b $, meaning:  
  $$
  b \leq n < b^2
  $$
- The sum of the **digits of $ n $ in base $ b $** is **equal to** $ \sqrt{n} $.

Let's represent $ n $ in base $ b $ as $ a \cdot b + d $, where $ a $ and $ d $ are the first and second digits of $ n $, respectively.

So we have:
$$
n = ab + d, \quad \text{with } 1 \leq a < b, \quad 0 \leq d < b
$$
and
$$
a + d = \sqrt{n}
$$

Substituting $ n = ab + d $ into the second equation, we have:
$$
a + d = \sqrt{ab + d}
$$

Squaring both sides:
$$
(a + d)^2 = ab + d
$$

Expanding:
$$
a^2 + 2ad + d^2 = ab + d
$$

Rewriting:
$$
a^2 + 2ad + d^2 - ab - d = 0
$$

This leads to:
$$
a^2 - ab + 2ad + d^2 - d = 0
$$

Rather than solving algebraically for all $ a, d $, note an important insight:

From the identity $ a + d = \sqrt{n} $ and $ n = ab + d $, it can be shown that:
$$
n = s^2
\quad \text{where } s = a + d
$$

So all $ b $-beautiful numbers are perfect squares. Moreover, the digit sum of $ s $ in base $ b $, i.e., $ a + d = s $, gives:
$$
n = s^2 \quad \text{and } a = \frac{s^2 - s}{b - 1}, \quad d = s - a
$$

For $ a $ and $ d $ to be valid digits in base $ b $, both must satisfy:
- $ 1 \leq a < b $,
- $ 0 \leq d < b $,
- $ a + d = s $,
- $ ab + d < b^2 $ (since $ n $ is a two-digit number in base $ b $).

This implies:
$$
n = s^2 \in [b, b^2 - 1]
$$

So, for $ s $, we must have:
$$
b \leq s^2 < b^2 \Rightarrow \sqrt{b} \leq s \leq b - 1
$$

---

### Reformulating the Problem

We are now looking to **count the number of $ s $** in the range $ \sqrt{b} \leq s \leq b - 1 $ such that:
$$
a = \frac{s^2 - s}{b - 1} \in \mathbb{Z}
\quad \text{and} \quad
d = s - a \in \mathbb{Z}, \quad 0 \leq d < b
$$

Equivalently, $ b - 1 $ must divide $ s(s - 1) $. So, we define:
$$
k = b - 1
$$

Then, the problem becomes:

> For each $ k \geq 1 $, how many integers $ s $ in the interval $ \lceil \sqrt{k + 1} \rceil \leq s \leq k $ satisfy $ k \mid s(s - 1) $?

This is the number of **$ k $-good values of $ s $**.

---

### Key Observation

It turns out that the number of such $ s \in [1, k] $ such that $ s(s - 1) \equiv 0 \mod k $ is equal to $ 2^{\omega(k)} $, where $ \omega(k) $ denotes the number of **distinct prime divisors** of $ k $.

This is because $ s(s - 1) \equiv 0 \pmod{k} $ means $ s \equiv 0 \pmod{m} $ and $ s \equiv 1 \pmod{n} $, or vice versa, for all coprime factor pairs $ m, n $ of $ k $ with $ mn = k $.

---

### Determining the Minimum $ b $

We need to find the **least $ k \geq 1 $** such that the number of $ s \in [\lceil \sqrt{k + 1} \rceil, k] $ with $ k \mid s(s - 1) $ is **more than ten**.

Let’s denote this number as $ N(k) $, so we are looking for the **smallest $ k $** such that:
$$
N(k) > 10
$$

We know:
$$
N(k) = 2^{\omega(k)} - \text{number of good } s \text{ in } [1, \lfloor \sqrt{k + 1} \rfloor - 1]
$$

For most $ k $, the number of such $ s $ in the lower range is **small** (often just 1 or 2), so approximately:
$$
N(k) \approx 2^{\omega(k)}
$$

To get $ N(k) > 10 $, we need $ 2^{\omega(k)} \geq 11 $, so:
$$
\omega(k) \geq 4
$$

---

### Finding the Smallest $ k $ with $ \omega(k) = 4 $

The **smallest integer** $ k $ with **4 distinct prime factors** is:

$$
k = 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 = 210
$$

Then $ b = k + 1 = 211 $ is the **least integer** such that there are **more than ten** $ b $-beautiful numbers.

Let’s verify:

- $ \omega(210) = 4 \Rightarrow 2^4 = 16 $
- Let’s check the number of $ s $ in $ [\sqrt{211}, 210] = [15, 210] $
- We subtract the number of valid $ s $ in $ [1, 14] $, which only includes $ s = 1 $
- So $ N(210) = 16 - 1 = 15 > 10 $

Also, any smaller $ k $ with $ \omega(k) = 4 $ would not exist, as the four smallest primes are $ 2, 3, 5, 7 $, and their product is 210.
