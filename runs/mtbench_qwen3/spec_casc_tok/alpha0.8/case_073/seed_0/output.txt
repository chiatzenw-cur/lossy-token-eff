The **central dogma of molecular biology** is a foundational concept that describes the flow of genetic information within a biological system. It was introduced by **Francis Crick** in 1957 and outlines the unidirectional transfer of genetic information from **DNA to RNA to protein**. Here’s a breakdown:

---

### **Key Processes Involved**:
1. **DNA Replication**  
   - DNA is copied to create identical copies of itself during cell division.  
   - **Enzymes involved**: DNA polymerase.  
   - **Purpose**: Ensures genetic continuity across generations.

2. **Transcription**  
   - DNA is transcribed into **messenger RNA (mRNA)** by the enzyme **RNA polymerase**.  
   - **Process**: A segment of DNA is used as a template to synthesize a complementary RNA strand.  
   - **Purpose**: Produces RNA molecules that carry genetic information from DNA to the ribosome.

3. **Translation**  
   - mRNA is translated into **protein** by **ribosomes**.  
   - **Process**: The sequence of nucleotides in mRNA is decoded into a sequence of amino acids, which fold into functional proteins.  
   - **Purpose**: Converts genetic information into the molecular machinery of the cell.

---

### **Flow of Information**:  
DNA → RNA → Protein  
This unidirectional flow emphasizes that genetic information **cannot** be directly transferred from protein back to DNA (as originally stated by Crick), though exceptions exist (e.g., **reverse transcription** in retroviruses like HIV).

---

### **Key Points**:  
- **Unidirectional**: Genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein but not in reverse.  
- **Exceptions**: Some viruses (e.g., retroviruses) use **reverse transcriptase** to convert RNA into DNA, which is not part of the original central dogma.  
- **Significance**: The central dogma forms the basis of modern molecular biology, explaining how genes are expressed and how hereditary information is passed.  

---

### **Who Named It?**  
**Francis Crick** coined the term **"central dogma"** in 1957 while working at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, UK. His work built on the discovery of DNA's structure by James Watson and himself, and his insights reshaped our understanding of genetics and molecular biology.
