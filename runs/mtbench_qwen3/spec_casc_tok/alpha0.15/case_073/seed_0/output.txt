The **central dogma of molecular biology** is a foundational concept describing the unidirectional flow of genetic information within cells. It was formulated by **Francis Crick** in 1957 and outlines how genetic information is transmitted from **DNA to RNA to protein**. Here's a breakdown:

### **Processes Involved:**
1. **DNA Replication**:  
   - While not explicitly part of the central dogma, DNA replication is essential for transmitting genetic information to daughter cells. It ensures the accurate duplication of DNA during cell division.  
   - **Note**: DNA replication is a distinct process that supports the central dogma by maintaining the genetic blueprint.

2. **Transcription**:  
   - DNA is copied into **messenger RNA (mRNA)** via the enzyme **RNA polymerase**. This process occurs in the nucleus (in eukaryotic cells) and involves reading the DNA template to synthesize RNA.

3. **Translation**:  
   - The mRNA is used as a template to synthesize **proteins** in the ribosomes. This process involves decoding the genetic code (via codons) to assemble amino acids into polypeptide chains.

### **Key Directionality**  
The central dogma emphasizes the **one-way transfer** of information:  
**DNA → RNA → Protein**.  
It excludes reverse processes (e.g., **protein → DNA**) and exceptions like **reverse transcription** (e.g., in retroviruses like HIV, where RNA is converted into DNA via the enzyme **reverse transcriptase**). These exceptions are acknowledged as exceptions to the central dogma but are not part of its original framework.

### **Significance**  
The central dogma laid the groundwork for understanding molecular genetics, explaining how genetic information is stored, expressed, and utilized in organisms. It remains a cornerstone of modern biology, even as additional mechanisms (e.g., RNA interference, non-coding RNAs) have expanded the scope of genetic processes.
