Socrates employed several distinctive methods to challenge the prevailing thoughts of his time, characterized by his emphasis on critical inquiry, dialogue, and the pursuit of truth. These methods, which became foundational to Western philosophy, are best understood through his student Plato's dialogues and other ancient sources. Here's a structured overview of his key approaches:

### 1. **Socratic Method / Dialectical Dialogue**  
   - **Core of Inquiry**: Socrates engaged in structured, question-and-answer dialogues to explore ideas. This method, often termed the Socratic method, involved probing questions to stimulate critical thinking and uncover contradictions in participants' beliefs.  
   - **Purpose**: By asking "what do you mean by X?" or "How do you know X is true?", he encouraged individuals to articulate their views, leading them to recognize gaps in their understanding. This process often revealed the fallibility of common assumptions or the inadequacy of existing definitions of concepts like justice, virtue, or knowledge.  
   - **Example**: In the *Euthyphro*, he challenges Euthyphro’s definition of piety by asking whether something is pious because the gods approve of it, or if the gods approve of it because it is pious—a question that exposed the instability of his claim.

---

### 2. **Elenchus (Cross-Examination)**  
   - **Expose Contradictions**: This method involved systematically interrogating an opponent’s beliefs to reveal logical inconsistencies. Socrates would discard apparent truths and push for deeper analysis, often leading to the collapse of unexamined claims.  
   - **Technique**: By asking successive questions, he would dismantle opponents’ arguments, forcing them to confront the contradictions in their own positions. This process, known as the *elenchus*, was a form of intellectual "measuring" to determine the validity of claims.  
   - **Impact**: It challenged the prevailing tendency to accept dogmatic or rhetorical assertions without scrutiny, particularly the teachings of Sophists who prioritized persuasive speech over truth.

---

### 3. **Socratic Irony**  
   - **Role of Pretended Ignorance**: Socrates frequently pretended to be ignorant (as described in the *Apology*) to provoke others into revealing their own ignorance. This "self-declared ignorance" was a rhetorical strategy to disarm defensiveness and encourage open inquiry.  
   - **Purpose**: By appearing as a seeker of wisdom, he elevated the interlocutor’s responsibility to engage in self-examination. This irony served to highlight the inadequacy of others’ knowledge and the necessity of philosophical dialogue.  
   - **Example**: In the *Alcibiades I*, he feigns ignorance to elicit Alcibiades’ confessions about his flaws, illustrating the method’s role in exposing unexamined beliefs.

---

### 4. **Questioning of Assumptions**  
   - **Challenge Accepted Truths**: Socrates targeted the unexamined assumptions of Athenian society, such as the belief in a pantheon of anthropomorphic gods or the supremacy of rhetoric over truth. He questioned norms like the value of wealth, status, or democratic traditions.  
   - **Ethical Focus**: He emphasized the examined life and the pursuit of virtue over material success, challenging the hedonistic or pragmatic values of his time. For instance, in the *Crito*, he debates whether one should obey the state’s laws, even when they conflict with personal moral principles.  
   - **Critique of Sophistry**: He opposed the Sophists’ focus on rhetoric and relativism, advocating instead for the pursuit of objective truth and moral integrity.

---

### 5. **Definition and Analysis**  
   - **Precision Through Inquiry**: Socrates sought to define abstract concepts (e.g., justice, beauty, courage) by asking participants to articulate their meanings. He would then subject these definitions to rigorous scrutiny, exposing their ambiguities or inconsistencies.  
   - **Example**: In the *Protagoras*, he engages in a dialectical exchange to define courage, revealing how different definitions (e.g., "the knowledge of what is fearful and what is not") might be flawed or incomplete.  
   - **Goal**: To move from vague, culturally accepted ideas toward more coherent and universally valid definitions.

---

### 6. **Dialectical Method**  
   - **Engaging Opposing Views**: Socrates often presented conflicting perspectives on a topic and explored their implications. This method encouraged participants to evaluate multiple viewpoints, fostering a deeper understanding of complex issues.  
   - **Example**: In the *Phaedo*, he examines the nature of the soul and the afterlife by considering opposing arguments (e.g., the immortality of the soul via recollection vs. physicalism).  
   - **Impact**: It challenged the era’s tendency to accept single, unchallenged viewpoints, promoting intellectual rigor.

---

### 7. **Challenge to Power and Tradition**  
   - **Critique of Authority**: Socrates questioned the authority of democratic institutions, religious doctrines, and societal norms. His trial and execution for "corrupting the youth" highlight the tension between his method and the state’s desire to maintain social order.  
   - **Role of the Philosopher**: He positioned the philosopher as a "midwife" of ideas (the *maieutic method*), helping others deliver "childbirth" of truth rather than merely imparting knowledge. This role undermined the traditional hierarchy of teacher-student and authority.

---

### **Legacy and Impact**  
Socrates’ methods challenged the prevailing beliefs of his time by:  
- **Encouraging Critical Thinking**: Rejecting uncritical acceptance of tradition and rhetoric.  
- **Prioritizing Ethics Over Utility**: Promoting the pursuit of virtue and truth over material success or political expediency.  
- **Democratizing Knowledge**: Making philosophy accessible through dialogue, rather than relying on written texts or elites.  

His approach, though non-confrontational in form, directly confronted the intellectual and ethical currents of classical Athens, leaving a lasting influence on epistemology, ethics, and the practice of philosophy itself.
