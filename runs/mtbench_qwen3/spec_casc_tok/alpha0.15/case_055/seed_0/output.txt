**What is a Language Model?**  
A language model is like a computer program that learns how human languages work. It can understand what you’re saying, predict the next word in a sentence, or even create new text that makes sense. Think of it as a "language detective" that studies how words are used in real life to figure out meaning, grammar, and context.  

**Is it trained using labeled or unlabeled data?**  
Language models are mainly trained using **unlabeled data**—this means they learn from raw text (like books, articles, or conversations) without being told what the "correct" answer is. For example, if you write a sentence like *"The cat sat on the "__*, the model learns from seeing similar sentences in its training data to guess the next word might be *"mat"*, *"table"*, or something else. It figures out patterns on its own, just like how you learn to speak by listening to others, not by being given a list of rules.  

**Why unlabeled data?**  
Labeled data (like sentences with answers or tags) is used for specific tasks like classifying emails as spam or identifying emotions in text. But for a general language model, the goal is to understand language as a whole, not to perform one narrow task. Using unlabeled data lets the model learn vast amounts of information about how words and sentences work in the real world.  

**In short:**  
Language models are like students who study by reading books and listening to conversations, not by being told the "right" answers. They use **unlabeled data** to learn the rules of language, enabling them to respond naturally to your questions and create meaningful text.
