The stages of life profoundly shape our understanding of time and mortality, as individuals navigate relationships, responsibilities, and existential reflections that evolve with age. Here's how each stage influences these perceptions:

---

### **1. Childhood: Time as Endless, Mortality as Abstract**
- **Time Perception**: Childhood is marked by a sense of boundless time. The world feels expansive, and the future is a distant, unstructured horizon. Children often experience time through sensory experiences rather than linear progression.
- **Mortality Understanding**: Death is largely conceptual or abstract. Children may not grasp it as a personal reality, often understanding it through stories, imagination, or ordeals like losing a pet. Their awareness of mortality is minimal, and their focus is on immediate experiences and growth.
- **Psychological Lens**: Erikson’s theory of "trust vs. mistrust" highlights how early experiences with caregivers shape a child’s sense of security and time. Mortality remains an alien concept, as their world is sheltered from the inevitability of loss.

---

### **2. Adolescence: Time as a Journey, Mortality as Uncertain**
- **Time Perception**: Adolescents perceive time as a series of milestones (education, career, relationships). They often experience time as slower, as they transition from a controlled (childhood) to a more autonomous (adulthood) existence.
- **Mortality Understanding**: Mortality becomes more tangible through peer experiences (e.g., peer pressure, sudden loss) and the realization that life is finite. Adolescents may grapple with existential questions about purpose and legacy, though their understanding remains fragmented.
- **Psychological Lens**: Erikson’s "identity vs. role confusion" stage emphasizes self-awareness and the quest for meaning. Mortality is not yet a dominant concern but lingers as a shadowy possibility, especially with the separation of adolescence from family structures.

---

### **3. Adulthood: Time as Control, Mortality as Personal Reality**
- **Time Perception**: Time becomes more structured, tied to responsibilities (work, family, career). As individuals age, the perception of time becomes "linear" and finite, with a growing awareness of its scarcity. Productivity and deadlines often dominate.
- **Mortality Understanding**: Mortality becomes a present concern, especially with the loss of loved ones, health, and aging. The "temporal discounting" phenomenon (prioritizing short-term gains over long-term goals) may emerge, reflecting a focus on immediate needs.
- **Psychological Lens**: Erikson’s "generativity vs. stagnation" stage (midlife) involves balancing personal goals with contributions to others. Mortality salience (the awareness of death’s inevitability) begins to influence behavior, such as career choices or family planning.

---

### **4. Old Age: Time as Reflection, Mortality as Acceptance**
- **Time Perception**: Time feels more compressed, with reinforced memories of the past and a focus on the present. The "posit audiences, predictive framing" theory suggests that older adults perceive time as cyclical rather than linear, emphasizing continuity and closure.
- **Mortality Understanding**: Mortality becomes a constant presence, often expressed through existential reflections (e.g., "time is short"). Older adults may engage in "life review" processes, re-evaluating their lives and legacy. Acceptance or fear of death can occur, depending on cultural, personal, and religious contexts.
- **Psychological Lens**: Erikson’s "integrity vs. despair" stage (late life) involves reconciling with one’s life choices. Mortality is intertwined with meaning-making, as individuals seek to affirm their existence or grapple with regret.

---

### **5. Cultural and Psychological Dimensions**
- **Cultural Influences**: Some cultures frame time as cyclical (e.g., Indigenous traditions, Hinduism), while others view it as linear (e.g., Western individualism). These frameworks shape how stages are perceived and how mortality is approached.
- **Psychological Theories**: The "theory of psychological time" theory proposes that time perception adapts to life stages. For example, children prioritize "future time" (e.g., new experiences), while older adults focus on "past time" (e.g., reflection).
- **Mortality Salience**: As people age, heightened awareness of mortality can drive behaviors like seeking meaning, spirituality, or interpersonal connections, as described by Terror Management Theory (TMT).

---

### **Conclusion: A Lifelong Evolution**
- **Time** becomes a lens through which individuals measure their lives, shifting from endlessness (childhood) to scarcity (adulthood) and reflection (old age).
- **Mortality** transitions from abstraction to personal reality, influencing values, priorities, and relationships. The stages of life are not just biological phases but also psychosocial milestones that shape how we navigate the finite nature of existence search for meaning and purpose. 

This evolving understanding of time and mortality underscores the interconnectedness of personal growth, cultural context, and the human condition.
