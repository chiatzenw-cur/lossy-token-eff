The **central dogma of molecular biology** is a foundational concept that describes the flow of genetic information within a biological system. It was introduced by **Francis Crick** in 1957 and outlines the primary directionality of molecular processes involving DNA, RNA, and proteins.

### **Processes Involved**:
1. **DNA Replication**:  
   - DNA is copied to produce identical copies of the DNA molecule. This ensures the genetic information is passed to daughter cells during cell division.

2. **Transcription**:  
   - DNA is transcribed into **messenger RNA (mRNA)**. The DNA sequence is used as a template to synthesize complementary RNA strands. This process occurs in the nucleus (in eukaryotes) and is catalyzed by **RNA polymerase**.

3. **Translation**:  
   - The mRNA is translated into a **protein** by ribosomes. The genetic code in the mRNA is read in triplet codons, which specify amino acids to build the protein. This process involves **tRNA** (transfer RNA) and **rRNA** (ribosomal RNA).

### **Key Principles**:
- The central dogma asserts that **genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein**, and **not the reverse**. That is, DNA is the blueprint, RNA acts as an intermediary, and proteins are the functional products.  
- While the original dogma emphasized a **one-way flow**, later discoveries (e.g., **reverse transcriptase** in retroviruses like HIV) showed that **RNA can be converted into DNA** (a process called **reverse transcription**). However, this exception does not negate the central dogma but highlights its limitations.

### **Who Named It**:
- **Francis Crick** coined the term "central dogma" in **1957** in his paper *"On the General Nature of the Genetic Code"*. The term reflects the idea that this flow of information is a central and fundamental principle in molecular biology.

### **Significance**:
The central dogma revolutionized genetics and molecular biology by providing a framework for understanding how genetic information is stored, transmitted, and expressed. It remains a cornerstone of modern biology, even as refinements and exceptions (e.g., non-coding RNA, epigenetic regulation) have expanded its scope.
