Socrates employed several distinctive methods to challenge the prevailing thoughts of his time, which were central to his philosophical practice and legacy. Here are the key methods he used:

### 1. **The Socratic Method**  
   - **Dialogue and Questioning**: Socrates engaged in dialogues with citizens of Athens, asking probing questions to stimulate critical thinking and expose contradictions in their beliefs. This method, often called the **elenchus**, aimed to bring hidden assumptions and inconsistencies in people's ideas to the surface.  
   - **Dialectical Inquiry**: He used a conversational, dialectical approach to explore ideas, often starting with a general statement and moving to more specific questions. This method demonstrated that even seemingly obvious truths could be flawed.  

### 2. **Socratic Irony**  
   - **Feigned Ignorance**: Socrates often appeared as a self-proclaimed "ignoramus," pretending to lack knowledge. This technique, known as **Socratic irony**, disarmed his interlocutors, encouraging them to reveal their own ignorance and engage in deeper reflection.  
   - **Humbling the Wise**: By adopting this posture, he highlighted the arrogance of those who claimed to know the "truth" without proper examination, challenging the overconfidence of figures like the Sophists.  

### 3. **Questioning National and Religious Beliefs**  
   - **Ethical and Philosophical Challenges**: Socrates interrogated societal norms and values, such as piety, justice, and virtue. He questioned the Athenian belief in the gods (e.g., his trial for impiety) and the reliability of popular opinion, advocating for a pursuit of truth over tradition.  
   - **Critique of Sophistic Rhetoric**: He opposed the Sophists' focus on persuasive rhetoric over moral truth, arguing that their methods prioritized winning arguments over understanding reality.  

### 4. **Focus on Self-Examination**  
   - **Examining the Self**: Socrates emphasized the importance of **self-examination** (analectics), urging individuals to question their own beliefs and actions. This challenged the passive acceptance of societal norms, promoting a personal commitment to truth.  
   - **The Unexamined Life**: His belief that "an unexamined life is not worth living" (from Plato's *Apology*) demanded a reevaluation of how individuals engaged with meaning and morality.  

### 5. **Use of Paradoxes and Contradictions**  
   - **Revealing Inconsistencies**: Socrates often bathed the contradictions inentes of his interlocutors' positions (e.g., the paradox of "the wisest man is the one who knows he knows nothing"). This exposed the limitations of their arguments and encouraged more rigorous inquiry.  

### 6. **Moral and Philosophical Inquiry**  
   - **Seeking Definitions**: He sought to define abstract concepts like justice, courage, and the good life (e.g., in *The Apology*, *Crito*, and *Euthyphro*). This method aimed to uncover the foundational principles of morality and knowledge, challenging dogmatic views.  

### 7. **Pedagogical Praxis**  
   - **Leading to Knowledge**: Socrates did not provide answers but instead facilitated the journey toward knowledge. His method, while not yielding definitive conclusions, aimed to cultivate intellectual humility and a pursuit of truth.  

### Impact and Significance  
Socrates' methods directly challenged the intellectual and moral landscape of fifth-century Athens. By challenging the Sophists' emphasis on rhetoric, questioning religious and ethical norms, and promoting self-examination, he challenged the complacency of his society. His legacy lies in his commitment to critical inquiry and the belief that truth, not tradition or popularity, should guide human life.  

These methods, though controversial, laid the groundwork for Western philosophy, emphasizing the importance of questioning, dialogue, and the pursuit of knowledge.
