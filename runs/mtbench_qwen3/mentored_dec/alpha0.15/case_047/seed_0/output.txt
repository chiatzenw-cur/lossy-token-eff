**Differences Between Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions**

**Definitions:**  
- **Exothermic reactions** release energy, typically in the form of heat, to the surroundings.  
- **Endothermic reactions** absorb energy from the surroundings, often causing a cooling effect.  

**Criteria to Distinguish:**  
1. **Energy Change (ΔH):**  
   - **Exothermic:** ΔH is negative (energy is released by the system).  
   - **Endothermic:** ΔH is positive (energy is absorbed by the system).  

2. **Direction of Energy Flow:**  
   - **Exothermic:** Energy flows **out** of the system into the surroundings.  
   - **Endothermic:** Energy flows **into** the system from the surroundings.  

3. **Effect on Surroundings:**  
   - **Exothermic:** Surroundings **warm up** (e.g., a reaction that increases temperature).  
   - **Endothermic:** Surroundings **cool down** (e.g., a reaction that decreases temperature).  

4. **Enthalpy of Products vs. Reactants:**  
   - **Exothermic:** Products have **lower enthalpy** than reactants.  
   - **Endothermic:** Products have **higher enthalpy** than reactants.  

**Real-World Examples:**  

1. **Exothermic Reaction:**  
   - **Burning Wood:** When wood combusts, it reacts with oxygen to produce heat and light. The reaction is:  
     $$
     \text{C (wood)} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{Energy}
     $$  
     The released heat warms the surroundings, making it a classic exothermic process.  

2. **Endothermic Reaction:**  
   - **Melting Ice:** When ice melts, it absorbs heat from the environment to transition from solid to liquid. This causes the surroundings to cool, such as when a cold pack is activated. The process is:  
     $$
     \text{H}_2\text{O (solid)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O (liquid)} + \text{Energy (absorbed)}
     $$  
     The absorption of heat from the surroundings (e.g., your skin) makes it feel colder.  

**Key Takeaway:**  
Exothermic reactions release energy (e.g., combustion, hand warmers), while endothermic reactions absorb energy (e.g., melting ice, photosynthesis in plants). The distinction is determined by the enthalpy change, temperature effect, and energy flow direction.
