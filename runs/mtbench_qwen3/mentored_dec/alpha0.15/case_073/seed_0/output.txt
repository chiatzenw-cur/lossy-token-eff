The **central dogma of molecular biology** is a foundational concept that describes the unidirectional flow of genetic information within a biological system. It was proposed by **Francis Crick** in 1958 and outlines the key processes by which genetic information is transmitted and expressed. 

### Key Components:
1. **DNA to RNA (Transcription)**:  
   - DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) serves as the primary repository of genetic information.  
   - During **transcription**, DNA is copied into **mRNA** (messenger RNA) by the enzyme **RNA polymerase**.  
   - This mRNA carries the genetic code from the DNA in the nucleus to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm.

2. **RNA to Protein (Translation)**:  
   - The mRNA is decoded by **ribosomes** during **translation**.  
   - Transfer RNA (tRNA) and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) facilitate the assembly of amino acids into a polypeptide chain, which folds into a functional protein.  

### Core Principle:
The central dogma emphasizes a **one-way flow** of genetic information:  
**DNA → RNA → Protein**.  
It asserts that **information cannot flow directly from protein back to DNA or RNA**, though exceptions exist (e.g., reverse transcription in retroviruses like HIV, which uses the enzyme **reverse transcriptase** to convert RNA into DNA). These exceptions are considered deviations rather than exceptions to the central dogma itself.

### Historical Context:
- **Francis Crick** coined the term in a 1958 lecture, later published in a scientific paper.  
- The concept was developed to explain how genetic information is stored in DNA, expressed through RNA, and ultimately used to produce proteins, which drive cellular function and traits.  

### Significance:
The central dogma forms the basis of modern molecular biology, underpinning fields such as genetics, biochemistry, and biotechnology. It highlights the hierarchical organization of genetic information and the role of nucleic acids (DNA/RNA) as intermediaries in cellular processes.
