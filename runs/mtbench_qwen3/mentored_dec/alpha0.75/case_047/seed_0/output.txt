**Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions: Differences and Criteria**

**Definitions and Key Differences:**  
1. **Exothermic Reactions:**  
   - These reactions **release energy**, usually in the form of **heat** (and sometimes light or sound).  
   - **Products have lower energy than reactants**.  
   - The **surroundings become warmer** as the system loses energy.  
   - **ΔH (change in enthalpy)** is **negative** (ΔH < 0).  

2. **Endothermic Reactions:**  
   - These reactions **absorb energy** from the surroundings, typically **heat**.  
   - **Products have higher energy than reactants**.  
   - The **surroundings become cooler** as the system gains energy.  
   - **ΔH (change in enthalpy)** is **positive** (ΔH > 0).  

---

**Criteria for Distinguishing Them:**  
- **Enthalpy Change (ΔH):**  
  - **Exothermic:** ΔH < 0 (energy released).  
  - **Endothermic:** ΔH > 0 (energy absorbed).  
- **Heat Transfer:**  
  - **Exothermic:** Heat flows from the system to the surroundings (surroundings warm up).  
  - **Endothermic:** Heat flows from the surroundings to the system (surroundings cool down).  
- **Temperature Change:**  
  - **Exothermic:** Temperature of the surroundings increases (e.g., a reaction flask feels hot).  
  - **Endothermic:** Temperature of the surroundings decreases (e.g., a reaction flask feels cold).  

---

**Real-World Example:**  
- **Exothermic Reaction:** **Combustion of methane (CH₄)**:  
  $$
  \text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{Heat}
  $$  
  - **Why it’s exothermic:** Bonds in the products (CO₂ and H₂O) are stronger than bonds in the reactants, releasing excess energy as heat.  
  - **Application:** This reaction powers engines and is used in gas heating systems.  

- **Endothermic Reaction:** **Melting of ice (H₂O)**:  
  $$
  \text{H}_2\text{O (solid)} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O (liquid)} + \text{Heat absorbed}
  $$  
  - **Why it’s endothermic:** Ice absorbs heat from the environment to break hydrogen bonds, causing it to melt.  
  - **Application:** This process is used in refrigeration or instant cold packs to cool injuries.  

---

**Summary:**  
- **Exothermic reactions** release heat (ΔH < 0), while **endothermic reactions** absorb heat (ΔH > 0).  
- The distinction is based on energy flow, enthalpy change, and the temperature of the surroundings.  
- Real-world examples highlight their practical applications, such as combustion (exothermic) for energy and melting ice (endothermic) for cooling.
