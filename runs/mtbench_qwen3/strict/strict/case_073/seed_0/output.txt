The **central dogma of molecular biology** is a foundational concept introduced by **Francis Crick** in 1958. It describes the unidirectional flow of genetic information within a biological system, specifically from **DNA to RNA to protein**. Here's a structured breakdown:

### **Processes Involved**
1. **DNA Replication**:  
   - DNA is copied into a new DNA molecule during cell division. This ensures genetic continuity and is a prerequisite for protein synthesis in daughter cells.

2. **Transcription**:  
   - **DNA to RNA**: The DNA sequence is transcribed into messenger RNA (mRNA) by the enzyme **RNA polymerase**. This process occurs in the nucleus (in eukaryotes) and involves creating an RNA copy complementary to a DNA template strand.

3. **Translation**:  
   - **RNA to Protein**: The mRNA is translated into a polypeptide (protein) by ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules decode the mRNA sequence into amino acids, which are then linked together to form the protein.

### **Key Principles**
- **Unidirectionality**: Genetic information flows in one direction—**DNA → RNA → protein**. Proteins cannot directly influence DNA or RNA synthesis in this context.
- **Explanatory Role**: It explains how hereditary information is stored (DNA), expressed (RNA), and utilized (proteins) to sustain life.

### **Exceptions and Modifications**
While the central dogma is a general principle, exceptions exist:
- **Retroviruses** (e.g., HIV) use **reverse transcriptase** to convert RNA into DNA, temporarily reversing the flow (RNA → DNA), which challenges the dogma but is an exception rather than the rule.
- **RNA-based organisms** (e.g., some viruses) may bypass DNA entirely, using RNA as the genetic material.

### **Who Named It?**
The term **"central dogma"** was coined by **Francis Crick** in his 1958 paper *"On Protein Synthesis"*. He emphasized its status as a "dogma" to highlight its foundational role in molecular biology, though he acknowledged exceptions like retroviruses.

### **Summary**
- **Central Dogma**: DNA → RNA → protein.  
- **Processes**: Replication, transcription, translation.  
- **Named by**: Francis Crick (1958).  

This framework remains central to understanding gene expression and molecular genetics, even as exceptions and complexities (e.g., non-coding RNAs, epigenetics) have expanded its interpretation.
