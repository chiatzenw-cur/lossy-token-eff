**Superposition in Quantum Physics:**  
In quantum mechanics, **superposition** refers to a principle where a quantum system can exist in a combination (or "superposition") of multiple states simultaneously. This is fundamentally different from classical physics, where objects have definite_options. For example, a qubit (the basic unit of quantum information) can be in a state that is both **0** and potentially **1** at the same time. Mathematically, this is represented by a linear combination of basis states (e.g., $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$), where $\alpha$$ and $\alpha$ are complex numbers describing the probability amplit expectation of the system collapsing to either state upon measurement.

**Quantum Entanglement:**  
**Quantum quantum entanglement** occurs when two or more particles become intrinsically linked, such that the state of one quantum cannot be described independently of the others, even if they are separated by vast distances. This "spooky action at a distance," as Einstein famously called it, means that measuring one entangled particle instantly determines the state of its partner, regardless of the physical distance between the pair. A classic example is the **Bell states**, which are entangled superpositions of two particles (e.g., $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$).
