The **central dogma of molecular biology** is a foundational principle that describe the unidirectional flow of genetic information within a biological system. It was formulated by **Francis Crick** in 1957 and formalized in his seminal article titled *“The Central Dogma of Molecular Biology”* published in *Nature*. It posits that **genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to proteins**, but not in reverse. 

The key processes involved in the central dogma are:

1. **Replication**: DNA is copied into DNA (though this is not explicitly part ofified central dogma, it is sometimes included as a prerequisite for the flow of information).
   - **DNA → DNA**: This occurs during cell division, ensuring genetic material is passed to daughter cells.

2. **Transcription**: DNA is transcribed into messenger RNA (mRNA).
   - **DNA → RNA**: Enzymes like **RNA polymerase** synthesize mRNA from a DNA DNA template, converting the genetic code into an RNA intermediate.

3. **Translation**: mRNA (or other RNA molecules) isGu translated into proteins.
   - **RNA → Protein**: Ribosomes decode messenger RNA into a sequence of amino acids, which are then folded into functional proteins.

### Key Aspects of the Central Dogma:
- **Unidirectonality**: Genetic information flows in **one direction**—DNA → RNA and RNA →.
- **No Reverse Flow**: The dogma initially excluded reverse processes like those seen in retroviruses (e.g., **reverse transcription**, where RNA is converted into DNA by enzyme **reverse transcriptase**). However, modern understanding acknowledges exceptions, but the central dogma remains a core framework for most cellular processes.

### Historical Context and Naming:
- **Francis ** coined the term "central dogma" in 1957 to emphasize the core principle of molecular biology, contrasting it with the earlier concept of the "genetic code," which described how DNA sequences translate to amino acid sequences.
- The original formulation was based on foundational discoveries, including the structure of DNA by Watson and Crick (1962) and the role of RNA as an intermediary in protein synthesis by Nirenberg and Khorana.

### Summary and Modern Perspective:  
While the central dogma originally described the flow from DNA to proteins, modern contexts acknowledge exceptions (e.g., non-coding RNAs, retroviruses), but the term still represents the **fundamental process of gene expression** in most eukaryytic and prokaryotic organisms. It remains a cornerstone of molecular biology, underscoring its importance in understanding how genetic information is stored, transmitted, and expressed.
