The **central dogma of molecular biology** is a foundational concept that describes the flow of genetic information within a biological system. It was formulated by **Francis Crick** in 1958 and outlines the unidirectional transfer of information from **DNA to RNA to protein**. Here's a breakdown:

### **Key Processes Involved:**
1. **DNA → RNA (Transcription):**  
   - DNA is used as a template to synthesize messenger RNA (mRNA).  
   - This process is catalyzed by **RNA polymerase**, which reads the DNA sequence and produces a complementary RNA strand.  
   - In eukaryotes, the mRNA undergoes **processing** (e.g., splicing, addition of a 5' cap, and poly-A tail) before being transported to the cytoplasm.

2. **RNA → Protein (Translation):**  
   - Ribosomes read the mRNA sequence and syn frameworks of the genetic code to assemble amino acids into polypeptide chains.  
   - Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules bring specific amino acids to the ribosome, guided by the mRNA codons.  

### **Why It's Called the "Central Dogma":**  
Crick termed it a "dogma" to emphasize its fundamental and unchallengeable nature. The principle asserts that **genetic information flows in one direction**: DNA to RNA to protein. While exceptions exist (e.g., **reverse transcriptase** in retroviruses converts RNA to DNA), these are considered special cases and do not invalidate the central dogma's core idea.

### **Summary:**  
- **Directionality:** DNA → RNA → Protein.  
- **Processes:** Transcription and translation.  
- **Proposed by:** **Francis Crick**.  

This framework remains central to understanding gene expression and molecular biology, even as modern fields like epigenetics and non-coding RNA challenge some of its assumptions.
