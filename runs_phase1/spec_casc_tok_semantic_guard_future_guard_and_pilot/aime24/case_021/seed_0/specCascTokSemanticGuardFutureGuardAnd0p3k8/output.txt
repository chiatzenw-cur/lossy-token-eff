### 1.  Counting the admissible \(k\)

Equation (1) shows that \(m\mid k(k-1)\).  
Let \(k\) run through the integers

\[
\lceil\sqrt{b}\rceil\le k\le b-1=m .
\]

(If \(k\) were larger, \(k^{2}\ge b^{2}\) would have at least three base‑\(b\) digits.)

For a fixed \(m\) the solutions of \(k(k-1)\equiv0\pmod m\) are exactly the
integers that are congruent to \(0\) or \(1\) modulo every prime‑power divisor of \(m\).
If \(m=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_i^{e_i}\) then by the Chinese Determine Theorem each prime
contributes two possibilities, leading to

\[
\#\{k\pmod m:\;k(k-1)\equiv0\pmod m\}=2^{\,r},
\]

where \(r\) is the number of distinct prime factors of \(m\).

The solutions \(k=0\) and \(k=1\) are never acceptable:  
\(k=0\) gives \(n=0\) (not positive) and \(k=1\) gives \(a=0\) (leading digit 0).
Consequently the number of admissible \(k\) is at most \(2^{\,r}-1\).

\[
2^{\,r}-1>10\quad \Longrightarrow\quad r\ge 4 .
