\[
\begin{aligned}
&\log_y(x^{4y})=10\,\Longrightarrow\,y^{10}=10\log _y(x^{4y})=x^{4y} \\
&\log_x(y^{x})=10\,\Longrightarrow\,x^{10}=y^{x}
\end{aligned}
\]

Taking natural logarithms gives

\[
10\ln x=x\ln y ,\qquad (1) \qquad (2)\qquad \ 10\ln y=4y\ln x .
\]

From (1) we write

\[
\ln y=\frac{10\ln x}{x}.
\]

Substitute this into (2):

\[
10\Bigl(\frac{10\ln x}{x}\Bigr)=4y\ln x
   \;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{100\ln x}{x}=4y\ln x .
\]

Since \(\ln x>0\) (because \(x>1\)), division by \( \ln x\) gives

\[
\frac{100}{x}=4y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y=\frac{25}{x}.
\]

Thus every possible pair \((x,y)\) that satisfies the necessary conditions must satisfy
\[
xy=25.
\]

To check whether the obtained relation is compatible with the original data,
substitute \(y=25/x\) back into one of the equations, e.g.

\[
x^{10}=y^{x}=(25/x)^{x}.
\]

Indeed, the two equations give the same condition for \(x\), implying that
every such pair \((x,y)\) exists, so the product is fixed.
