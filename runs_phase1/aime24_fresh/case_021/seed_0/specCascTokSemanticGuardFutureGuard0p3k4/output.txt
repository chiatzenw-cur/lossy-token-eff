### 1.  Counting the solutions

Let \(m=b-1\).  
The requirement \(a\) an integer is equivalent to  

\[
m\mid k(k-1).
\]

For a fixed \(m\) the congruence \(k(k-1)\equiv 0\pmod m\) has exactly
\(2^{\omega(m)}\) solutions modulo \(m\), where \(\omega(m)\) is the number of
distinct prime factors of \(m\).  
Indeed, for each prime factor \(p^e\!\mid m\) we must have
\(p^e\mid k\) or \(p^e\mid (k-1)\); the two choices are independent,
giving \(2^{\omega(m)}\) residue classes mod \(m\).

In the range \(2\le k\le m\) we exclude the residue class \(k\equiv1\pmod m\),
so the number of admissible \(k\) is

\[
N(m)=2^{\omega(m)}-1 .
\]

Each admissible \(k\) yields a distinct \(b\)-beautiful number \(k^{2}\).
Thus the number of \(b\)-beautiful integers is

\[
N(b)=2^{\omega(b-1)}-1 .
\]

### 2.  Requiring more than ten numbers

We need \(N(b)>10\), i.e.

\[
2^{\omega(b-1)}-1>10\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
2^{\omega(b-1)}\ge 12 .
\]

The smallest power of two that exceeds 11 is \(2^{4}=16\),
so we require \(\omega(b-1)\ge 4\); \(b-1\) must contain at least four
distinct prime factors.

The smallest integer with four distinct prime factors is

Thus the smallest possible \(b\) is
