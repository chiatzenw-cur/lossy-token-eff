### 1.  A useful equation

Put \(s=a+c\).  Then \(s\ge 1\) and  

\[
s^2=n=(a\,b+c)=a(b-1)+s .
\]

Hence  

\[
a(b-1)=s(s-1)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
a=\frac{s(s-1)}{\,b-1\,}.          \tag{1}
\]

Consequently \(b-1\mid s(s-1)\).  
Because \(\gcd(s,s-1)=1\), every prime factor of \(b-1\) must divide
*either* \(s\) or \(s-1\) (not both).  
Write  

\[
b-1=x\,y ,\qquad \gcd(x,y)=1 ,
\]

and let

\[
x\mid s,\qquad y\mid s-1 .
\]

Thus \(s\equiv 0\pmod{x}\) and \(s\equiv 1\pmod{y}\).  
By the Chinese remainder theorem there is exactly **one** solution
\(s\ (1\le s\le b-1)\) for each ordered pair \((x,y)\) that satisfies
\(xy=b-1\) and \(\gcd(x,y)=1\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Counting the solutions

Let \(r\) be the number of distinct prime factors of \(k=b-1\).

*Number of coprime factorisations of \(k\).*  
Each prime factor of \(k\) must be assigned to either \(x\) or \(y\);  
there are \(2^{\,r}\) ordered pairs \((x,y)\).
The unordered pairs amount to \(2^{\,r-1}\).

For each unordered pair we obtain two ordered pairs, hence
\(2^{\,r}\) solutions \(s\) in total.
Among them, the pair \((1,k)\) gives \(s=1\), which is **not** in the
required range \(s\ge\sqrt{k}\) (since \(k>1\)), while all other
solutions satisfy \(s\ge\sqrt{k}\).  
Therefore the number of admissible \(s\) (and hence the number of
\(b\)-beautiful integers) is

\[
\boxed{\,2^{\,r}-1 \,}.
\]

### 3.  When does the count exceed ten?

We need \(2^{\,r}-1>10\), i.e. \(2^{\,r}>11\).  
The smallest integer \(r\) with this property is \(r=4\), because
\(2^{3}=8<11\) and \(2^{4}=16>11\).
